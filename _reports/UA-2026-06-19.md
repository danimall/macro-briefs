**Research Date: June 19, 2026**

## Executive Summary

Under Armour is a mid-tier performance apparel and footwear brand attempting a founder-led "premium reset" turnaround that has not yet produced an operating inflection. The fundamentals are deteriorating across the metrics that matter most. Revenue has fallen for four consecutive fiscal years (FY23 $5.90B → FY24 $5.70B → FY25 $5.16B → FY26 $4.97B), the company is burning cash (FY26 operating cash flow of -$75M and FCF of -$162M), and its largest market, North America, declined 7.9% in FY26 and 10.3% in the December quarter. The headline FY26 net loss of -$495.6M is dominated by a $294.8M income tax expense recorded against a pretax loss, the result of valuation allowances against all U.S. federal deferred tax assets. That accounting move requires negative evidence about future U.S. profitability to outweigh positive evidence, which is management conceding, under the rules, that the North American recovery is in serious doubt.

The bull case rests almost entirely on one data point: Prem Watsa's Fairfax Financial, a 10%+ owner, has aggressively accumulated UA at depressed prices, buying well over $100M in January 2026 (including a 5.0M-share Class C block at $5.89) and adding more in May 2026. That is a genuine, conviction-level contrarian signal from a respected value investor that a standardized screen would miss. It is a positioning signal, not an operating catalyst, and it does not change the trajectory of the business.

We rate UA AVOID. The combination of secular top-line erosion, negative free cash flow, a tightening liquidity profile being managed through high-coupon debt and supply-chain-finance expansion, the loss of the flagship signature athlete in the weakest category, and an unresolved insurance-litigation cash overhang outweighs the Watsa accumulation and the optically cheap-looking valuation. There is no dated catalyst that would close the gap between a depressed price and intrinsic value in the next 12 months, and the dual-class structure leaves Class C holders without voting recourse.

## Company Overview

Under Armour develops, markets, and distributes branded performance apparel (~68% of net sales), footwear (~22%), and accessories (~8%). It sells through wholesale (~57% of revenue) and direct-to-consumer (~43%, via roughly 442 owned Brand and Factory House stores plus e-commerce). International is approximately 48% of revenue. Manufacturing is asset-light and concentrated: 10 manufacturers produce about 69% of apparel and 7 produce essentially all footwear, with no long-term supply or customer contracts.

The competitive position is structurally squeezed. UA competes against far larger and better-capitalized rivals (Nike, adidas) on one side, nimble lifestyle and athleisure entrants on the other, and retailer private label below. Founder Kevin Plank returned as President and CEO in April 2024 and is executing a premium-reset strategy: raising average selling prices, reducing promotional activity, and protecting brand positioning rather than chasing volume. The intent is to trade near-term revenue for margin and brand integrity. In execution, it is compounding the revenue decline while competitors out-invest UA.

Note on the security: "UA" is the non-voting Class C share; "UAA" is the Class A (one vote per share). The two classes trade at different prices, and Class C has historically carried a discount.

## Financial Analysis

**Revenue is in secular decline.** FY26 revenue of $4.97B was down 3.8% year-over-year and roughly 16% below the FY23 peak of $5.90B. By region in FY26: North America (the largest segment at $2.86B) fell 7.9%; APAC fell 4.8%; EMEA rose 8.6% and Latin America rose 8.7%. EMEA and Latin America are the only growth markets, and they are too small to offset the core. By category: apparel -1.6% ($3.40B), footwear -10.8% ($1.08B, the weakest), accessories +0.9%. The December quarter showed no improvement, with revenue -5.2% to $1.33B and North America -10.3%.

**Profitability has collapsed.** The FY26 operating loss was -$163M, with gross margin down 240bps to 45.5% (155bps of which came from tariffs) and worsening to 44.4% in the December quarter. The net loss of -$495.6M dwarfs the operating loss because UA recorded a $294.8M income tax expense despite a pretax loss of roughly -$201M. The effective tax rate was -146.9%, driven by valuation allowances against all U.S. federal deferred tax assets. ROE was -35% and ROA -11.2%.

**Cash flow turned negative and the balance sheet is eroding.** Operating cash flow was -$75M in FY26, with FCF of -$162M. Cash fell to $309M from $501M a year earlier. Equity dropped to $1.41B from $1.89B as losses consumed retained earnings. D/E stands at 2.12 and the Altman Z-Score at 2.02 sits in the grey zone.

**Capital-structure maneuvers signal stress.** UA issued $400M of 7.25% Senior Notes due 2030 in June 2025, a high coupon that reflects weakened credit, to defease its $600M 3.25% Notes maturing June 2026. Interest expense jumped to -$30M from -$6M. The company drew its $1.1B revolver ($200M outstanding at year-end, at 4.83%), grew supply-chain-finance payables to $265M from $144M, repatriated roughly $650M of foreign earnings (including a $250M dividend), and cut capex to about 2% of revenue. It remains in compliance with its leverage (<3.25x) and interest-coverage (>3.5x) covenants, but falling EBITDA against fixed covenant thresholds bears watching.

**Capital allocation is internally inconsistent.** Despite negative FCF and mounting losses, UA continued repurchasing Class C stock ($25M in FY26, $115M of a $500M authorization to date). Buying back stock while burning liquidity is a questionable use of capital at this point in the cycle.

The earnings surprise record is volatile and low-quality: two beats and two misses over the last four quarters, with the December 2025 quarter posting a +536.9% surprise (a swing to a $0.09 EPS print) but the March 2026 quarter missing by 69.5%. The visibility into forward earnings is poor.

## Growth Analysis

There is no credible near-term growth thesis. Revenue has declined every year since FY23, North America is still contracting at a high-single-digit to double-digit pace, and footwear — the category where scale and signature-athlete credibility matter most — fell 10.8% in FY26 and was just structurally weakened by the loss of Stephen Curry. Management's own actions point the wrong way: the premium reset deliberately sacrifices volume, and the U.S. deferred-tax write-down codifies internal doubt about the domestic recovery timeline.

The premium-reset strategy may eventually stabilize margins and rebuild brand equity, but the FY26 results show it is currently accelerating the top-line decline while gross margins compress under tariff pressure (~$80M COGS hit, ~160bps, expected in FY26). Management guided the second quarter of FY26 to revenue down 6-7% and gross margin down 340-360bps. The turnaround remains pre-inflection, and the data offers no evidence that the inflection is imminent.

The only growth pockets, EMEA and Latin America, are too small to move the consolidated trajectory. We do not see a path to revenue growth within the 12-month horizon, and the structural headwinds (competitive scale disadvantage, retailer consolidation, promotional pressure) are durable.

## Valuation Assessment

UA screens optically cheap by some measures and meaningless by others. The trailing and forward P/E are both negative (-4.76), reflecting the loss, so earnings multiples do not anchor a valuation. Market cap is roughly $1.19B against ~$5.0B in revenue, implying a price-to-sales of about 0.24x, low for the sector but appropriate for a business with no profits, negative free cash flow, and a contracting top line.

Against peers in Apparel, Accessories & Luxury Goods, the picture is mixed and unhelpful. Peer median P/E is 16.7x (G-III 12.5x, Carter's 16.7x, Capri 32.1x), but those are profitable comparables; UA and Oxford Industries (-13.7x) are the loss-makers in the group. The companion Class A shares (UAA) trade at -5.2x and sit 26% below their 52-week high; UA Class C is 26% off its 52-week high of $7.91 as well. There is no clean peer-multiple read because the comparable set is heterogeneous and UA is among the impaired names.

The valuation problem is that cheapness here reflects structural impairment, not a temporary trough with a visible recovery catalyst. A low price-to-sales on a declining, cash-burning business is a value trap absent an identified inflection. The cycle-adjusted argument that normally rescues a high trailing multiple does not apply: there is no estimate-revision tailwind (the March quarter missed by 69.5% and visibility is poor), no revenue acceleration, and no margin expansion. The one credible argument for upside is the Watsa accumulation at $5.50-5.89, which suggests a sophisticated value investor sees intrinsic value well above the current price, but that is a thesis to monitor, not a margin of safety we can underwrite from the fundamentals. We assign a modestly below-neutral valuation score: the absolute price is low, but there is no specific reason to believe the market is mispricing the business to the upside on a 12-month view.

## Competitive Landscape

UA is structurally disadvantaged versus Nike and adidas on scale, marketing budget, sourcing leverage, and retailer shelf relationships, and is exposed to the same tariff and promotional pressures as the broader apparel sector. Its relative strength in EMEA and Latin America contrasts with persistent share loss in North America, where the brand is most mature and most contested.

The trajectory is deteriorating. The premium-reset strategy is a deliberate retreat from volume to defend margin and brand, which is credible in principle but is currently producing top-line decline rather than stabilization. Footwear is both UA's weakest segment and the one just weakened further by the November 2025 departure of Stephen Curry, who in June 2026 signed a reported 10-year, ~$400M deal with Chinese brand Li-Ning. UA thus loses its only marquee signature footwear athlete in its fastest-declining category. The Curry exit removes a large fixed cost but subtracts brand equity in basketball and footwear at exactly the wrong moment.

## Risk Assessment

**U.S. deferred-tax valuation allowance / structural domestic weakness.** The full write-down of U.S. deferred tax assets is the most consequential FY26 development. It is a non-cash drag, it codifies management's pessimism about North American recovery, and it leaves UA unable to recognize tax benefits on future U.S. losses.

**Insurance-carrier litigation (cash risk).** On January 20, 2026, the Fourth Circuit reversed a favorable ruling, requiring UA to repay $90M of previously funded D&O insurance proceeds tied to the old securities-fraud settlement. UA accrued $98.5M (including potential prejudgment interest) and faces a near-term cash outflow if its rehearing petition fails, a meaningful amount against $309M of cash.

**Tariffs and trade-policy uncertainty.** Substantially all products are made offshore. Management expects roughly $80M of COGS impact (~160bps of gross margin) from tariffs, with ongoing IEEPA/trade-policy litigation creating significant FY27 uncertainty. Potential refund recovery is uncertain upside, not a base case.

**Liquidity engineering.** The 7.25% coupon, revolver draws, ballooning supply-chain-finance payables, foreign-earnings repatriation, and capex cuts collectively show a company actively managing a tightening liquidity position even as it maintains it has adequate liquidity for at least the next twelve months.

**Governance and controls.** A material weakness in internal control over financial reporting (balance-sheet reconciliations) was identified in FY24 and FY25. The December 2025 10-Q still reported controls "not effective"; the FY26 10-K claims remediation as of March 31, 2026. Given the company's history of "pull-forward" sales accounting and prior SEC/DOJ scrutiny, the recurrence warrants skepticism. The dual-class structure entrenches Plank's control through Class B super-voting shares; Class C holders have no votes.

**Wholesale concentration.** No long-term customer contracts; exposure to retailer consolidation and promotional pressure.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case
The premium-reset strategy eventually stabilizes gross margin and the brand, North America stops declining, and the company returns to modest revenue growth and positive free cash flow over a multi-year horizon. The Curry separation removes a large fixed cost. Fairfax sees intrinsic value materially above the current price, and a turnaround plus short-covering could re-rate the stock toward $8-9 (above the 52-week high of $7.91). A favorable IEEPA tariff-refund outcome would be incremental upside.

### Bear Case
Revenue continues to erode, North America keeps losing share, free cash flow stays negative, and liquidity tightens further as high-coupon debt service, the $90-98.5M insurance repayment, and supply-chain-finance unwinds compress cash. EBITDA decline pressures the leverage covenant. The Curry exit accelerates footwear's decline. The stock retests the 52-week low of $3.95 or lower (down roughly 33% from current $5.87), a level consistent with a structurally impaired, cash-burning apparel brand with no inflection in sight.

## Short Candidate Assessment

**Catalyst**: There is no specific, dated catalyst that reliably drives the stock materially lower in the near term. The next earnings report is the most natural trigger, and the business is deteriorating, but quarterly results have been volatile, so directionality is not predictable. The insurance-litigation cash outflow is a real overhang but its timing depends on the rehearing-petition outcome, which is undated.

**Timing window**: Diffuse. The secular decline plays out over quarters and years, not on a single dated event. A short would carry across an uncertain window with no clean resolution point.

**Short interest and squeeze risk**: 24.2M shares short with 7.5 days to cover is a crowded position. This is the decisive mechanical problem for a short. With a sophisticated 10%+ holder (Fairfax) actively accumulating at current prices, any operating stabilization or continued block buying could force rapid covering against a substantial short base.

**Borrow and options signal**: The options feed is non-informative. There is no clean options confirmation of the bearish thesis, which removes one of the supports a short would want.

**Downside target and risk/reward**: The plausible downside to the 52-week low (~$3.95) is roughly 33%. The upside risk from a Fairfax-driven re-rating plus short-covering toward the 52-week high (~$7.91) is roughly +35%. That is approximately 1:1, below the 2:1 threshold required to justify the carry and squeeze risk of an active short.

**Verdict**: **CROWDED SHORT** — The bearish fundamental thesis is correct, but short interest is already heavy, a respected value investor is buying aggressively into the decline, and the risk/reward is roughly symmetric. The right action is to avoid owning the stock, not to short it.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Re-entry criteria**: We would revisit a constructive view if (1) North America revenue stabilizes — specifically, the decline narrows to flat or positive year-over-year for two consecutive quarters; (2) operating cash flow and free cash flow turn positive on a trailing basis, demonstrating the premium reset is converting to cash rather than consuming it; (3) the insurance-litigation overhang resolves without a material cash outflow, or is fully absorbed; and (4) gross margin reverses its compression as tariff and promotional pressures ease. Continued accumulation by Fairfax alongside any of these operating signals would meaningfully raise our conviction.

**Timeline**: We expect the headwinds — secular revenue decline, tariff-driven margin pressure, the loss of Curry in footwear, and a tightening liquidity profile — to persist through at least the next two to four quarters. The premium-reset turnaround, if it works, is a multi-year process, and the FY26 data shows it remains pre-inflection. There is no near-term event that resolves the thesis in the bulls' favor.

## Conclusion

Under Armour is a structurally impaired apparel brand in a four-year revenue decline, burning cash, managing a tightening liquidity position with high-coupon debt and supply-chain-finance expansion, and facing a $90-98.5M near-term litigation outflow. The full U.S. deferred-tax write-down is management's own admission that the North American recovery is in doubt, and the loss of Stephen Curry to Li-Ning weakens the already-deteriorating footwear category. The premium-reset strategy is coherent in theory but is currently compounding the top-line decline rather than arresting it, and there is no estimate-revision tailwind or operating inflection to underwrite a recovery on a 12-month view.

Fairfax's accumulation at depressed prices is worth respecting and monitoring, and it is the reason the stock is not an active short. A positioning signal from one investor does not substitute for an operating inflection, and the dual-class structure leaves Class C holders without voting recourse. We rate UA **AVOID**. We would reconsider only on evidence that North America has stabilized and free cash flow has turned positive.
