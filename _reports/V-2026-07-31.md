## Executive Summary

Visa's operating results through the nine months ended June 30, 2026 are the strongest in several years: net revenue $33,764M (+15%), operating income $20,848M for a 61.7% margin (up from ~60.0% in FY2025), net income $17,502M (+17%), and GAAP diluted EPS of $9.14 (+20%). Value-added services revenue reached $10.3B in nine months, +33%, versus ~$10.9B for all of FY2025. Fiscal Q3 payments volume exceeded $4 trillion in a single quarter for the first time (+10%), with cross-border volume +13% — the highest-yield line growing roughly three points faster than total volume. Consensus EPS estimates have risen ~1.0% over 90 days for both FY2026 ($13.20) and FY2027 ($14.95).

The largest overhang is closer to resolution than at any point in the last decade. The amended U.S. injunctive-relief settlement received preliminary approval on June 9, 2026, the motion for final approval was filed July 15, 2026, and roughly 95% of opt-out claim volume has been settled with all April 2026 trials resolved. The cash consequence is already visible: the litigation escrow fell to $888M from $2,990M against ~$3.1B of litigation payments in the period, which is the entire reason operating cash flow declined to $16,342M from $16,821M while net income rose 17%. That drag mechanically reverses once the U.S. obligations are discharged.

What keeps this a HOLD rather than a BUY is the combination of price and three unquantified legal vectors. At $366.27 the stock trades at 27.7x FY2026 and 24.5x FY2027 consensus for a business consensus expects to grow EPS ~13% next year — a PEG near 1.8 and roughly parity with Mastercard on forward earnings, so there is no peer-relative discount to harvest. Meanwhile the settlement reportedly modifies the honor-all-cards rule and expands brand-level surcharging, which attacks the premium-rewards flywheel Visa itself identifies as its highest revenue-per-cardholder cohort; a February 18, 2026 UK Competition Appeal Tribunal decision removed Visa's pass-on defense and is under appeal, plausibly opening a European accrual cycle as the U.S. one closes; and the September 2024 DOJ debit-monopolization suit has an unverified procedural status. This is the fourth consecutive HOLD on this name, and the bar for repeating it should be high. The reason it repeats is that the improvement in fundamentals (revenue acceleration, VAS mix, settlement progress) has been matched by a 25% move off the April low, leaving base-case upside of roughly 6% against bear-case downside near 20%.

## Company Overview

Visa operates VisaNet inside what its 10-K calls the four-party model — consumer, issuer, acquirer, seller. The structurally important point, which management repeats and which is legally load-bearing, is that Visa is not a financial institution: it does not issue cards, extend credit, set cardholder rates, bear credit risk, or earn revenue derived from interchange reimbursement fees or merchant discount rates. It sets its own fees to issuers and acquirers independently of interchange. Visa sets *default* interchange rates; it does not collect them.

Scale, per the FY2025 10-K: 329 billion Visa-branded payments and cash transactions, of which 258 billion were processed on VisaNet; $17 trillion of total payments and cash volume; ~5 billion credentials; >175 million merchant acceptance locations; ~14,500 financial-institution clients. The 71 billion gap between branded and processed transactions — about 22% of branded count — is the practical measure of processing disintermediation, driven largely by European regulatory unbundling and U.S. debit routing mandates.

Revenue comes in five reported lines: service revenue (indexed to prior-quarter payments volume), data processing, international transaction (cross-border plus FX, the highest-yield line), other, and client incentives as a contra-revenue item. The three stated growth pillars are Consumer Payments (>$40T addressable spend ex-Russia/China, >$20T still cash or non-card electronic), Commercial & Money Movement Solutions (Visa Direct processed >12.5 billion transactions for >650 partners in FY2025), and Value-Added Services, which management sizes at a ~$520B annual revenue opportunity across issuing, acceptance, risk and advisory.

## Financial Analysis

**Margin structure and earnings quality.** FY2025 delivered $23,994M of operating income on ~$40.0B of net revenue (60.0% margin), $20,058M of net income (50.1% margin), on a 17.1% effective tax rate. Nine-month FY2026 operating margin expanded to 61.7% despite absorbing a $563M severance charge in the June quarter, which pushed quarterly personnel expense up roughly 40% year over year. Revenue growing 15% against payments volume growing 10% implies yield expansion, and the visible driver is VAS at +33%.

**One unexplained item.** FY2024 net income of $19,743M grew only 1.6% to $20,058M in FY2025 against double-digit revenue growth. The most likely explanation is litigation provisioning or other non-operating items in one or both years, but I did not isolate the line. FY2026 year-to-date results are inconsistent with operating deterioration, so I treat this as an accounting-comparability gap rather than evidence of business weakness — but it is a gap.

**Cash flow.** Nine-month operating cash flow of $16,342M was below the prior-year $16,821M while net income rose 17%. The 10-Q attributes this to ~$3.1B of litigation payments plus higher client incentive payments. Corroborating balance-sheet evidence: escrow down to $888M from $2,990M and accrued litigation of $1,274M. Note the definitional distinction — the ~$2.1B escrow decline and the ~$3.1B of payments are different measures, and the difference implies additional escrow funding during the period. Adding back the ~$3.1B of legacy settlement cash gives underlying nine-month operating cash flow near $19.4B. I am not extrapolating a full-year figure from that, because fiscal Q4 is seasonally distinct and incentive timing is lumpy; the directional point is that once U.S. covered litigation is discharged, roughly $3B/year of cash returns to discretionary use. On ~1,880M as-converted shares at $365.68 (~$687B), $3B of incremental annual repurchase retires about 0.44% of the as-converted base per year.

**Balance sheet and contingent exposures.** Two disclosures are more informative than the standardized solvency screens. First, commercial paper authorization increased from $3.0B to $7.0B in July 2026 — an authorization, not borrowings, and outstanding CP was not verified. A more-than-doubling of the ceiling is plausibly bridging capacity for litigation payments, buybacks and the Argentina acquisition; it could equally be routine housekeeping. Second, new Brazilian regulation will require Visa to extend settlement guarantees to sellers, not only acquirers, expanding the set of counterparties to whom Visa is obligated on intermediary failure. Neither is quantified.

A standardized Altman Z screen returns a grey-zone reading for Visa. That is a model artifact: Z penalizes low asset turnover, low retained earnings relative to assets, and low working capital, and Visa's balance sheet carries $30B+ of goodwill and intangibles from Visa Europe and subsequent M&A plus settlement receivables, customer collateral and litigation escrow that generate no revenue. Against $16B+ of nine-month operating cash flow and modest debt service, this is not a solvency signal.

**Share count and the multiple-basis trap.** Visa reports ~1,702M Class A shares at June 30, 2026 and ~1,880M on an as-converted basis including Class B/B-1/B-2/B-3 and C. The quoted $606.9B market cap implies ~1,660M shares — Class A only, and slightly stale. As-converted equity value is approximately $687B, about 13% higher. This matters for EV/EBITDA, EV/sales and market-cap screens, which understate Visa's valuation by roughly that margin. It does *not* distort P/E, because reported diluted EPS and sell-side consensus are already on the as-converted basis. Separately, the supplied EPS field of $10.20 does not reconcile with the supplied 31.12x P/E at $365.68 (which implies ~$11.75 of trailing EPS); the P/E is consistent with trailing four-quarter GAAP diluted EPS of roughly $11.8, and I use the consensus forward figures rather than the stale EPS field.

A May 2026 Class B exchange offer converted ~3M B-1 and ~120M B-2 shares into ~61M B-3 and ~23M C shares, with an associated Makewhole Agreement disclosed by 8-K. The economics: Class B holders bear U.S. covered-litigation losses through downward conversion-rate adjustment; each exchange releases participating holders from further indemnification and shifts residual covered-litigation economics toward Class A. I did not read the Makewhole Agreement and cannot quantify the transferred exposure. The as-converted count fell ~2.6% from ~1,930M to ~1,880M, driven by buybacks rather than by the exchange.

## Growth Analysis

The growth picture is genuinely good and broad-based. Nine-month net revenue +15%, net income +17%, GAAP diluted EPS +20%, non-GAAP diluted EPS $9.79 (+15%). Fiscal Q3 revenue of $11.633B beat, and reported adjusted EPS of $3.32 exceeded the structured consensus of $3.29 by 0.8% (an external summary cited a lower $3.22 consensus, implying a 3.1% beat — the estimate bases differ, and I use the more internally consistent structured figure). Payments volume crossed $4 trillion in a quarter for the first time at +10%; cross-border volume rose 13% (+12% ex intra-Europe).

Two qualifiers on the cross-border number. Visa is a FIFA World Cup 2026 sponsor, and management attributed part of the cross-border strength to World Cup–related travel, so there is a one-time seasonal component embedded in FY2026 that should be normalized out of FY2027 expectations. And cross-border is the line most exposed to a consumer slowdown, discretionary travel being the first spending category to compress.

VAS at +33% to $10.3B in nine months is the most important structural development. On a crude run-rate basis that tracks toward ~$13.7B annualized, roughly 34% of revenue. VAS is largely fee-for-service, not indexed to interchange, and increasingly network-agnostic — Visa is deliberately selling fraud and risk products to competing rails. Two caveats. The +33% is not disaggregated between organic and acquired growth, and Featurespace (December 2024), Pismo, Tink and the ~$1.5B Argentine platform (February 2026) all contribute. More importantly, a change in *product* mix is not the same as reduced *counterparty* dependency: VAS is sold overwhelmingly to the same ~14,500 financial institutions whose economics depend on interchange. If interchange compresses, issuer budgets for Visa's issuing and cardholder-engagement products compress with it. No direct customer-concentration disclosure was located, so I cannot claim diversification of counterparty risk from the mix shift.

**On the estimate-revision signal:** it is positive but modest. FY2026 consensus rose 0.4% over 30 days and 1.0% over 90 days; FY2027 rose 0.5% and 1.0%. The current quarter was trimmed 0.3%. This is steady positive momentum, not the >30% ninety-day revision velocity that marks an earnings inflection. The four-quarter surprise record is two beats and two misses with magnitudes between -1.5% and +4.8%, which argues against assuming large forward beats. I am therefore treating consensus as a reasonable central case rather than a floor to be exceeded.

The peer median of 21.0x is not a usable benchmark: the set is heterogeneous and contains impaired assets (PYPL at 7.7x and 29% below its high; FISV at 9.0x and 70% below its high) alongside XYZ at 52.5x. Mastercard at 28.1x trailing is the only true comparable, and Visa at ~31x trailing / 27.7x forward is at approximate parity — a modest trailing premium that closes on forward estimates. There is no peer-relative discount to arbitrage.

The bull argument on valuation is the five-year de-rating documented in Visa's own proxy. Cumulative TSR indexed to 100 stands at 176.94 for Visa versus 245.77 for its designated peer group at FY2025, with Visa lagging in each of the five disclosed years — roughly 69 percentage points of cumulative underperformance while net income compounded 13.0% annually and adjusted EPS 17.8%. That gap is best explained by multiple compression tied to litigation, regulation and the real-time-payments narrative, not by operating shortfall. With the U.S. MDL at final-approval stage and revenue accelerating, the setup for partial multiple recovery is the best it has been in that window.

Two problems with leaning on it. The peer group is company-selected and may be weighted toward higher-beta payments and fintech names whose multiples expanded more, making part of the gap a selection artifact. And a five-year de-rating is a long time for the market to be wrong; the alternative reading is that the market has been correctly capitalizing a structural threat to per-transaction economics. Notably, the de-rating has already partially reversed — the stock is up ~25% from the April 1 low of $293.89 and sits 2% below its 52-week high, so a meaningful portion of the settlement-resolution and revenue-acceleration case is now in the price.

Anchoring the base case: 26x FY2027 consensus EPS of $14.95 gives $389, about 6% above spot. That multiple assumes modest recovery from today's 24.5x as litigation clears, which I regard as the central outcome. Getting to the $412 consensus mean requires 27.6x FY2027, and to the $420 median requires 28.1x — achievable but requiring both clean settlement mechanics and no European accrual cycle. Valuation scores 5.5: the multiple is defensible against the growth and margin profile, there is no structural impairment, but there is no identified mispricing large enough to produce asymmetric upside at this price.

## Competitive Landscape

Visa's absolute position is exceptional. Nilson CY2024 data in the 10-K puts Visa payments volume at $13,433B versus Mastercard $8,014B (1.68x), transactions at 311B versus 204B (1.52x), and cards at 4,805M versus 3,146M. The comparison carries footnote caveats (Mastercard excludes Maestro/Cirrus; Amex/Discover/JCB are credit-only), so it is directionally strong rather than precise. The 60%+ operating margin corroborates the scale advantage.

Trajectory is where the score comes down. Three specific pressures:

**Processing unbundling.** Item 1 states that as a result of the EU/UK Interchange Fee Regulation, other networks and processors may process Visa transactions directly with issuers and acquirers, and Visa Europe must separate scheme from processing for accounting, organizational and decision-making purposes. When Visa keeps the brand but loses processing, it earns service revenue and forgoes data processing revenue — a direct yield-compression mechanism, and part of the 329B-versus-258B gap.

**Real-time payment rails.** The 10-K concedes RTP networks have launched in at least 80 countries "driven by strong government sponsorship," names FedNow, PIX and UPI, and states that cross-border RTP linkages such as PayNow–UPI "will compete with our cross-border business" — the highest-yield line. China, India, Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam and South Africa are named as imposing domestic-provider, local-ownership, data-localization or domestic-processing requirements. Visa's counter is two-track: pull A2A flows onto card rails (Visa Pay, Flex Credential) and sell services to competing rails. The literal relationship matters here: when an RTP scheme buys Visa risk-scoring, that operator is Visa's customer for services while competing for the transaction. Visa can lose transaction economics and gain service fees in the same relationship, and the net effect on yield per transaction is not disclosed.

**The settlement's rule changes.** Reported terms include an honor-all-cards modification permitting merchants to reject certain card categories and expanded, brand-differentiated surcharging rights. These are externally reported and unconfirmed against the agreement itself. If adopted at scale, they pressure the premium/affluent card segment that Item 1 identifies as generating significantly higher cross-border, credit and ecommerce spend per cardholder — degrading issuer rewards economics and potentially slowing the premium mix shift that has driven Visa's yield. The counterargument is that surcharging rights already exist in many U.S. states and in Australia with modest observed uptake, constrained by merchants' reluctance to alienate customers.

Where Visa remains structurally defended: acceptance footprint (175M locations, 5B credentials) that no domestic scheme can replicate; cross-border and FX, where RTP alternatives are overwhelmingly domestic and cross-border linkages nascent; and fraud/risk data scale trained on 258B processed transactions, deliberately built network-agnostic. Against that, the 10-K explicitly acknowledges that generative AI "can create new and better offerings that compete with our value-added services" — Visa positions AI as both its growth vector and a threat to its fastest-growing revenue line.

Competitive position: 7.5. Best-in-class level, mildly deteriorating trajectory on rules and rails.

## Risk Assessment

**1. Settlement rule changes (largest unquantified item).** The widely reported "$38 billion" figure is characterized in press coverage as the estimated value of interchange reductions to merchants over five years, not a cash payment by Visa — Visa does not collect interchange. The behavioral terms (0.1pp interchange reduction, honor-all-cards modification, expanded surcharging) are the real exposure and are unconfirmed against primary documents. Final approval has not been confirmed as granted, and Walmart-led objectors who filed in December 2025 retain appeal rights.

**2. Europe as the next litigation cycle.** New UK High Court claims were filed April–June 2026, and the February 18, 2026 UK Competition Appeal Tribunal decision found that, except in certain merchant categories, interchange was *not* passed on by merchants — removing Visa's pass-on defense and increasing the merchant damages base. Visa has sought permission to appeal. This is a liability-stage ruling subject to appeal and per-claimant quantification, and UK/EEA interchange has been IFR-capped for years, constraining the overcharge base. The exposure is not sized anywhere in the disclosures reviewed, which is a genuine gap. The consequence is that the free-cash-flow relief from U.S. escrow exhaustion may be partly offset rather than fully realized.

**3. DOJ debit monopolization suit — status unverified.** DOJ sued in September 2024 alleging monopolization of U.S. debit. I could not establish the current procedural posture (motion to dismiss, discovery, trial date). It must not be assumed resolved. The theory targets network exclusivity and merchant routing — an adverse outcome would most plausibly attack the debit-routing incentive agreements that run through the client incentives contra-revenue line, Visa's primary competitive tool against Mastercard.

**4. Regulation II in legal limbo.** A North Dakota court vacated Regulation II in August 2025 while a Kentucky court reached the opposite result, with appeal pending, and the Fed has separately proposed lowering the debit cap. Visa's exposure is indirect but debit economics drive issuer willingness to route over VisaNet.

**5. Consumer volume exposure.** Payments volume growth of 10% and cross-border of 13% are levered to discretionary spending, and a slowdown in U.S. and cross-border discretionary spend is the most likely path to consensus FY2027 EPS being cut. It is not reflected in a 24.5x forward multiple.

**6. Balance-sheet scope expansion.** Brazil's seller settlement-guarantee regulation expands guaranteed counterparties without a quantified estimate. The Argentina acquisition booked ~$1,034M of goodwill on ~$1.5B of consideration (~69%), normal for a processing platform but carrying impairment exposure in a volatile macro. Four global data centers are disclosed as critical to the processing environment, making availability and cybersecurity risk existential for a network whose stated value proposition is reliability.

**Partial offsets:** ~95% of U.S. opt-out claim volume settled, April 2026 trials resolved, escrow down to $888M and accrued litigation to $1,274M — the U.S. tail is materially smaller than two years ago. Short interest is 1.4% of float with 2.4 days to cover, so there is no crowded bearish position to unwind in either direction.

Risk factors: 5.5. Known risks are partly priced through the five-year de-rating, but three live vectors are unquantified and one has unverified status.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull case ($440–450, +20% to +23%).** Final approval of the U.S. settlement lands without material objector delay, and merchant adoption of surcharging and card rejection proves as limited as the Australian and U.S. state precedents suggest. Roughly $3B/year of litigation cash returns to buybacks, accelerating as-converted share retirement beyond the 2.6% achieved between FY2025 and June 2026. VAS continues compounding above 25%, lifting the mix toward 35%+ of revenue and reducing sensitivity to interchange rate setting. The $563M severance charge produces visible FY2027 operating leverage on a 61.7% base margin. The multiple recovers toward 29x FY2027 EPS of $14.95 as the five-year litigation discount unwinds, supported by the same relative-TSR gap Visa discloses in its own proxy.

**Bear case ($290–300, -18% to -21%).** Merchant surcharging and card-category rejection gain real traction, issuer rewards budgets compress, and the premium credit mix shift that has driven yield expansion stalls — visible first as decelerating service and international transaction revenue. The UK Court of Appeal declines Visa's appeal of the pass-on ruling and a European accrual cycle builds, absorbing the escrow-driven FCF relief. A DOJ adverse ruling or settlement constrains debit routing incentives. Simultaneously, an H2 2026 consumer slowdown pulls payments volume growth toward mid-single digits and cross-border below 8% as the World Cup comparison rolls off. FY2027 EPS is trimmed toward $14.50 and the multiple compresses to 20x.

**Base case ($389, +6%).** 26x FY2027 consensus EPS of $14.95. Payments volume growth normalizes to 8-9% as World Cup effects fade, cross-border to 9-10%, VAS decelerates to 20-25% as acquisition contributions anniversary, and net revenue growth settles around 11-12%. Settlement final approval is granted; surcharging adoption is real but modest. The European exposure builds slowly and is provisioned incrementally rather than in a step change. Multiple recovers modestly from 24.5x as the U.S. overhang clears, offset by the new European vector.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Recommendation: HOLD (growth_hold).** There is a positive twelve-month thesis — settlement discharge, FCF step-up, VAS mix, partial multiple recovery — but at 24.5x FY2027 within 2% of the 52-week high, base-case upside of ~6% against bear-case downside of ~20% does not support adding. Own the position; wait for a better entry.

- **Base case target:** $389 (26x FY2027E $14.95) — **+6.2%** from $366.27
- **Bull case target:** $440–450 (29x–30x FY2027E) — **+20% to +23%**
- **Bear case target:** $290–300 (20x trimmed FY2027E ~$14.50) — **-18% to -21%**
- **Timeframe:** 12 months, anchored to fiscal Q4 results and FY2027 guidance (late October 2026), the final-approval order on the interchange settlement, and the UK Court of Appeal permission decision.

**Upgrade to BUY on any of:**
1. Price into the low $320s (≈21.5x FY2027E), which would restore ~20% base-case upside against the same bear case.
2. Final approval order confirming settlement mechanics, combined with FY2027 guidance showing operating cash flow stepping above $22B as litigation payments normalize.
3. FY2027 consensus revisions accelerating above +5% over a 90-day window — the current +1.0% is steady, not inflecting.
4. Verified favorable resolution or dismissal of the DOJ debit suit.

**Downgrade to AVOID on any of:**
1. Payments volume growth decelerating below 7%, or cross-border below 8% ex-World-Cup comparison, in two consecutive quarters.
2. A UK/EEA litigation accrual above $2B, or denial of permission to appeal the CAT pass-on ruling accompanied by damages quantification.
3. Client incentives rising while net revenue growth decelerates below 10% — the specific combination that signals competitive intensity outpacing volume.
4. Evidence of material merchant adoption of brand-level surcharging or card-category rejection showing up as decelerating service and international transaction revenue yield.

**Reassessment point:** fiscal Q4 FY2026 results and initial FY2027 guidance, expected late October 2026.

## Conclusion

Visa is executing well. Fifteen percent nine-month revenue growth with a 61.7% operating margin, VAS at +33%, the first $4 trillion payments-volume quarter, and cross-border outgrowing total volume by three points constitute a strong operating record, and the U.S. interchange overhang that has weighed on the multiple for five years is at final-approval stage with the escrow drawn down to $888M. The proxy's own pay-versus-performance disclosure quantifies the de-rating: 69 percentage points of cumulative five-year underperformance against Visa's designated peer group while net income compounded 13% annually.

Three things prevent that from being a BUY at $366. First, roughly 25% of the de-rating has already reversed since April, and 24.5x FY2027 consensus for 13% EPS growth at approximate parity with Mastercard leaves no valuation cushion. Second, the settlement resolves cash exposure while changing operating rules in ways that pressure the premium-card economics driving Visa's yield expansion, and those terms remain unconfirmed against primary documents. Third, the U.S. accrual cycle is closing as a European one plausibly opens, with the February 2026 CAT pass-on ruling adverse and unsized, and the DOJ debit suit of unverified status. The overall score of 6.75 marks a marginal further decline from 6.9, reflecting the newly surfaced European vector rather than any deterioration in the operating business — the Q3 print was a beat and revisions are positive.

Hold the position for the free-cash-flow step-up and the partial multiple recovery. Add in the low $320s, or on FY2027 guidance that converts the escrow discharge into a visible cash-flow inflection.
