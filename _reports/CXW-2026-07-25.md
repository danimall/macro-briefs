**Research Date: July 25, 2026**

## Executive Summary

CoreCivic is the largest owner of partnership correctional and detention real estate in the U.S. and, together with GEO Group, one half of a private-detention duopoly that has become intensely levered to federal immigration enforcement. The FY2025–Q1 2026 results are the strongest in the company's history: FY25 revenue rose 12.7% to ~$2.21B, Q1'26 revenue jumped 25.8% YoY to $614.7M, ICE revenue nearly doubled to ~$261M, and diluted EPS climbed 65% to $0.38. The engine is the July 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which appropriated $45B for detention capacity through September 2029, and the January 2025 executive orders that catalyzed CoreCivic's activation of five previously idle facilities carrying roughly $500M of combined annual revenue.

The stock has responded, rising 57% over the trailing twelve months to ~$31.35, sitting just below its 52-week high of $32.69. The single largest recent event is the July 2, 2026 sale of the California City and Otay Mesa detention centers to DHS/ICE for ~$1.5B gross (~$1.1B net after tax), a transaction equal to roughly a third of the market cap. This is an asset-value crystallization rather than a recurring earnings event, and it is precisely the "government buys instead of rents" risk the 10-K explicitly flagged. The proceeds provide meaningful deleveraging and buyback firepower against $1.41B of debt at a 7.3% weighted-average rate and a $256M remaining repurchase authorization.

The tension in the story is that the headline momentum is decelerating even as YoY comps look spectacular. National ICE detention population peaked near 70,800 in late January 2026 and fell ~15% (roughly 10,500) by early April on a DHS-centered government shutdown, a leadership reorganization, and redeployment of ICE agents; CoreCivic's own ICE census dropped ~3,000 in the same window. The entire growth thesis rests on immigration-enforcement policy under one administration, contracts are largely terminable for convenience or non-appropriation, and the core real-estate moat is being partially disintermediated by the government's own build-and-buy strategy. At ~26x trailing earnings on a business whose highest-visibility catalyst monetizes rather than compounds its assets, we rate CXW a HOLD.

## Company Overview

CoreCivic operates through three segments. **CoreCivic Safety** (~92% of segment net operating income) comprises 44 correctional and detention facilities with roughly 68,000 beds, 40 of them owned or long-term leased, plus the TransCor prisoner-transportation business. **CoreCivic Community** (~5%) runs 20 residential reentry centers (~4,000 beds) plus electronic monitoring and case management. **CoreCivic Properties** (~3%) leases five facilities to government agencies (~8,000 beds).

The company converted from a REIT to a taxable C-corporation in 2021. Its durable advantage is owned real estate: the U.S. has a structurally constrained supply of correctional and detention beds, high barriers to build (zoning and community opposition), and CoreCivic owns the majority of the beds it operates, which supports historically high contract renewal rates. Its principal competitor is GEO Group; it also competes against government-run facilities. Functionally this is a government-services and real-estate hybrid, with revenue heavily concentrated in federal immigration enforcement.

## Financial Analysis

Q1 2026 is the pivotal data point. Revenue of $614.7M grew 25.8% YoY from $488.6M. Net income rose 51% to $37.9M ($0.38 diluted, up 65% from $0.23). Federal revenue climbed 48.4% to $358.1M, with ICE revenue roughly doubling to ~$261M from ~$133M. Facility net operating income rose 29% to $147M, portfolio operating margin ticked up to 24.0% from 23.6%, average compensated occupancy improved to 79.6% from 77.0%, and revenue per compensated man-day rose 13.5% to $116.53.

The earnings-surprise record supports current estimates on a near-term basis: three beats and one miss over four quarters, with Q1'26 beating by 36.0% ($0.38 vs. $0.28), Q4'25 by 39.9%, and Q2'25 by 78.2%. Consensus revisions are moving modestly higher — current-year EPS at $1.64 (up 2.2% in 30 days, up 1.6% in 90 days), next-year at $2.13 (up 7.3% in 30 days). The current-quarter estimate was cut 13.4% over 90 days even as full-year and next-year estimates edged up, consistent with the population-decline drag flowing through near-term numbers while the longer-dated view still embeds the OBBBA build-out.

The balance sheet carries measurable stress relative to a typical support-services peer. Total debt was $1.41B at 3/31/26, up from $1.23B at year-end 2025, at a 7.3% weighted-average rate and 3.6-year average maturity. The stack includes $500M of 8.25% notes (2029), $238.5M of 4.75% notes (2027), a $110M term loan, $425M drawn on the revolver, and a $133M non-recourse Kansas mortgage. The 8.25% coupon reflects a structural cost of capital penalty tied to lender and ESG restrictions on private-prison financing. The company is within its 4.5x leverage covenant, expanded the revolver from $275M to $575M in December 2025, and added a $100M incremental term loan in April 2026. Cash was $209.7M.

Operating cash flow is the item to watch. Q1'26 operating cash flow was only $13.8M versus $44.5M a year earlier, driven by a ~$78M working-capital drag from receivables build on new ICE contracts plus government-shutdown payment delays. This is a timing effect rather than a profitability problem, but it coincides with the population decline and bears monitoring. Capex is falling sharply as activations complete — $75M in 2025 dropping to $40–45M in 2026 — which frees cash for capital return. In Q1'26 the company repurchased $44.7M (2.3M shares) at ~$19; cumulatively it has retired ~28.1M shares (~$444M), cutting share count roughly 10% YoY from ~110M to ~99M, with $255.8M remaining on a $700M authorization. No dividend.

The DHS sale, closing July 2, 2026 and netting ~$1.1B after tax, dwarfs the remaining buyback authorization and, layered on falling capex, points to significant deleveraging and potential further share reduction. It should be modeled as a one-time asset monetization, not recurring earnings.

## Growth Analysis

The reported growth is real and large, but its composition matters. The five reactivated ICE facilities — Dilley (2,400 beds, ~$180M annual revenue), California City (2,560), Diamondback (2,160), Midwest/Leavenworth (1,033), and West Tennessee (600) — carry ~$500M of combined annual revenue and are the bulk of the YoY step-up. That activation cycle is now largely complete, which is why capex drops by roughly half in 2026. Much of the 2026 YoY comparison is lapping the 2025 activations, so the exceptional headline growth rate will not repeat mechanically.

Against that, three headwinds compress the forward growth path. First, the ICE detention population already fell ~15% nationally from its late-January peak and ~3,000 in CoreCivic's own care by early April 2026. Even within a supportive administration, detention demand proved politically and operationally volatile. Second, one of the growth facilities (California City) has now been sold to the government; if the company retains only a management fee rather than ownership economics, the revenue and margin profile of those beds degrades even if the census holds. Third, 35 contracts representing ~31% of FY25 revenue ($688M) were scheduled to expire on or before 12/31/26, with 13 carrying no renewal option — a recurring re-bid risk.

The offsetting positives are the OBBBA's $45B multi-year detention appropriation through 2029 (the largest ever), the CSP pharmacy acquisition (~$148M) that adds recurring, higher-margin, less-politically-exposed correctional-healthcare revenue across 600+ facilities in 28 states, and the Farmville acquisition (736-bed ICE facility). We view growth prospects as positive in aggregate but decelerating from an unrepeatable 2025 activation surge, with a genuine near-term census headwind not yet fully visible in trailing comps.

## Valuation Assessment

At ~$31.35 the stock trades at ~26x trailing EPS ($1.08) and ~19x current-year consensus EPS ($1.64), or ~15x next-year ($2.13). The peer-comparable set in Diversified Support Services is heterogeneous and of limited use — UNF at 37x, KBR at 10x, several peers with negative or non-meaningful multiples (OPLN -49x, VSTS -102x), and a peer-median P/E of 37.3 that is distorted by these outliers. The more relevant anchors are CXW's own history and the nature of the earnings.

Two considerations argue against a high valuation score. The estimate-revision profile does not meet the inflection-buy bar — current-year revisions are up only 1.6% over 90 days and the current-quarter estimate was cut 13.4%, so this is not the >30% revision velocity that would justify treating a rich trailing multiple as trough earnings. And the marquee transaction crystallizes asset value rather than compounding it: the sale converts owned, appreciating, cash-flowing real estate into a management fee (or, worse, into no ongoing economics), so it supports the balance sheet but does not by itself expand the multiple the market should pay for recurring earnings. The government's willingness to pay ~$1.5B for two facilities raises a question about the mark-to-market value of the remaining owned portfolio, and that is the strongest bull argument for a higher number, but it is speculative and depends on further sales the market cannot yet size.

Against a 57% twelve-month run, the analyst consensus target band ($30 low, $36 median, $41 high, $36.4 mean) implies modest upside to the midpoint and puts the current price near the low end of estimates. We do not treat those targets as ceilings mechanically, but at ~19x current-year earnings on a policy-dependent, decelerating-census business, the multiple is not obviously cheap relative to the risk. Historically, discounted valuation multiples in this system have been anti-predictive absent a specific catalyst, and here the specific catalyst (the DHS sale) is one-time and double-edged. We score valuation modestly below neutral: the price fairly reflects the good news and does not offer asymmetric upside at current levels.

## Competitive Landscape

CoreCivic's competitive position is stable at the operating level but structurally challenged at the real-estate level. The near-term moat — owned beds, constrained supply, high build barriers, strong renewal history — remains intact, and the duopoly with GEO Group is not threatened by new private entrants. The problem is the customer. ICE is deliberately diversifying its detention footprint across military bases (including Guantanamo), soft-sided/tent facilities, BOP facilities, state and local facilities, warehouses, and outright government ownership. The 10-K explicitly warns that governmental ownership of detention facilities "could divert potential occupancy from our facilities," and that operating a facility the company has sold "may be less profitable than our ownership."

The DHS purchase is the clearest expression of this dynamic: the government's structural intent to own rather than rent, executed against CoreCivic's own assets. The company's response — the CSP pharmacy acquisition to build recurring correctional-healthcare revenue — is a sensible hedge but small relative to the detention base. The trajectory of the core competitive advantage is therefore mildly deteriorating: the operating relationships persist, but the ownership economics that underpin the durable moat are being partially disintermediated by the largest customer. That trajectory, not the current level of market leadership, drives a below-neutral score.

## Risk Assessment

**Policy and customer concentration.** ICE, USMS, and BOP together were 54% of FY25 revenue ($1.19B); ICE alone was 35% ($770.7M) and rising. The entire growth thesis depends on immigration-enforcement policy under the current administration. A future administration could reverse course, as the Biden administration did with a 2021 DOJ order, and contracts are generally terminable for convenience or non-appropriation. This is a binary, high-magnitude risk that a normal cyclical framing would understate.

**Population decline already underway.** The national and company-specific detention decline between late January and early April 2026 is the most under-appreciated near-term headwind, and it is masked by strong YoY comps against the 2025 activation trough.

**Government ownership.** The build/buy-versus-rent dynamic, now made concrete by the sale, threatens the long-term value of the owned portfolio even where management contracts are retained.

**Legislative and litigation.** California's proposed AB1633 would impose a 50% gross-receipts tax on private detention operators from January 1, 2027; several states (CA, CO, NJ, MD, IL, OR, WA) restrict state/local ICE detention contracts. Active litigation includes the California City provisional class certification (injunctive relief could impair economics), the long-running Otay Mesa forced-labor class action, a DOJ CRIPA investigation into Trousdale Turner, and a $27.8M Montana verdict (settled, largely insured).

**Financing access and political optics.** ESG-driven lending restrictions raise the cost of capital (the 8.25% notes). Separately, CXW's appearance in the President's financial disclosure, with the sale closing days later, creates headline and political-reversal risk that a standardized screen would miss; press framing describes a personal investment holding, not an operational relationship, but the optics are a governance risk regardless.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull case.** The OBBBA appropriation underwrites multi-year demand; the census decline is a shutdown-and-reorganization air pocket that reverses as DHS funding normalizes. The DHS sale crystallizes ~$1.1B after tax, funds aggressive deleveraging and buybacks (~10% share reduction already achieved, with far more firepower now), and reveals a rich private-market value for the remaining owned portfolio. Falling capex plus asset-sale proceeds drive a large capital-return cycle, and the CSP pharmacy deal diversifies toward recurring, higher-margin revenue. If the government keeps paying ~$1.5B for pairs of facilities, the SOTP value of CXW's real estate exceeds the current equity market cap. Bull target ~$41 (top of analyst range), on the market re-rating the balance-sheet transformation and portfolio-monetization optionality.

**Bear case.** Detention population continues to decline as immigration policy hits operational and political limits; the government's build/buy strategy accelerates, converting owned, cash-flowing assets into thin management fees or nothing; AB1633 imposes a 50% California gross-receipts tax in 2027; and the 2028 election cycle reintroduces reversal risk. The YoY growth was a one-time activation surge lapping easy comps, operating cash flow stays pressured, and the 8.25% cost of debt caps re-rating. Bear target ~$22 (roughly -30% from current), a de-rating to low-double-digit forward earnings on recurring, ex-monetization earnings power with a shrinking real-estate moat.

**Base case.** The company completes deleveraging and buybacks, retains most management contracts on sold facilities at reduced economics, and the census stabilizes below peak. Recurring EPS settles around the $1.64–$2.13 consensus range. At ~15–17x normalized forward earnings, that supports roughly $33–$36. Base target ~$34, modestly above current price and consistent with the analyst median of $36 net of policy risk.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

This is a HOLD with an uncertain rationale: the near-term catalyst has largely played out in the price, the census trend is a real headwind, and the binary policy exposure prevents a confident directional call in either direction.

**Reassessment timeline:** Next earnings report (Q2 2026, expected early August), which will show whether the census decline reversed or persisted, the after-tax proceeds deployment, and any confirmation of "more sales coming."

**Upgrade triggers (to BUY):**
- ICE census stabilizes or reverses the Q1 decline, with compensated occupancy holding above ~78%, alongside confirmation that CoreCivic retains ownership-equivalent economics (not thin management fees) on sold facilities.
- Management deploys the ~$1.1B proceeds into a combination of debt reduction (retiring the 8.25% notes) and buybacks that meaningfully lowers cost of capital and share count, with a resulting forward P/E under ~13x on stabilized earnings.
- A clean sum-of-the-parts case emerges — additional facility sales at comparable ~$1.5B-per-pair valuations that mark the remaining owned portfolio well above the current equity value.

**Downgrade triggers (to AVOID):**
- ICE detention population continues to fall through Q2/Q3 2026, or company ICE census declines a further ~10%+ from the April level.
- AB1633 (California 50% gross-receipts tax) passes, or additional states enact detention-contract restrictions.
- Operating cash flow remains depressed for a second consecutive quarter absent a clear working-capital reversal, signaling the receivables build is structural rather than timing.
- A credible 2028 policy-reversal signal or a material contract non-renewal within the ~$688M expiring cohort.

## Conclusion

CoreCivic has executed well on the immigration-enforcement capex wave and just completed a $1.5B asset monetization that transforms its balance sheet. But the stock's 57% run has priced in most of the good news, the reported growth is decelerating and lapping unrepeatable 2025 activations, the ICE census has already declined from its peak, and the marquee transaction converts the company's core real-estate moat into lower-margin fees while confirming the government's intent to own rather than rent. Revenue concentration of 54% in federal contracts tied to a single administration's policy is a binary risk that the current ~19x forward multiple does not adequately compensate. The capital-return firepower and portfolio-monetization optionality are genuine, but they are offset by a deteriorating competitive trajectory and elevated legislative, litigation, and political-optics risk.

The score trajectory (6.6 → 6.2 → 5.3, with valuation and management-quality dimensions leading the decline) reflects the shift from a clean growth-and-activation story to a more ambiguous monetization-and-deceleration setup, and our current work confirms rather than reverses that trend. We rate CXW a HOLD, with the next earnings report as the decisive reassessment point.
