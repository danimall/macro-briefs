**Research Date: July 19, 2026**

## Executive Summary

Lockheed Martin presents an unusual disconnect between a superb top-line setup and deteriorating earnings quality. The company sits on a record $193.6B backlog (roughly 2.6x revenue), a proposed ~$1.5T U.S. FY2027 defense budget that directly favors its Missiles and Fire Control and Aeronautics franchises, and reported $43B+ in new missile awards including sole-source Sentinel A4 and THAAD. Against that, net income has fallen for three straight years — $6.9B (2023), $5.3B (2024), $5.0B (2025) — while revenue rose from $67.6B to $75.0B. The mechanism is recurring reach-forward losses on fixed-price development programs, and in Q1 2026 those losses migrated into production programs (F-16, C-130), free cash flow turned negative at -$291M, and management paused buybacks.

The investment debate reduces to a single question: does the munitions and budget supercycle overwhelm the margin and execution drag, and does the classified Aeronautics program stop bleeding? On the evidence, we cannot answer yes with confidence. Management explicitly warns further material losses on the classified program (cumulative ~$1.8B) are possible, the pattern is broadening rather than resolving, and a securities class action filed in July 2025 (amended January 2026) alleges the company misrepresented these very programs. The stock trades at ~24x trailing EPS on a declining earnings base, a premium to Northrop (17.3x) and General Dynamics (22.6x), the two most comparable large primes.

We maintain an AVOID. The defense demand backdrop is among the strongest in decades; this is a company-specific execution and per-share-value call. Lockheed is growing revenue while destroying operating leverage, its buyback-dependent EPS model faces a policy headwind from a potential executive order restricting contractor capital returns, and the valuation offers no discount to compensate for the live tail risk. We would revisit on evidence that the classified program losses have been fully realized and margins have inflected, not before.

## Company Overview

Lockheed Martin is the world's largest pure-play defense contractor, with FY2025 sales of $75.0B across four segments:

- **Aeronautics ($30.3B, 40% of sales):** Anchored by the F-35 Lightning II, the single largest program at 27% of consolidated revenue (67% of Aeronautics). Also C-130, F-16, F-22, and classified Skunk Works work. 1,293 production F-35s delivered through 2025; 368-aircraft backlog against a U.S. objective of 2,456.
- **Missiles and Fire Control ($14.5B):** The current growth engine — PAC-3, THAAD, JASSM, LRASM, GMLRS, PrSM, Javelin (JV with RTX), hypersonics. Backlog surged to $46.7B from $38.8B.
- **Rotary and Mission Systems ($17.3B):** Sikorsky helicopters (Black Hawk, Seahawk, CH-53K), Aegis, radar, combat systems. Restructured January 2026.
- **Space ($13.0B):** Trident II FBM, Next Gen OPIR, Next Generation Interceptor, Orion, GPS III; ULA equity stake.

Customer mix is 72% U.S. Government (63% DoD), 28% international. The competitive moat is entrenched incumbency on multi-decade franchise programs (F-35, Trident, Aegis), classified relationships, scale, and switching costs. Primary competitors are Boeing, General Dynamics, Northrop Grumman, RTX, and L3Harris, with rising pressure from non-traditional entrants (Anduril, Palantir, SpaceX).

## Financial Analysis

The central financial story is margin compression, not growth. Revenue grew steadily from $66.0B (2022) to $75.0B (2025), but consolidated operating margin fell to ~10.3% (2025) from 12.6% (2023), and diluted EPS declined from $27.55 to $22.31 to $21.49. The damage is concentrated in reach-forward losses on fixed-price development contracts:

- **2024:** $1.4B on a classified MFC program; $555M on a classified Aeronautics program.
- **2025:** an additional $950M on the same classified Aeronautics program (cumulative ~$1.8B); $570M on the Canadian Maritime Helicopter Program; $95M on Türkish Utility Helicopter; ~$140M unfavorable on C-130.

Segment margins were hit hard: Aeronautics fell to 6.9% (from 10.3% in 2023); RMS to 7.6% (from 11.5%).

FY2025 cash generation held up — operating cash flow $8.6B, FCF $6.9B (9.2% margin), aided by One Big Beautiful Bill Act tax benefits (bonus depreciation, R&D expensing). Capital returns were $3.0B in buybacks plus ~$3.2B in dividends, with the dividend raised to $3.45/quarter in 2026. Reach-forward losses are non-cash when booked but drain cash as the loss-making work is performed in subsequent years, so the reported FCF flatters the forward cash trajectory.

On the balance sheet, the headline Altman Z-Score of 1.53 and D/E of 7.9 read as distress but are artifacts of decades of buybacks: equity is only $6.7B against $59.8B assets because retained earnings have been returned, which also inflates ROE to 74.6%. Total debt is ~$21.7B (all fixed-rate public notes, covenant-compliant) against $8.6B annual OCF. This is a thin-equity structure rather than a solvency problem, but it leaves little cushion for pension and goodwill swings ($11.3B goodwill = 19% of assets), and the December 2025 $479M non-cash pension settlement charge from a $943M annuity transfer illustrates the episodic-charge risk.

Q1 2026 confirmed the deterioration is continuing and broadening. Sales were roughly flat at $18.0B; net income fell to $1.49B ($6.44 EPS) from $1.71B ($7.28), a 5.2% miss versus the $6.79 estimate. New unfavorable adjustments of $125M on F-16 (production/development delays) and $85M on C-130 show losses migrating from development into production programs. Free cash flow was negative $291M (versus +$955M a year earlier) on working-capital timing (contract assets +$2.9B, largely F-35) and higher software capex, and there were no buybacks (versus $750M prior year); cash was preserved, $1.0B debt repaid, cash down to $1.9B. Segment margins slipped across the board: Space 8.2% (from 11.8%), RMS 10.6%, Aeronautics 8.9%.

## Growth Analysis

The demand setup is genuinely strong, and this is the core of the bull case. Backlog rose to $193.6B at YE2025 from $176.0B, with the MFC franchise backlog jumping to $46.7B. The administration's April 2026 request seeks a ~$1.5T defense budget emphasizing munitions, Golden Dome missile defense, shipbuilding, and additional F-35 procurement — a direct tailwind to MFC and Aeronautics. Reported new awards of $43B+ including sole-source Sentinel A4 and THAAD (single-source news items, pending confirmation in subsequent filings) support the MFC ramp. NATO/European rearmament, Middle East and Pacific tensions, and depleted munitions stockpiles reinforce a multi-year munitions supercycle in which Lockheed's PAC-3/THAAD/JASSM/GMLRS portfolio is well positioned.

The problem is conversion. Consensus EPS estimates are essentially flat: current year $29.90 (revised -0.1% over 90 days), next year $32.13 (+0.3%). The estimate revisions are stable-to-flat, which means the market is not pricing an acceleration and the backlog is not translating into rising per-share earnings expectations. Revenue growth in the low single digits (Q1 sales flat YoY) with margins declining is the opposite of the operating-leverage story a top-line supercycle should produce. Growth prospects on the demand side are real, but the earnings that reach shareholders are not growing, which is what matters for the equity.

## Valuation Assessment

At ~$509, Lockheed trades at ~24x trailing EPS of $21.49 and ~17x current-year consensus of $29.90 — though the forward multiple relies on estimates that themselves embed a recovery from the loss-driven 2025 base. Against peers, the median A&D P/E is 39.3, but the relevant comparables are the large primes: Northrop at 17.3x and General Dynamics at 22.6x. Lockheed's trailing multiple sits above both despite the worst three-year earnings trajectory of the group. The 2.7% dividend yield exceeds NOC (1.8%) and GD (1.9%), providing some support, but dividend coverage is a concern given negative Q1 FCF and the potential executive-order restriction on contractor capital returns.

The cycle-adjustment argument does not apply here. This is not a trough-earnings inflection where a high trailing multiple masks tripling earnings: estimate revisions are flat, revenue growth is low single digits, and the earnings decline is driven by structural fixed-price loss recognition rather than a cyclical demand trough. A cheap-looking forward multiple on a business with declining margins and a live, unquantified loss tail is a value-trap warning. Analyst targets (median $600, high $756) sit well above the current price, but on a stock with falling EPS and repeated downside surprises, those targets reflect budget-catalyst optimism that has not yet shown up in the numbers; we treat them as aspirational rather than as a floor.

The valuation offers no asymmetry. Downside is real (further classified-program losses, margin drag, buyback restriction), upside requires a margin inflection that is not yet visible, and the entry multiple is a premium to the cleaner primes. We score valuation 4.0.

## Competitive Landscape

Lockheed's franchise position remains strong in absolute terms — sole-source or dominant on F-35, Trident, Aegis, THAAD, and PAC-3 — but the trajectory is what matters, and it is under pressure on two fronts. First, the DoD is structurally shifting toward fixed-price development contracts and rapid-acquisition OTAs that transfer risk to contractors; this is the exact mechanism producing Lockheed's reach-forward losses, and it disproportionately penalizes a company taking on large, complex development bets. Second, non-traditional entrants in software/AI (Palantir, Anduril) and new-space (SpaceX) are contesting adjacencies where legacy primes have been slow.

The clearest evidence of relative competitive weakness is Lockheed's own proxy: cumulative TSR of 156.4 versus a peer group at 230.5. The market is penalizing Lockheed specifically, not the sector, for execution and margin issues while European rearmament and the budget request should have been a tailwind. Management's stated strategy — CEO Taiclet's decision to avoid "must-win competitions" and accept short-term penalties on the classified aircraft program to reposition the portfolio — is a candid acknowledgment that the fixed-price development bets went badly. That is prudent forward strategy, but it also confirms the moat has been dented by self-inflicted execution rather than sector dynamics. We score competitive position 5.0 — a strong incumbent whose trajectory is flat-to-eroding relative to peers.

## Risk Assessment

**Fixed-price development losses (dominant, company-specific).** The classified Aeronautics program is the marquee risk: ~$1.8B cumulative losses, with management explicitly warning of possible further material losses tied to software degradation, safety-critical redesigns, and schedule realignment. The classified MFC program carries ~$1.46B cumulative losses with unexercised loss-making options management still deems "probable" of exercise. CMHP (~$670M) and TUHP (~$130M, plus up to $115M more) round out the pattern. Recoverability of growing pre-contract inventory costs hinges on an uncertain customer funding decision. This is an unquantified tail, and Q1 2026 showed it broadening into production programs.

**Securities litigation (new).** A putative securities class action (S.D.N.Y., filed 7/28/2025, amended 1/12/2026) alleges §10(b)/§20(a) violations regarding the classified programs and F-35 during 1/23/2024–7/21/2025; a related derivative suit (D. Md., 9/11/2025) names directors and management. Even if immaterial financially, this is a credibility overhang for a company whose value rests on execution reputation.

**Policy and capital-return risk.** 72% U.S. Government dependence exposes the company to continuing resolutions and shutdowns. A potential executive order could restrict "excessive" dividends, buybacks, and executive comp for underperforming contractors via a new DFARS clause, uniquely threatening to Lockheed's thin-equity, buyback-dependent EPS model. The DoD's push toward less favorable progress payments adds cash-flow timing risk.

**F-35 concentration (27% of revenue), supply chain / rare earths / tariffs (~$485M cash impact in 2025), and pension volatility** round out the risk set, though the F-35 Lots 18/19 definitization (296 aircraft, Sept 2025) is a positive.

### Options Market Signal

The options market is pricing near-term stress. ATM IV has jumped to 50.5% from 27.3% a month earlier, versus 27.6% one-year historical vol — rich by 22.9 points and in the 82nd percentile of the current chain. The term structure is in backwardation (near-term 50.5% vs. long-term 28.2%), and put/call OI has risen to 1.10 from 0.82 with a 7.8% put skew (elevated put demand). The implied move for the nearest expiry is ±5.5%. This positioning aligns with an earnings event and reflects the market pricing an imminent binary, consistent with the fixed-price-loss tail risk. Short interest is low at 1.8% of float (2.6 days to cover), so this is genuine hedging/directional put demand rather than short-covering pressure, and there is no squeeze cushion for the bull case.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull Case (~$650–740):** The FY2027 budget and backlog drive an MFC-led revenue reacceleration; the classified Aeronautics losses are fully realized and margins inflect back toward historical norms; F-35 sustainment and international demand hold; and multiple expansion toward the peer median follows a return to EPS growth. Sentinel A4 and THAAD awards validate the munitions supercycle. In this scenario the market re-rates Lockheed on demonstrated margin recovery, and the dividend yield plus buyback resumption compound returns.

**Bear Case (~$450):** The classified program books further material losses, the securities litigation forces disclosure that erodes credibility, negative FCF persists as loss-making work is performed, the executive order constrains buybacks and caps the EPS-support mechanism, and margins remain in the 8–10% range. On flat-to-declining EPS the multiple compresses toward Northrop's ~17x, implying meaningful downside from current levels.

The asymmetry is unfavorable at ~$509. The bull case requires a margin inflection that estimate revisions do not yet reflect, while the bear case rests on a pattern already broadening across programs and cash flow already negative in Q1.

## Short Candidate Assessment

**Catalyst:** The most likely negative catalyst is a further reach-forward loss on the classified Aeronautics program or continued production-program deterioration disclosed at the next quarterly print. Management has flagged this explicitly, but the timing of any "discrete event" is not fixed to a date, and the FY2027 budget catalyst runs the other way. There is no single dated event with a clear negative skew.

**Timing window:** The next earnings release is the natural resolution point, but the outcome is genuinely two-sided — a strong award cycle or a clean quarter could reverse sentiment. A short here is a bet against a company with a record backlog into the strongest defense budget in decades.

**Short interest and squeeze risk:** Short interest is only 1.8% of float, 2.6 days to cover — uncrowded, so more room to work but also a signal that informed investors are not positioned short. Low SI with high institutional ownership means limited squeeze fuel, but it also means the bearish thesis is not consensus.

**Borrow and options signal:** Near-term IV at 50.5% (82nd percentile) and a 7.8% put skew confirm hedging demand, but rich IV makes put-buying expensive and the backwardation means carry works against a short-dated bearish position.

**Downside target and risk/reward:** Bear case ~$450 (~12% downside) against a plausible squeeze/beat scenario toward $600+ (~18% upside) on any award or margin surprise. That is worse than 1:1 reward:risk against the short. The demand tailwind, record backlog, and cheap-looking forward multiple all create meaningful upside risk.

**Verdict: NOT SHORTABLE.** This is a mediocre-execution business in an exceptionally favorable demand environment, not an actively collapsing one. The margin and execution problems justify avoiding the stock, but the record backlog, budget catalyst, low short interest, and unfavorable risk/reward make it inappropriate as an active short.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Recommendation: AVOID.**

**Re-entry criteria:**
- Evidence in two consecutive quarters that classified-program reach-forward losses have been fully realized with no new discrete events, and consolidated operating margin recovering toward 11%+.
- Positive EPS estimate revision velocity (>5% over 90 days) confirming the backlog is converting into per-share earnings growth, not just revenue.
- Resumption of buybacks alongside positive FCF, with clarity that the contractor capital-return executive order does not materially constrain Lockheed.
- A valuation reset that discounts the execution risk — a forward multiple below the ~17x Northrop comparable would create a margin of safety the current price lacks.

**Timeline:** The fixed-price loss overhang and margin drag are likely to persist through at least the next 2–4 quarters as loss-making development work is performed and the securities litigation proceeds. The FY2027 budget could provide an offsetting demand catalyst on a 12–18 month horizon, but we want to see execution before the demand story earns a re-rating.

**What would change our mind fastest:** A clean quarter with no new program losses, positive FCF, and rising estimates would move this toward a HOLD; a demonstrated margin inflection with buyback resumption would move it toward a BUY.

## Conclusion

Lockheed Martin's score trajectory (5.7 in late May to 4.8 by mid-June, with competitive position, management quality, and risk factors all stepping down) reflects a genuine deterioration in execution, not a price artifact. The new data confirms rather than reverses that trend: Q1 2026 losses broadened into production programs, FCF turned negative, and the buyback was paused. The stock has declined ~24% from its high, but that decline is warranted by fundamental deterioration — declining net income for a third consecutive year, margin compression, and a live unquantified loss tail — rather than an unjustified sell-off in an intact franchise.

The demand backdrop is exceptional and the backlog is a real asset, which is why this is an AVOID rather than a short. A top-line supercycle that a company cannot convert into per-share earnings does not create equity value, and at ~24x trailing earnings on a falling base, above the cleaner large primes, the valuation offers no compensation for the execution risk. We would need to see the classified program stop bleeding and margins inflect before the demand story earns a re-rating. Until then, the risk/reward is unfavorable.
