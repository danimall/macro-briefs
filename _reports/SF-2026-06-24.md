**Research Date: June 24, 2026**

## Executive Summary

Stifel Financial is a 135-year-old, St. Louis-based hybrid wealth manager and middle-market investment bank with an embedded bank holding company. FY2025 was a record-revenue year: net revenue of $5.53B (+11.3%), driven by a 25.7% surge in investment banking and a 10.7% rise in asset management, yet net income to common fell 6.9% to $646.5M because of a $180M pre-tax ($1.16/share after-tax) legal provision tied to the Q1 2025 FINRA arbitration matter. Normalizing for that charge, the franchise grew underlying EPS to roughly $7.03 (pre-split). The headline decline masks a healthy operating trajectory, and separating the two is the central analytical task here.

The stock trades around $73.56, up about 11% over the trailing twelve months but down roughly 45% from its $134.74 52-week high (the high reflects pre-split pricing; the split was 3-for-2 effective 2/27/2026, so the comparison overstates the actual drawdown). On a post-split forward basis, SF trades at roughly 11.7x the $6.31 current-year consensus and ~10.5x next year, a meaningful discount to the 21.8x median of its Investment Banking & Brokerage peers. The discount is not unwarranted: it reflects a recurring structured-notes supervision problem (the Chuck Roberts litigation cluster, with three adverse awards, punitive damages, and 19 unresolved claims from the same advisor's clients), a cash-sweep class-action overhang that targets a core profit lever, and net interest income facing rate-cut headwinds.

I rate SF a HOLD with a growth-hold rationale. The franchise is genuinely improving, estimate revisions have turned modestly positive over 30 days after a soft 90-day patch, and the April 2026 settlement of the lead Roberts arbitration removed the single largest litigation overhang. But the residual legal tail, the structural threat to sweep economics, and a falling-rate environment that drags both NII and sweep fees keep the setup balanced rather than asymmetric. The trajectory has improved across three reports (5.7 → 5.7 → 7.0), and this read tempers that somewhat as the post-split price comparison and residual risk come into sharper focus.

## Company Overview

Stifel operates through three segments:

- **Global Wealth Management (~64% of net revenue):** The Private Client Group — 2,200+ advisors across 402 branches in 48 states — plus Stifel Bancorp, holding a ~$22B loan book and ~$30B in deposits funded largely by sweeping client brokerage cash. This is the earnings engine.
- **Institutional Group (~35% of net revenue):** Equity and fixed income sales/trading/research, investment banking (M&A advisory, ECM, DCM), public finance, and the KBW franchise, a leading financial-institutions investment bank.
- **Other:** Corporate overhead and unallocated items, structurally running a ~$500–560M pre-tax loss.

The model combines a wealth manager, a middle-market investment bank, and a bank. Competitive advantages come from scale in the mid-tier (built through opportunistic acquisitions of Thomas Weisel, KBW, Sterne Agee, and recently Bryan Garnier), a Protocol-driven advisor recruiting machine, and a sticky low-cost deposit base derived from the firm's own brokerage clients. Client assets reached a record $551.9B (+10.1% YoY); fee-based assets hit $224.5B (+16.5%).

## Financial Analysis

The top line is the cleanest story. Net revenue of $5.53B in 2025 followed $4.97B (2024) and $4.35B (2023), a two-year compound growth rate above 12%. Investment banking jumped 25.7% to $1.25B (advisory +25.0% to $722M; capital raising +26.7%), lifting Institutional pre-tax income to $329M from $223M. Asset management rose 10.7% to a record $1.70B (now ~31% of revenue) on market appreciation and net new assets.

Net interest income grew 4.9% to $1.09B, with NIM flat at 3.07%, already down from 3.44% in 2023, signaling the rate tailwind has plateaued. Management is candid that further Fed cuts (now 3.50–3.75%) pressure both deposit-sweep fees and margin-loan yields.

Margins diverged across segments. GWM pre-tax margin compressed to 31.2% (from 36.8%) on the legal provision and higher credit provisions. Institutional margin expanded to 17.2% (from 14.0%). The compensation ratio held near 59.2% of net revenue, normal for the model.

Cash generation is solid: operating cash flow of $1.12B against ~$62M of capex yields ~$1.05B of free cash flow, roughly a 9-10% FCF yield on the current market cap. Total assets are $41.3B, funded predominantly by $29.8B of deposits, with only $617M of senior notes. Credit metrics are mixed: allowance/loans 0.73%, nonaccruals improving to 0.57% from 0.77%, but C&I net charge-offs elevated at 1.22%. The Altman Z-Score of 0.33 and D/E of 5.9 from generic screens are not meaningful for a bank holding company; the relevant test is regulatory capital, and the bank/broker subsidiaries are well-capitalized and above net-capital minimums. The balance sheet holds $12.9B readily convertible to cash.

Capital return was disciplined: $244.6M of buybacks (2.5M shares at ~$98 average, pre-split — above the current pre-split-equivalent level) and $249M of dividends in 2025, with the dividend raised 11%. 7.6M shares remain under authorization. The earnings track record supports forward confidence: three beats and one in-line over the last four quarters, all within 0.3–2.9%.

## Growth Analysis

Three durable growth vectors are in play. First, the investment banking cycle recovery lifted advisory and capital-raising revenue more than 25% in 2025, with management describing a constructive 2026 pipeline. KBW gives Stifel a differentiated position in financial-institutions M&A, a category benefiting from depository consolidation. Second, asset management compounds on rising client assets and a structural shift toward fee-based accounts, which de-risks revenue from transactional volatility. Third, the recruiting-and-acquire engine continues: Bryan Garnier (European tech/healthcare IB) and a portion of B. Riley's wealth book (36 advisors, ~$4B AUM) were integrated in 2025, both funded from operating cash.

Estimate revisions are the key forward tell, and they are modestly constructive. The current quarter was revised +1.9% over 30 days (after a -5.1% 90-day move), and current-year consensus rose +1.9% over 30 days to $6.31 (still -3.0% over 90 days). Next-year sits at $7.00, off -1.6% over 30 days. The pattern is a soft 90-day patch that has begun stabilizing and turning up at the 30-day mark: positive momentum, but not the >30% revision velocity that would flag a powerful acceleration. The 90-day softness reflects the rate-cut drag on NII; the 30-day improvement reflects the IB pipeline and removal of the lead litigation overhang.

The honest counterweight: net interest income, a meaningful earnings lever, faces a headwind rather than a tailwind. With NIM already at 3.07% and the Fed cutting, sweep fees and margin-loan yields compress unless clients aggressively redeploy cash. Growth from here leans on IB cyclicality and asset gathering rather than the deposit-spread engine that flattered 2022–2023.

## Valuation Assessment

At ~$73.56 on a $6.31 forward EPS, SF trades at roughly 11.7x current-year and ~10.5x the $7.00 next-year estimate. Against the peer set, the discount is stark: EVR at 20.0x, HLI at 21.8x, PIPR at 19.7x, SNEX at 23.0x, BGC at 30.4x, a 21.8x median. SF sits at roughly half the median multiple.

Part of this gap is structural. SF's bank-heavy model carries a different risk profile and lower growth ceiling than pure-play advisory boutiques (EVR, HLI), and its wealth-management base is steadier but lower-multiple than the IB-leveraged comps. Part is the litigation overhang and cash-sweep regulatory risk that the boutiques do not carry. A wealth-plus-bank hybrid should trade at a discount to advisory pure-plays, but a ~50% discount is wide even after adjusting for these factors.

I would caution against treating the discount as a pure buy signal. The valuation dimension has been a reliable value-trap warning in this regime: cheap multiples have sorted the wrong way absent a specific catalyst, and Financials BUYs carry a structural alpha deficit versus the market. SF's discount needs an independent catalyst to close: either a clean resolution of the remaining Roberts claims, a favorable cash-sweep outcome, or a re-acceleration of IB revenue that forces estimates higher. The settled lead arbitration is a step in that direction but does not fully clear the path.

The growth-adjusted math is supportive but not compelling. On ~7-8% normalized EPS growth and a ~10-11x multiple, the PEG is favorable, and the ~1.8% dividend yield plus buyback adds a return floor. Analyst targets — low $79, median $87.5, mean $87.75, high $103 — imply 7-40% upside, and on a cheap-relative-to-growth name these targets are more floor than ceiling. But the residual risk keeps me from treating the discount as asymmetric value rather than a discount the market has assigned for identifiable reasons.

## Competitive Landscape

Stifel occupies the mid-tier full-service niche: larger and broader than regional brokers, more relationship-driven than discount/fintech platforms, smaller than bulge-bracket banks. Its strategy exploits consolidation among mid-tier firms to recruit advisors and acquire books, a self-reinforcing model when the firm's currency (stock, recruiting packages) is strong. KBW gives it a top-tier, differentiated position in financial-institutions investment banking, and depository M&A advisory is a noted growth lane.

The competitive trajectory is stable-to-improving. Record client assets and the growth in fee-based assets indicate share gains in wealth, and the IB segment's margin expansion to 17.2% reflects operating leverage on the cycle recovery. The threats are secular and shared across the group: fixed-income fee compression from price transparency, the passive shift pressuring asset-management fees, fintech entrants, and expensive competition for advisor talent ($228.5M of transition notes issued in 2025, $733.8M of unrecognized deferred comp). These are headwinds the whole industry faces; SF is managing them about as well as its peers, which supports a competitive position score modestly above neutral rather than a clear standout.

## Risk Assessment

**The Chuck Roberts / structured-notes litigation cluster is the dominant idiosyncratic risk.** A 3-person FINRA panel awarded $132.5M in March 2025, including $79.5M of punitive damages, to four family members over structured-notes losses through a now-former Stifel advisor. This was the third adverse award tied to the same advisor, and the 10-K discloses 19 additional unresolved claims from other clients of that advisor. Post-10-K, a magistrate recommended denying Stifel's vacatur petition (Feb 2026), Judge Gayles confirmed the award (March 2026, pushing it to ~$143.5M with interest), and Stifel reportedly settled the lead case in April 2026. Aggregate Roberts-related exposure has been reported approaching ~$200M. The recurrence of punitive damages on the same fact pattern points to a possible systemic supervisory lapse around structured-notes suitability, a reputational and tail-risk issue that extends beyond the dollar amounts. The April settlement caps the largest single case but not the remaining claims.

**Cash-sweep litigation is a potential systemic revenue risk.** Multiple putative class actions (filed March 2025, E.D. Missouri) allege Stifel failed to pay reasonable interest on swept cash, seeking treble damages. This is an industry-wide issue, but it targets a core profit driver: the firm earns fees on FDIC-swept deposits and the spread on Smart Rate balances ($14.7B), and the bank's 2.58% average deposit cost is the foundation of its NII. A forced repricing toward higher client rates would compress the spread that funds the wealth-management margin. The 10-K explicitly flags this regulatory risk.

**Other risks:** ERISA 401(k) litigation (smaller, partly mitigated by restorative payments and a DOL no-action letter); interest-rate sensitivity, now a drag rather than a tailwind; and key-man/succession risk (CEO Kruszewski, 67, in the role since 1997; President Zemlyak, 66). The 10-K's aggregate disclosed reasonably-possible loss in excess of reserves is ~$100M.

On governance, the compensation optics warrant a flag. CEO total comp rose to $18.9M in 2025 with "comp actually paid" of $42.8M in a year net income to common fell 6.9%. The "actually paid" figure largely reflects equity appreciation (5-year TSR of 271 vs. peer 181), so it tracks stock performance, and the performance metrics are heavily non-GAAP (pre-tax income, net revenue, EPS, ROE) that can exclude legal charges. This is genuinely tied to superior long-run TSR, but the non-GAAP framing that strips out the very legal charges driving the EPS decline is a point of caution. Insider activity is neutral: routine grants, RSU vesting, and tax-withholding sales, with no open-market buying or selling signaling a view.

### Options Market Signal

The options market is sanguine. Put/call OI ratio sits at 0.21 (call-heavy), 1-month skew is -6.5% (favoring calls), and short interest is negligible at 0.0% of float (8,382 shares). The 1-month implied move is ±6.3%, with 3-month ATM IV at 31.3% against 25.9% trailing realized vol, modestly elevated but not signaling acute stress. The absence of put demand and the call-heavy skew suggest the market is not pricing a near-term litigation or earnings shock, which aligns with the view that the lead Roberts case has been settled. The light short interest means informed bearish positioning is minimal, neither a contrarian buy signal nor a warning.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull Case ($95–103):** The IB cycle recovery sustains, advisory and capital-raising compound through 2026, asset gathering pushes client assets and fee-based revenue higher, and the remaining Roberts claims and cash-sweep actions resolve without material incremental cost. Estimate revisions continue turning up, consensus migrates toward $7.00+ next year, and the peer-multiple discount narrows toward 15x as the litigation overhang clears. At 15x on $7.00, that is ~$105.

**Base Case ($87):** The franchise grows mid-single-digits to high-single-digits on EPS, with IB strength partly offset by NII drag from rate cuts. The litigation overhang persists as a chronic but manageable drag, capping the multiple in the 12-13x range. The dividend and buyback provide a return floor. This lands roughly at the analyst median.

**Bear Case ($58–62):** A cash-sweep class action forces deposit repricing, compressing the bank's spread and the GWM margin; additional Roberts-cluster claims produce further punitive awards; and the IB pipeline stalls as markets cool. Estimates fall back toward $6.00, and the multiple stays depressed at 10x, implying ~$60, roughly 18% below current.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Reassessment timeline:** Next earnings (Q2 2026, expected late July) and the subsequent 10-Q, which should clarify the cash-sweep litigation status and any reserve changes for the remaining Roberts claims.

**Upgrade triggers (to BUY):**
1. A clean resolution or favorable ruling on the cash-sweep class actions, removing the threat to deposit-spread economics, OR settlement of a meaningful portion of the 19 outstanding Roberts claims at contained cost.
2. Current-year consensus rising above $6.60 with 30-day revisions accelerating (above the +1.9% recent pace), confirming the IB recovery is re-accelerating against the NII drag.
3. The forward multiple staying below ~13x while these overhangs clear, the discount-plus-catalyst combination that would make the value asymmetric.

**Downgrade triggers (to AVOID):**
1. A new punitive damages award in the Roberts cluster, or aggregate litigation exposure clearly exceeding the ~$200M reported range.
2. An adverse cash-sweep ruling or settlement forcing deposit repricing that compresses NIM below ~2.7%.
3. Current-year EPS estimates falling below $6.00 on combined NII and IB-pipeline weakness.

## Conclusion

Stifel is a well-managed, record-revenue franchise with a sticky deposit-funded bank, a recovering investment-banking cycle, and a disciplined capital-return record, trading at roughly half the peer-median multiple. The discount is real but largely explained: a recurring structured-notes supervision problem with punitive damages and outstanding claims, a cash-sweep class action that targets the firm's core profit lever, and a rate-cut environment that has turned NII from tailwind to headwind. The April 2026 settlement of the lead Roberts arbitration and the modestly improving 30-day estimate revisions are genuine positives, and the trajectory across prior reports has been one of improvement.

I maintain a HOLD with a growth-hold rationale. The franchise is worth owning for the IB recovery, asset-gathering compounding, and the return floor from dividends and buybacks, and the analyst targets imply meaningful upside if the overhangs clear. But the cheap multiple alone is not a buy signal absent a catalyst that closes the discount, and the residual litigation tail plus the NII drag keep the risk/reward balanced rather than asymmetric. The appropriate posture is to hold for the growth thesis while monitoring the cash-sweep and Roberts resolutions, which are the swing factors between a re-rating to the peer cohort and a continued discount.
