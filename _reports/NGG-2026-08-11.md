## Executive Summary

National Grid is a regulated electricity and gas network monopoly operating in England and Wales (transmission), the Midlands/South West/South Wales (distribution) and New York and Massachusetts (~6.5 million US customers). FY2025/26, ended 31 March 2026, delivered underlying operating profit of £5,680m (+8.8% constant currency), underlying EPS of 78.0p (+8.3% cc), statutory EPS of 65.5p (+9%), capital investment of £11,576m (+21.3% cc) and a dividend of 48.49p (+3.8%, tracking UK CPIH). Management has committed to at least £70bn of capital investment over FY27–FY31, ~10% annual asset growth, 8–10% annual underlying EPS growth, and FY27 underlying EPS up 13–15%. Ofgem's RIIO-3 final determination of 4 December 2025 covers exactly the same five years, which is why the plan and the price control are effectively the same document.

A unit-mismatch in the market-data feed needs correcting before any valuation discussion. The reported trailing and forward P/E of 93.7x and EPS of $0.863 are per-ordinary-share figures priced against a per-ADS quote: 65.5p statutory EPS at ~1.32 GBP/USD is $0.865, which is exactly the feed's EPS. On the conventional 1 ADS = 5 ordinary shares basis, the ADS earns ~$4.32 statutory and ~$5.15 underlying, putting the shares at ~18.7x trailing statutory, ~15.7x trailing underlying, and ~13.6–13.9x the ~88–90p FY27 underlying EPS implied by guidance. That is a discount to US regulated peers guiding to faster EPS growth than most of them. [Peer forward multiples not available in the provided data — see Valuation Assessment.]

The reason the discount exists is the funding arithmetic. FY26 capex was 2.04x underlying operating profit; the plan requires £14.0bn average annual capex, another 20.9% above an FY26 base that itself grew 21.3%. With a 62% payout on underlying earnings (74% on statutory) and reported net debt of ~£44.2bn, the programme cannot be internally funded. Debt, disposals and — at the margin — equity are the levers, and the cost of the first is repricing continuously against allowed returns that are fixed for five years. Base case $88 (+8.8%) plus ~4% yield against a $66 bear case (-18%) is an adequate but unremarkable risk-adjusted return. Hold; the entry is not compelling enough to add.

## Company Overview

The business is a portfolio of administratively priced network franchises rather than a competitive enterprise. Revenue is set by Ofgem in the UK and by state public utility commissions in New York and Massachusetts. The economic engine is mechanical: deploy capital into the regulated asset value (UK) or rate base (US), earn the allowed return on that capital, recover depreciation and operating costs in tariffs. Growth in earnings comes from growth in the asset base, and the allowed return determines how much of that growth reaches shareholders.

Five reporting units: UK Electricity Transmission; UK Electricity Distribution; the US electric-and-gas business; National Grid Ventures, which the company explicitly describes as running separately from core regulated operations; and National Grid Partners, a corporate venture arm.

FY26 continued a multi-year narrowing toward regulated networks, with the completed disposals of National Grid Renewables and Grain LNG. Seven weeks after announcing that narrowing, on 1 July 2026, the company committed $1.75bn for a 35% stake in Joulent, a US platform developing power infrastructure for data centres. National Grid is the capital provider holding a minority interest; Joulent owns and develops the assets. At roughly 2% of the five-year capital programme it does not change the aggregate thesis, but the direction — capital deployed outside the regulatory perimeter, with no allowed return underwriting it — is worth tracking. Deal structure, funding source and accounting treatment are not verified in the available evidence, including whether the $1.75bn sits inside or outside the £70bn figure.

A disclosure caveat that materially affects confidence: as a UK foreign private issuer, National Grid files 20-F annual reports (most recent filed 3 June 2026) and 6-Ks, not 10-Ks, 10-Qs, proxies or Section 16 Form 4s. Reporting is half-yearly. Structured XBRL financials, standardized financial-health screens, insider-transaction filings and compensation disclosure are all unavailable through US channels. The FY26 headline figures below are issuer-sourced and internally consistent (each stated growth rate reconciles precisely to the underlying levels), but statutory revenue, operating cash flow, RAV/rate-base absolute values, gearing and the underlying-to-statutory reconciliation were not obtainable.

Two accounting notes. First, the FY25 comparatives are constant-currency restatements, so the stated growth rates are constant-currency rates; the reported-currency rates that actually accrue to a GBP or USD holder are not disclosed in the accessible material. A company normalizes for FX when FX is material, and with a large US business it plainly is. Second, the 12.5p wedge between underlying (78.0p) and statutory (65.5p) EPS means statutory earnings are 84% of underlying, with 16% excluded. For a utility with two completed disposals and a large derivative book, that is within normal range; the reassuring detail is that statutory EPS grew 9% against underlying 8.3%, so the wedge did not widen in growth terms. Without the 20-F reconciliation table I cannot confirm the composition, and this remains a named gap.

**Capital intensity is the defining characteristic.** £11,576m of capex against £5,680m of underlying operating profit is 2.04x. A business investing two pounds for every pound of operating profit, while distributing 62% of underlying and 74% of statutory earnings, does not self-fund. The mechanism runs: capex exceeds operating cash flow → the gap is filled with debt, disposal proceeds and retained earnings → net debt grows with the asset base → interest expense is set by market rates today while allowed returns were set in December 2025 → any widening between the two compresses achieved returns until the next reset in 2031.

The only leverage figure available is ~£44.2bn of net debt, from a tier-3 source (29 July 2026) that does correctly reproduce the £11.6bn capex and 48.49p/+3.8% dividend figures, which lends it some credibility without verifying it. Against £5,680m of underlying operating profit that is ~7.8x, a multiple that is meaningless for a regulated network without the two metrics that actually govern it: net debt/RAV (regulatory gearing) and FFO/net debt. Neither was obtainable, along with credit ratings, covenant terms, the maturity profile and the index-linked debt proportion. I am not asserting financing stress — nothing in the evidence indicates it — but I cannot measure headroom in either direction, and that uncertainty is priced into the financial-health score.

**Dividend.** 48.49p is 62.2% of underlying and 74.0% of statutory EPS. The 3.8% increase was in line with UK CPIH, consistent with the stated policy. Dividend growth at ~3.8% against guided EPS growth of 8–10% de-levers the payout ratio over time and retains progressively more cash — rational for a capital-hungry network, but income holders should not expect the dividend to track EPS.

On the ADS, 48.49p × 5 = 242.45p = £2.4245, or ~$3.20 at 1.32, a yield of ~3.96% on $80.88 (3.9–4.1% across a 1.30–1.35 GBP/USD band). The market-data feed shows 5.36%, which cannot be reconciled with the disclosed FY26 DPS at a 5:1 ADS ratio; it may reflect a trailing window capturing three payment dates. I use ~4.0% and flag the conflict.

**Earnings delivery.** The four reported half-year periods show two small beats (+5.7%, +3.0%) and two small misses (-1.9%, -0.8%), all within ±6%. For a regulated utility that pattern indicates tight, low-variance delivery rather than either conservatism or aggression. Note that the estimates in that table ($0.48–$0.49) are on the per-ordinary-share USD basis, confirming the unit mismatch discussed above.

## Growth Analysis

The five-year framework is the investment case, so it is worth decomposing.

**£70bn ÷ 5 = £14.0bn average annual capex**, or 20.9% above FY26 actual. FY26 already grew 21.3%. The plan therefore requires another fifth on top of a year that grew a fifth, sustained for five consecutive years. Management states supply chains are "secured for most of the plan" — a management claim, with "most" doing real work. Transformer, HVDC converter, cable and skilled-labour bottlenecks are the standard constraint in programmes of this size. Under-delivery slows asset growth and therefore EPS; over-spend risks disallowance.

**Asset base compounding at 10% for five years** gives 1.10^5 = 1.611, a combined RAV plus rate base roughly 61% larger by FY31. That is the engine.

**EPS brackets.** From 78.0p: 8% CAGR → 114.6p by FY31; 10% CAGR → 125.6p. FY27 at +13–15% → 88.1p to 89.7p.

**The framework is front-loaded, which the headline range conceals.** If FY27 grows 14% at the midpoint, the FY28–FY31 CAGR required to land on the framework endpoints is (1.10^5 ÷ 1.14)^0.25 − 1 = 9.0% at the top and (1.08^5 ÷ 1.14)^0.25 − 1 = 6.5% at the bottom. The most coherent explanation is that RIIO-3 allowances and US rate-case revenues switch on at the start of FY27 as a one-time step, after which growth reverts to asset-base compounding. An alternative reading is that a depressed FY26 comparator in one or more segments flatters the FY27 figure; the arithmetic holds either way. An investor anchoring on 13–15% as the run rate will be disappointed from FY28.

**Demand backdrop.** After two decades of flat-to-declining developed-market electricity demand, load growth has returned. The Joulent investment is explicitly framed around AI-driven electricity demand; the 2026 AGM saw shareholder scrutiny specifically on data centres. For a network operator load growth is unusually favourable, because it spreads fixed costs over more units (easing bill-affordability pressure) while creating reinforcement capex that grows the asset base. The load-growth theme is now appearing in a second geography beyond the US market.

The growth is real, contracted at the regulatory level, and above utility norms. It is also granted rather than earned, front-loaded, and dependent on deploying £14bn a year without cost overruns. Score 6.5.

## Competitive Landscape

National Grid does not compete for customers. It competes with SSE/SSEN, Scottish Power Transmission, UK Power Networks, Northern Powergrid, Con Edison, Avangrid, Eversource and others for capital, supply-chain slots and skilled labour, and it is benchmarked against them by regulators in cost-efficiency assessments. That benchmarking channel is how peer performance reaches National Grid's allowed revenues.

RIIO-3 is sector-wide, not company-specific: SSEN Transmission received the same framework and publicly endorsed it. National Grid is therefore not differentially advantaged by the settlement — the competitive question is relative delivery against a common allowance, not relative allowance. Ofgem raised baseline electricity-transmission totex from £8.8bn at the July 2025 draft to £10.3bn at the December 2025 final, a 17.0% increase, while still landing below what companies requested. Two caveats: the identical £10.3bn/£8.8bn figures appear in third-party notes on both National Grid and SSE, so the entity scope is ambiguous, and totex is not the return. A company can receive generous volume allowances and still earn a poor equity return if the allowed cost of equity is set low. That figure was not obtainable and is the most consequential missing input in this analysis.

Data-centre load creates a distinctive regulatory contest that a generic utility screen would miss: who pays for reinforcement driven by hyperscale load — the incremental large customer or the general ratepayer base — and who bears the stranded-asset risk if a campus is cancelled after the network is built. Both New York and Massachusetts have this question open, and the accessible evidence contains no specific approved ROE, revenue increase or rate-year schedule for either state despite management asserting improved US regulatory clarity. Roughly half the asset base is unverified on this dimension.

Trajectory is stable with a favourable demand shift and no evidence of franchise erosion; the offset is that the return is administratively set and the Joulent stake moves capital marginally outside the protective perimeter. Score 6.0.

## Risk Assessment

**Allowed-return risk (highest severity, unmeasured).** The entire 8–10% EPS framework rests on the return earned on rapidly growing RAV. The allowed cost of equity under RIIO-3 was not obtainable. This is determined and therefore in principle known to the market, so it should be priced; the risk to this analysis is that I cannot independently confirm the plan's arithmetic.

**Funding and dilution.** Investing 2.04x operating profit, paying out 62–74% of earnings, and carrying ~£44bn of net debt leaves debt, disposals and equity. FY26 used disposals. Debt capacity is bounded by the ratings a regulated utility must defend to keep its cost of capital inside allowances. That makes equity the residual lever. There is no announced or planned raise in the evidence, and I am not forecasting one — but the combination makes future issuance a live scenario rather than a remote one. A large 2024 equity raise is widely referenced in market commentary but is not verified in the available evidence, and future dilution should be treated as a scenario, not a base case.

**Rate sensitivity.** With ~£14bn a year of gross investment to finance, National Grid issues continuously into whatever market exists. Allowed returns reset once every five years; the cost of new debt marks daily. Higher-for-longer real rates compress the spread between sticky allowed returns and marked funding costs for exactly this business model.

**Execution.** A 20.9% average capex step-up on top of a 21.3% year. There is a truncated third-party reference to UK grid cost overruns that I could not resolve and am not using, but systematic overruns would feed directly into disallowance risk.

**Currency.** For a USD holder, the ADS embeds GBP/USD directly. A move from 1.32 to 1.28 is a ~3% headwind to the ADS at constant GBP earnings, independent of operations.

**Governance and CEO transition.** The AGM coverage attributes the plan's reiteration to "Yujnovich," implying a new chief executive as of mid-2026. A leadership change at the moment a company commits to the largest capital programme in its history introduces strategy-continuity risk in both directions. Effective date, mandate and compensation structure are unverified; no proxy exists, and no insider-transaction data is available because Section 16 does not apply. The absence of an insider signal here is absence of data, not evidence.

**Options Market Signal.** Near-term ATM implied volatility of 22.7% against 21.9% one-year realized is fair (0.8pp spread), at the 15th percentile within the current chain. Put/call skew is -1.9%, described as balanced; put/call volume 0.71 and open interest 0.82. Term structure is in contango (22.7% near versus 26.0% at six months), the normal, complacent shape. Short interest is 0.1% of float with 0.7 days to cover. The options and short-interest markets are pricing essentially no idiosyncratic event risk over the next month, which is consistent with a regulated utility between half-yearly reports and with the FY27 guidance already public since May. Two implications: near-term option premium is not rich enough to make protection expensive if you want it, and there is no positioning cushion — a genuine surprise at the November half-year would land into complacent structure. The six-month tenor at 26.0% with a -3.2% skew is where the modest hedging demand sits, roughly coinciding with the H1 FY27 report.

Score 5.5: no near-term binary events, but funding/dilution and the unverified allowed return are material and only partly reflected in a multiple that already sits below peers.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull case ($99, +22%).** RIIO-3 allowed returns plus New York and Massachusetts rate-case outcomes deliver the FY27 step-up in full, and FY27 underlying EPS prints at or above 89p. Asset growth compounds at 10%, taking RAV plus rate base ~61% higher by FY31. Data-centre load allows cost recovery without an affordability backlash, because rising volumes spread fixed costs. The programme is funded within existing ratings headroom using debt, retained earnings and disposal proceeds, with no equity issuance. The multiple closes part of its gap to peers, re-rating from ~13.7x to 16.5x forward on rising earnings, and GBP firms toward 1.35. The dividend compounds at CPIH with a falling payout ratio.

**Bear case ($66, -18%).** The allowed cost of equity proves thin relative to the marked cost of new debt, so asset growth converts into materially less earnings growth. Supply-chain and labour constraints cause the £14bn annual run-rate to slip, or costs to overrun and be partially disallowed. New York or Massachusetts resolves the data-centre cost-allocation question against the company, or affordability pressure trims allowed returns. Funding requires equity, and the per-share growth guided at 8–10% arrives at 5–6% after dilution. GBP weakens toward 1.28. The multiple compresses to 12x on 84p and the shares retest the $67.52 low.

**What tips the balance to HOLD.** The base case at 15x guided FY27 earnings gives $88, or ~13% total return with the dividend. That is a reasonable return, and the yield plus regulator-underwritten cash flows provide a real floor that the bear case only breaches on a combination of adverse outcomes. It is not enough asymmetry to justify adding capital ahead of the November half-year, given that the funding gap must be closed continuously and that the sector's history of converting load growth into share issuance rather than per-share earnings is a live risk. Existing holders are adequately compensated to wait.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Reassessment timeline:** H1 FY27 results, expected November 2026 — the first period reported under the new price control and the first read on whether the 13–15% FY27 EPS guidance and the £14bn capex run-rate are being delivered. Secondary checkpoints: any 6-K disclosing US rate-case orders, financing actions, or an update to the Joulent transaction.

**Upgrade triggers (to ACCUMULATE or BUY):**
1. H1 FY27 underlying EPS tracking to the upper half of the 13–15% FY27 range **with** confirmation that the capital programme is funded without new equity — specifically, regulatory gearing and FFO/net debt disclosed within rating thresholds and no announced issuance.
2. Disclosure of the RIIO-3 allowed cost of equity at a level that, applied to ~10% asset growth, independently reproduces 8–10% EPS growth without leverage escalation.
3. Price toward $70–72 on unchanged guidance, which would put the ADS near 12x forward underlying earnings with a ~4.6% yield and restore genuine asymmetry.

**Downgrade triggers (to REDUCE or AVOID):**
1. Any equity raise, or a scrip take-up structure that materially expands the share count, that reduces implied FY27–FY31 per-share EPS growth below ~6%.
2. FY27 underlying EPS guidance cut, or capital investment guided below ~£13bn for FY27, indicating the £70bn plan is slipping.
3. An adverse New York or Massachusetts determination on data-centre cost allocation, or an allowed-ROE reduction in either state, affecting the roughly half of the asset base I cannot currently verify.
4. Dividend growth falling below CPIH, which would signal the payout is being used to close the funding gap.

**Position guidance:** Existing holders maintain exposure for the ~4% yield and regulated asset-base compounding. Prospective buyers have no urgent entry signal at $80.88; the $70–72 area, or verified confirmation that the plan is fully funded within ratings headroom, is where the risk/reward improves enough to initiate.

## Conclusion

National Grid offers something scarce in the regulated-utility universe: a five-year, regulator-underwritten programme that compounds the asset base ~61% and is guided to deliver 8–10% annual EPS growth, priced at 13.5–14.0x forward underlying earnings. The FY26 results reconcile precisely across all four disclosed growth rates, statutory and underlying EPS moved together, and the earnings-delivery record is tight.

The discount reflects identifiable mechanics rather than sentiment. Capex at 2.04x operating profit and a 74% statutory payout mean the plan is externally funded; the required £14bn annual run-rate demands a 20.9% step-up on a year that already grew 21.3%; net debt of ~£44bn is being refinanced and extended continuously, against allowed returns fixed until 2031; and the allowed cost of equity that determines whether asset growth becomes shareholder growth is not verifiable in the available evidence. Guidance is also front-loaded — FY28–FY31 implies 6.5–9% growth once the FY27 reset is absorbed.

Base case $88 against $80.88, a $99 bull and a $66 bear, with a ~4% dividend, gives roughly 9–10% probability-weighted total return. That justifies holding for the income and the regulated compounding, and waiting for either a funded-plan confirmation or a lower price before committing new capital.

**Thesis:** National Grid is a monopoly network franchise whose FY27-FY31 plan (>=GBP70bn capex, ~10% asset growth, 8-10% EPS growth) is effectively a pass-through of RIIO-3 and US rate cases. At ~13.5-14.0x guided FY27 underlying EPS with a ~4% yield, the regulated compounding is owned cheaply, but the programme is externally funded, so per-share outcomes hinge on the allowed return and the cost of capital raised.

**Variant view:** Consensus reads 13-15% FY27 EPS growth as a run rate; the arithmetic implies only 6.5-9.0% for FY28-FY31 once the reset is absorbed. The apparent peer discount is also unquantifiable here because peer benchmarks were unavailable, so the relative-value leg of the bull case is weaker than it looks.

- **6-12 months:** Adverse New York or Massachusetts ruling on data-centre cost allocation, or an allowed-ROE reduction in either state.
