## Executive Summary

Oracle has become the cleanest single-name expression of one question: does AI compute demand justify committing fixed, long-dated capital at a scale that exceeds internally generated cash? Oracle has answered yes with more conviction than any other operator. FY2026 (ended May 31, 2026) revenue was $67.357B, up 17.3%, with cloud infrastructure revenue up roughly 77% for the year and 93% in the fourth quarter. Remaining performance obligations reached $638B against approximately $138B a year earlier, and the disclosed conversion schedule (~12% within twelve months) implies roughly $77B of next-twelve-month revenue — approximately Oracle's entire FY26 revenue base — already contracted.

The cost of producing that growth is the reason the stock sits 58% below its September 2025 peak. FY26 capital expenditures were $55.663B, equal to 82.6% of revenue and 5.6x the $9.958B of incremental revenue produced. Free cash flow was -$23.686B. Oracle raised roughly $48B in FY26 (a $43B senior-note program with the 40-year tranche clearing at 6.85%, plus a $5B 6.50% mandatory convertible preferred), established a $20B at-the-market equity program, doubled its commercial paper capacity to $10B, replaced a $6B revolver with a $10B facility, and has signalled roughly $40B more of FY27 financing. S&P cut the rating to BBB− in July 2026, one notch above speculative grade. Buybacks fell to $93M in the first nine months of FY26 from $450M a year earlier, while the common dividend was raised to $0.50 per quarter — approximately $6.1B of annual dividends including preferred, funded in accounting substance by debt.

Our overall score of 4.9 is essentially unchanged from the prior review (4.8 on July 20 at $126.41). The trajectory of declining scores — driven by management quality falling from 7.0 to 4.0 and financial health from 6.0 to 3.5 across five reviews — reflects a business model change that is now fully visible in the filings, not a new deterioration. What has changed since July 20 is the price: up 13.9% with no fundamental development of comparable magnitude. That reduces prospective return without reducing risk, which is why we are holding rather than upgrading. The offsetting positive is a July 27 report of Nvidia offering up to $250B toward OpenAI data-center expansion including lease guarantees; credit support for Oracle's largest presumed counterparty reduces Oracle's collection risk, though Oracle's specific role in the reported Ohio project is not established.

## Company Overview

Oracle reports three segments — cloud and software, hardware, and services — with cloud and software at 86% of trailing revenue, services 9%, and hardware 5%. Within cloud and software, four revenue lines behave very differently, and their divergence is the central fact about the company. For the nine months ended February 28, 2026: cloud infrastructure $12,314M (+70% reported, +68% constant currency); cloud applications $11,762M (+12%, +10% cc); software license $2,856M (−11%, −13% cc); software support $14,861M (+2% reported, **0% constant currency**).

For the full FY26 year, cloud infrastructure reached approximately $18.1B and cloud applications approximately $15.9B, while license declined ~9% and support grew ~1%. In constant currency, cloud infrastructure contributed 85% of Q3 cloud revenue growth and 82% of nine-month growth.

The literal reading: Oracle's highest-margin annuity — software support, roughly $20B annually, priced as a percentage of a legacy perpetual-license base that is no longer being replenished — has stopped growing in real terms. Applications SaaS grows at a low-double-digit rate. Essentially all incremental growth comes from a capital-intensive GPU-and-power business. Revenue is also geographically concentrated: the Americas at 66% of nine-month revenue contributed 86% of constant-currency total revenue growth.

Leadership changed at the same time the capital structure did. Clay Magouyrk and Mike Sicilia became co-CEOs in September 2025, Safra Catz moved to Executive Vice Chair, and Larry Ellison remains Executive Chairman and CTO. Hilary Maxson was appointed CFO in early April 2026; Douglas Kehring signed the Q3 10-Q as principal financial officer, meaning Oracle operated without a titled CFO through the period in which it raised roughly $48B. At least four Item 5.02 officer-change filings were made in nine months.

## Financial Analysis

**Income statement.** FY26 revenue $67.357B (+17.3%); net income $17.087B versus $12.443B. The comparison overstates operating progress: net income includes a $2.7B non-operating realized gain on the sale of Oracle's Ampere Computing interests (SoftBank acquired Ampere in November 2025 for $4.3B of proceeds to Oracle), roughly 16% of FY26 net income and non-recurring. Effective tax rate was ~12.6%, flattered by $1.5B of stock-compensation benefits and burdened by a $958M One Big Beautiful Bill Act charge in Q1.

**Incremental economics.** Cloud and software segment margin fell from 63% to 58% year over year in Q3 FY26 and from 63% to 59% for nine months. The mechanism is explicit in the 10-Q: segment revenue rose 24% in Q3 while segment cloud-and-software direct expenses rose 70%, "primarily due to higher infrastructure expenses." Computing the incremental directly — Q3 segment revenue +$2,897M against direct expenses (cloud/software cost plus sales and marketing) +$1,819M — implies incremental direct margin of 37.2% against a 58% segment average. Consolidated operating margin held at 32% in Q3 only because sales and marketing expense fell 4% and amortization of acquired intangibles fell 25% as older assets ran off. Neither offset repeats.

**Depreciation is ahead, not behind.** FY26 depreciation was approximately $7.6B (nine-month $5.208B) against net PP&E that grew from $43.5B to $100.0B. Capex of $55.7B is a period flow; the $100.0B is an ending balance, and the gap between capex and depreciation means the charge has not caught up to the asset base. As a sensitivity rather than a forecast: if FY27 capex approaches the ~$90B figure discussed in press coverage and assets enter service, FY27 depreciation plausibly lands in a $13-17B range. Each incremental $1B of depreciation is roughly 1.5 points of FY26-scale operating margin, and unlike goodwill amortization this is cash already spent on hardware with a finite life. Consensus is not naive about this: FY27 non-GAAP EPS of $8.05 implies only +6.6% earnings growth against likely 25-30% revenue growth, which is the depreciation and interest drag showing up in the estimates.

**Cash flow, and the Q4 improvement.** The trailing twelve months to February 28, 2026 showed OCF $23,514M against capex $48,250M — FCF of -$24,736M versus net income of $16,210M. Subtracting nine-month figures from full-year figures isolates Q4 FY26: OCF approximately $14.62B (46% of the full year in one quarter), capex approximately $16.49B, FCF approximately **-$1.87B** versus -$21.81B across the first nine months. That is the empirical basis for the bull "cash flow inflection" argument, and it is a real improvement. It should not be annualized. Oracle states in the 10-Q that cloud contracting is highest in Q4 with related cash flows highest in the following quarter; FY26 also included roughly $4.6B of customer prepayments containing a significant financing component and $1.4B of financing receivables sold to financial institutions in nine months. Both pull cash forward without a revenue or margin event. The honest range is: a $58B annualized run rate on Q4 OCF is a labelled sensitivity; the full-year $32.0B already embeds the seasonal peak and is the normalized figure. Against FY27 capex plausibly near $90B, either figure leaves a substantial funding gap.

**Balance sheet (February 28, 2026).** Total assets $245.24B (from $168.36B); notes payable $134.6B current-plus-noncurrent ($130.9B par); cash and marketable securities $39.13B for net debt of approximately $95.5B; total stockholders' equity $39.05B, or 15.9% of assets; operating lease liabilities $21.31B and finance lease liabilities $6.25B; goodwill $62.27B and intangibles $3.64B, giving tangible book equity of approximately **-$26.9B**.

**Forward interest coverage.** FY26 interest expense of approximately $4.6B against operating income of approximately $20.6B is 4.5x. But that interest was incurred on an average balance far below the $134.6B year-end figure. A forward run rate on ~$135B at a blended 5.0-5.3% is $6.8-7.2B, plus approximately $325M of annual preferred dividends (50,000 shares at $6,500). On unchanged EBIT, coverage falls to roughly 2.9-3.0x. One item we could not resolve: Oracle does not separately disclose capitalized interest, and with a large construction-in-progress balance some interest may be capitalized into PP&E rather than expensed, which would mean reported interest understates the economic burden while inflating the depreciable base.

The new $10B revolver requires Consolidated EBITDA / Consolidated Net Interest Expense of at least 3.0x at each fiscal quarter end. Using nine-month FY26 proxies (operating income $14,473M + depreciation $5,208M + amortization $1,239M = $20,920M; net interest $3,160M − $491M interest income = $2,669M) gives roughly 7.8x, and the credit agreement's definitions likely add back stock compensation and restructuring, making true headroom larger. The covenant is not a near-term threat. It is the mechanism by which a demand disappointment becomes a financing problem: net interest at ~$7B against EBITDA of ~$32B would be about 4.6x.

**The Altman Z-Score screen requires correction.** The screening figure of 0.86 sits in the distress zone, but recomputing the public-firm Z with February 2026 balance-sheet data and market equity of ~$415B gives approximately 1.79 (working capital/TA 0.058; retained earnings/TA −0.029; EBIT/TA ~0.084; market equity/total liabilities 2.01; sales/TA 0.275). Two drivers are artifacts. The negative retained-earnings term reflects buyback history — accumulated deficit was −$15.48B at 5/31/25, improving to roughly −$4.3B at FY26 year end after Oracle retired well over $100B of stock over the prior decade — not accumulated losses. Low asset turnover is mechanically produced by the build itself: assets are on the books before the revenue they carry. The Z-Score's direction and its reason (a thin equity cushion against a long-dated liability stack) are informative; its absolute level should not be read as bankruptcy probability for a BBB− issuer with $39B of liquid assets, an undrawn $10B revolver and $10B of CP capacity.

## Growth Analysis

The top-line case is strong and verifiable. Q4 FY26 revenue grew 21% with cloud infrastructure up 93%. RPO of $638B against approximately $138B a year earlier is a 363% increase, attributed in the filings to "certain significant cloud contracts entered into during the period." The disclosed conversion schedule is ~12% within twelve months, 31% in months 13-36, 35% in months 37-60, remainder thereafter.

Three scope limitations travel with that number. RPO is neither deferred revenue nor cash — total deferred revenue at February 28, 2026 was $11.18B, so almost none of the $638B has been paid. Oracle has elected the optional exemption not to disclose variable consideration allocated entirely to wholly unsatisfied performance obligations, so usage-based components are not fully captured in either direction. And 88% converts beyond twelve months with 35% in years four and five, a horizon over which counterparty solvency, AI compute pricing, and unit economics are all genuinely uncertain.

Opposite the RPO sits an obligation stack that is the most under-appreciated item in the filings. Beyond $134.6B of debt and $27.6B of capitalized lease liabilities, the Q3 10-Q discloses **$261B of additional lease commitments**, substantially all data centers, expected to commence between Q4 FY26 and FY28 for terms of fifteen to nineteen years and not reflected on the balance sheet, plus $11B of unconditional purchase obligations primarily for data-center power, plus a lease on which Oracle has guaranteed up to $2.2B of the lessor's borrowing maturing September 2026. Summing gives approximately $434B of committed outflows against $638B of contracted RPO. The two are not directly comparable — RPO is gross revenue, the obligations are costs, and timing differs — but the ratio frames the economics: roughly two-thirds of contracted revenue is committed to fixed capacity and power before GPUs, labor, or interest. These leases will capitalize onto the balance sheet as they commence, raising reported leverage mechanically with no new financing decision.

Estimate revisions are stable rather than inflecting: current quarter +3.6% over 90 days, next quarter +3.1%, current year +0.3%, next year +1.6%. This is not the >30% ninety-day revision pattern that flags an earnings inflection; consensus is tracking, not chasing. The earnings-surprise record — three beats and one miss in four quarters, with recent beats of 1.4% and 3.1% — suggests limited upside slack in near-term estimates.

The legacy side is a genuine structural headwind. Support at 0% constant currency and license at −13% means each cloud "pivot" converts a high-margin support dollar into a lower-margin cloud dollar. Management expects substantially all support customers to renew, so this is decay in growth rather than retention, and a stable ~$20B annuity remains a formidable funding base. But it cannot fund an $80-90B annual capex program.

## Valuation Assessment

The supplied forward P/E field (24.77) mirrors the trailing figure and is not usable; we work from consensus estimates directly. FY26 non-GAAP EPS summed from reported quarterly actuals is $7.55 ($1.47 + $2.26 + $1.79 + $2.03). Trailing GAAP diluted EPS is $5.83.

At $144.00:
- 24.7x trailing GAAP EPS of $5.83
- **17.9x** FY27 consensus non-GAAP EPS of $8.05 (+6.6% growth)
- **13.2x** FY28 consensus non-GAAP EPS of $10.89 (+35.3% growth)
- EV of approximately $510B (market cap $414.8B plus net debt ~$95.5B) = **7.6x** FY26 revenue
- Dividend yield 1.39%

Peer medians in application software are P/E 41.6 with a wide dispersion — PLTR 147.4, CDNS 89.4, APP 41.6, CRM 19.3, ADBE 12.8. Oracle is cheaper than the median on any basis, but the comparison set is not informative: none of these companies runs an $80B capex program, and the derated names (CRM at 19.3x, ADBE at 12.8x) are being marked down for AI-driven application-layer disruption, a different problem. Oracle's economic comparables are hyperscaler capex programs, where Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet fund builds from operating cash flow while carrying net cash or modest net debt, and neo-clouds, where the funding model matches Oracle's but the scale does not.

Analyst targets have not adjusted to the stock. The distribution is 8 strong buy, 29 buy, 6 hold, 1 sell, with a mean target of $248.15, median $240, high $400, low $110 against a $144 price — a 72% gap to the mean. Proximity to or distance from consensus targets is a floor only when a stock is cheap relative to rising estimates. Here estimates are roughly flat and the market's concern is financing and returns on capital rather than earnings trajectory, so a 72% target gap on a name down 58% from its peak is more likely stale coverage than independent confirmation of upside.

Our view is that the multiple is neither stretched nor asymmetrically attractive at $144. Three specific reasons the apparent cheapness is compensation rather than mispricing:

1. **The multiple applies to non-GAAP EPS that excludes stock compensation while the true capital cost is only partly recognized.** FY26 depreciation of ~$7.6B on a $100B net PP&E base that is still growing means the reported margin has not absorbed the assets already in place, let alone those to come.
2. **No free-cash-flow anchor exists.** At -$23.7B in FY26 and likely negative again in FY27 against ~$90B of capex, discounted-cash-flow valuation depends almost entirely on terminal assumptions about OCI returns on invested capital, which no disclosure supports either way.
3. **Enterprise value understates committed capital.** Adding even a conservatively discounted present value of the $261B of lease commitments and $11B of power obligations moves effective enterprise value well above $510B, and those commitments run 15-19 years against GPU generations that turn over far faster.

Against that, 13.2x FY28 for a company growing revenue above 20% with $638B of contracted backlog is a low multiple in absolute terms, and if the Q4 FY26 cash-flow improvement proves partly structural rather than wholly seasonal, the multiple re-rates. We score valuation 5.3: two-sided, with the deep-value entry having been at $115-120 in late July rather than $144 today.

## Competitive Landscape

Oracle's OCI growth of 93% in Q4 is share gain in AI infrastructure, and the database franchise remains the mechanism — enterprise customers value running Oracle Database adjacent to compute, and Oracle can offer cloud, on-premise, hybrid and multicloud deployment where hyperscalers cannot. Substantially all support customers renew.

The funding disadvantage is structural and quantitative. Microsoft funds AI expansion from roughly $46.7B of operating cash flow while generating positive free cash flow; Oracle generated $32.0B of OCF against $55.7B of capex. Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft carry net cash or modest net debt; Oracle carries approximately $95B of net debt against $39B of book equity and negative tangible equity. This gap, not growth, is the explanation for Oracle trading at 17.9x forward earnings while delivering 93% cloud infrastructure growth.

Positioned within the sector, Oracle operates as a neo-cloud at hyperscaler scale with a large legacy software annuity attached. The annuity is both differentiator and vulnerability: roughly $20B of high-margin support revenue and $39B of liquidity capacity that a pure neo-cloud lacks, but flat and decaying as customers migrate.

On the applications side against SAP, Salesforce and Workday, 10-12% constant-currency growth is respectable but not category-leading — and it is the lower-capital-intensity, higher-durability half of Oracle's cloud business growing slowly while the capital-hungry half compounds. Revenue mix shifted to 52% cloud in Q3 from 44%, but a changing revenue mix is not customer diversification. The FY26 10-K added language that in certain OCI offerings Oracle is "more concentrated among a number of large customers," with associated collection and credit risk. Mix and concentration establish different things and should not be conflated: by Oracle's own disclosure, concentration increased.

We hold competitive position at 6.0 — share gains in the fastest-growing part of enterprise IT, offset by a decaying annuity and a cost-of-capital disadvantage that competitors do not carry.

## Risk Assessment

**Customer concentration.** The 10-K's new language acknowledges concentration but names no customer. The widely repeated claim that more than half of the backlog ties to OpenAI is externally reported and unconfirmed by any Oracle filing. S&P's cited rationale of "high exposure to OpenAI" corroborates that exposure is large without establishing a figure. The asymmetry is structural: Oracle's costs are contractual, non-cancellable and 15-19 years long; a meaningful portion of its contracted revenue depends on counterparties that are themselves pre-profit.

**Financing and rate risk.** Roughly $40B must be raised in FY27 into a market that has already repriced Oracle's credit — the February 2026 40-year tranche cleared at 6.85%, and S&P moved to BBB−, one notch above speculative grade, in July 2026. If spreads widen or the $20B ATM must be used at depressed equity prices, the cost of the build rises against contracts whose pricing is fixed. The ATM is a contingent, not current, dilution item: zero shares had been sold as of May 31, 2026. At $144, full use would be approximately 139M shares — a 4.8% increase in count and 4.6% ownership dilution. The mandatory convertible converts January 15, 2029 into 24.99-31.24M shares, at maximum a 1.07% ownership dilution.

**Depreciation and margin.** Segment margin compression from 63% to 58% is already visible, and the depreciation charge has not caught up to the asset base. This is the single most predictable negative in FY27-28 reported earnings.

**Litigation and governance.** A putative securities class action filed February 3, 2026 in D. Del. names Oracle, its CTO, both CEOs, two other executives, and a sitting director, alleging false and misleading statements about the cloud infrastructure business. Naming a director individually in a Rule 10b-5 case is comparatively unusual; that said, no schedule had been set for responses, such complaints routinely follow large declines, and nothing here establishes wrongdoing. The Netherlands GDPR class action (Privacy Collective) had an adverse non-binding Advocate General opinion on January 30, 2026 with judgment scheduled June 28, 2026, outcome not established. Tax disputes are active in twelve jurisdictions against $11.4B of non-current income taxes payable.

**Restructuring.** The FY2026 plan carries up to $2.1B of estimated cost, with approximately $1.78B expensed for the year. Press reports of ~30,000 job cuts are not confirmed in the filings, which disclose only dollar amounts described as primarily severance.

### Options Market Signal

Near-term ATM implied volatility of 65.2% is fair against 64.1% one-year realized (spread 1.1%), with the IV percentile within the current chain at 36% and near-term put/call skew balanced at 2.6%. The informative part is the term structure and the delta. The three-month tenor prices 74.7% IV with a ±20.1% implied move by September 18 — the market is pricing the Q1 FY27 print (expected around September 10) as a large binary, not a routine quarter. Six-month skew is −4.4%.

Since the July 20 snapshot, ATM IV fell 13.6 points from 78.9% to 65.3% while the price rose 13.9% and put/call open interest fell from 1.54 to 0.91. Protection was unwound into the bounce, which means the shareholder base carries less cushion into September than it did in July. Short interest is only 2.9% of float at 1.4 days to cover, so there is neither squeeze fuel nor crowded bearish positioning — the bounce off $114.99 was long-side buying rather than a short covering event, and it can reverse without mechanical support. Fresh put buying at the $137 August 21 strike (6.5x volume/open interest) is consistent with hedging the September event rather than a directional bet.

None of this changes the fundamental view. It does argue against adding into the print.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case — $235 (+63%)

Oracle has contracted $638B of revenue that competitors did not bid for, and the ~12% converting within twelve months (~$77B) is already visible in FY27 estimates. Q4 FY26 free cash flow of approximately -$1.9B versus -$21.8B for the prior nine months shows that as capacity enters service, cash inflows begin to offset the build. If customer prepayments reflect genuinely improved commercial terms — customers funding capacity in advance — that is a durable working-capital improvement rather than a pull-forward. Reported credit support from Nvidia for OpenAI's expansion reduces the collection risk that S&P cited. On FY28 consensus non-GAAP EPS of $10.89, a 22x multiple gives $240, roughly the analyst median. Ellison has not sold and both his October 2025 option grant and CFO Maxson's $185.35 strike are underwater, so leadership's economic exposure is squarely to the share price.

### Bear Case — $98 (−32%)

Capex of 82.6% of revenue and 5.6x incremental revenue is a business model, not a quarter. FY27 depreciation plausibly rises $5-9B, taking 8-14 points off the FY26-scale operating margin before interest, while forward interest coverage falls toward 2.9-3.0x. Roughly $40B of new financing must be raised at BBB− with new-issue coupons already at 6.85%. The $261B of 15-19 year lease commitments will capitalize onto the balance sheet over FY26-28 and cannot be cancelled if AI compute pricing compresses or inference migrates to cheaper substrates. The annuity funding all of this — support at 0% constant currency, license at −13% — is decaying. A funding stumble at a major counterparty converts contracted revenue into stranded capacity, and 12x FY27 non-GAAP EPS of $8.05 gives $97; 15x trailing GAAP $5.83 gives $87.

### Base Case — $160 (+11%)

Oracle raises FY27 capital, cash burn narrows but stays negative, and FY27 non-GAAP EPS lands near consensus $8.05 as depreciation and interest absorb most of the revenue growth. A 20x multiple on $8.05 gives $161; 15x FY28's $10.89 gives $163. We use $160.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Recommendation: HOLD** (hold rationale: uncertain — the range of outcomes on financing and returns on invested capital is genuinely wide, and neither leg is resolvable from current disclosure).

**Base case $160 (+11.1%). Bull case $235 (+63.2%). Bear case $98 (−31.9%). Reassessment: Q1 FY27 earnings, expected around September 10, 2026.**

At $144, the bear case is 32% below and the base case 11% above. That is unattractive skew for new capital. At $115-120, the same scenario set gave roughly 33-39% upside to base against 15-18% downside to bear, which is why this was a more interesting entry three weeks ago than it is today.

**Upgrade triggers (any two would move us to BUY):**
1. Q1 or Q2 FY27 free cash flow better than -$4B *without* reliance on prepayments with a significant financing component or receivable sales — i.e., the Q4 FY26 improvement proving structural.
2. FY27 financing completed at a blended cost inside 5.5% with no ATM issuance, evidencing that the credit market will fund the build without equity dilution.
3. Disclosure that quantifies OCI unit economics or largest-customer concentration, removing the single largest analytical gap.
4. Price near $120-125 with the above fundamentals intact.

**Downgrade triggers (any one would move us to AVOID):**
1. A second rating downgrade to speculative grade, or ATM issuance at prices below $140.
2. RPO declining sequentially, or disclosure of contract modification, deferral or cancellation on a significant cloud contract.
3. Cloud and software segment margin falling below 55%, or forward interest coverage guided below 2.5x.
4. Any credible disclosure of funding distress at a major OCI counterparty.

## Conclusion

Oracle is executing on revenue. The market's concern is not whether OCI grows — it grew 93% in the fourth quarter — but whether $55.7B of FY26 capex and $261B of fifteen-to-nineteen-year lease commitments earn a return above a cost of capital that has visibly risen within a single fiscal year. The filings do not answer that question, and neither does the RPO: $638B of contracted revenue sits opposite roughly $434B of committed outflows, with 88% of the revenue converting beyond twelve months.

Financial health at 3.5 and risk factors at 3.5 are the binding constraints. Negative tangible book equity, forward interest coverage near 2.9-3.0x, a dividend of roughly $6.1B paid while burning $23.7B of free cash flow, and $40B of FY27 financing to raise at BBB− are not offset by 17.9x forward earnings. Management quality at 4.0 reflects four senior officer changes in nine months spanning the CEO office and the finance function during the largest capital raise in company history, a compensation framework whose six disclosed performance measures are all growth or margin metrics with no free-cash-flow, return-on-capital or leverage measure among them, and zero open-market insider purchases through a 58% drawdown. On that last point we would not over-read: large-cap executives under pre-clearance and 10b5-1 constraints rarely buy, Ellison's existing stake makes incremental purchases economically irrelevant, and Jeffrey Henley's $66M June sale was driven by options expiring June 30. The absence of buying is weak evidence.

Growth at 6.5 and competitive position at 6.0 are real and keep this out of AVOID territory. Oracle has near-term liquidity ($39.13B of cash and securities, an undrawn $10B revolver, $10B of CP capacity, an untapped $20B ATM) and covenant headroom of roughly 7.8x against a 3.0x test. There is no imminent solvency question.

We hold. The 25% rally from $114.99 on July 24 to $145.74 on August 4 consumed most of the margin of safety without resolving any of the open questions, and the options market is pricing a ±20% move by September 18. We would re-engage in the low $120s, or on evidence in the Q1 FY27 print that FY27 cash burn is narrowing on an unadjusted basis.
