## Executive Summary

Oxford Industries is no longer a diversified multi-brand apparel company in any operational sense. It is a Tommy Bahama business with four attachments. Tommy Bahama produced $94.6M of the $142.9M of pre-corporate segment EBITDA in fiscal 2025 (year ended January 31, 2026) and $40.1M of $56.9M in Q1 fiscal 2026 — 66% and 70% respectively, on 56% of sales. Lilly Pulitzer, the segment that grew 4.3% last year at a 15.4% EBITDA margin, saw Q1 sales fall 8.8%, gross margin fall 440bp to 61.2% and segment EBITDA fall 34.9%. Johnny Was lost $8.5M in fiscal 2025 after roughly $168M of cumulative impairments since 2023, and the 10-K names its trademark and Jack Rogers' goodwill as the assets with the least excess of fair value over carrying value. Seven brands are currently delivering the risk profile of one.

The reason to continue owning the shares rather than selling them is arithmetic, not narrative. Capital expenditure falls to roughly $60M in fiscal 2026 from $108M in fiscal 2025 as the Lyons, Georgia distribution center completes. Against an operating cash flow base of roughly $110-120M — the level actually delivered last year despite a GAAP net loss — that implies free cash flow of roughly $50-65M against a $41.8M dividend, or about 1.2-1.6x coverage. In fiscal 2025 the same distribution paid out roughly $97M against $11.3M of free cash flow, with long-term debt rising $85.3M to fund the gap. The dividend was declared at $0.70 both alongside the loss-making 10-K in March and alongside the June guidance revision, and Oxford has paid one every quarter since 1960. Separately, approximately $45M of IEEPA tariffs paid before the February 2026 Supreme Court invalidation sit as an unrecognized claim worth roughly $3.01 per share pre-tax and $2.17 after tax at the guided 28% rate — invisible in book value, in EPS and in every screening ratio.

The reason not to add capital is that both of those supports are conditional on trade policy that is presently unresolved in two directions at once. Management's fiscal 2026 guidance explicitly assumes current lower tariff rates persist. The replacement Section 122 tariffs imposed February 24, 2026 were ruled unlawful by the Court of International Trade in May but remain under administrative stay pending the government's appeal. If that stay resolves for the government, the guidance breaks and so does the coverage math. Our score moves from 4.7 to 4.35, and the recommendation stays HOLD. The June 10 print partially confirmed the prior upgrade (adjusted EPS floor raised, capex halved, Tommy Bahama inflecting positive) and partially reversed it (Lilly Pulitzer materially weaker, Q2 revenue guided 5.8% below consensus, the non-GAAP adjustment list expanded from one category to five).

## Company Overview

Oxford, incorporated in Georgia in 1942 and NYSE-listed since 1964, owns and operates Tommy Bahama, Lilly Pulitzer, Johnny Was, Southern Tide, The Beaufort Bonnet Company, Duck Head and Jack Rogers. Direct-to-consumer is approximately 82% of sales: full-price retail 35%, e-commerce 34%, food and beverage 8%, outlets 5%, with wholesale at 18%. There were 351 DTC locations at May 2, 2026, down from 355 at year-end.

Three structural features define the business. First, Tommy Bahama operates 28 food-and-beverage locations, and stores adjacent to one generate roughly twice the sales per square foot of other domestic full-price stores. That is a genuine and hard-to-copy format, at a cost of more than $5M per Marlin Bar build-out. Second, the target consumer is 45+ with household income above $150K, which has historically supported low-60s gross margins and insulated the brands from fast-fashion price competition. Third, the footprint is deliberately resort and coastal: Tommy Bahama DTC is 34% Florida, 16% California, 12% Hawaii; Lilly Pulitzer full-price retail is 51% Florida.

That third feature is the one the current macro environment stresses. Management's own June commentary cited "weak consumer sentiment and higher energy prices." A consumer whose discretionary budget is anchored in second-home wealth and resort travel is directly exposed to these pressures.

Sourcing is entirely third-party — roughly 360 suppliers, top ten at ~25% of purchases, none above 10%, no long-term contracts.

## Financial Analysis

The five-year arc is a post-pandemic peak that has more than halved:

| | FY2022 | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net sales | $1,411.5M | $1,571.5M | $1,516.6M | $1,477.8M |
| Gross margin | 63.0% | 63.4% | 62.9% | **60.7%** |
| SG&A % of sales | ~47% | 48.1% | 51.9% | **55.3%** |
| Operating income | $218.8M | $81.0M | $119.0M | **−$31.3M** |
| Diluted EPS | — | $3.82 | $5.87 | **−$1.86** |
| Adj. EBITDA | — | — | $186.9M | **$95.6M** |

The controlling fact is the cost structure, not the revenue line. SG&A has risen roughly 830 basis points as a percentage of sales since fiscal 2022 while gross margin has fallen about 230bp. Store count went from 292 at January 2023 to 355 at January 2026 alongside a new distribution center, into a revenue line that peaked in fiscal 2023 and has declined twice since. Fiscal 2025's $30.9M SG&A increase decomposes as roughly $15M from new bricks-and-mortar, $13M employment, $8M software, $6M occupancy, $6M consulting and $5M of credit-loss provision (Saks Global and one other wholesale bankruptcy), offset by a $7M advertising cut. Cutting advertising while occupancy and software rise is the wrong side of that trade for a brand-dependent retailer.

The fiscal 2025 GAAP loss was dominated by $61.0M of non-cash goodwill and intangible impairment ($57M Johnny Was, $4M Jack Rogers, $3.02 per share after tax). Excluding it, operating income was approximately +$29.7M, a 2.0% margin against 7.8% in fiscal 2024 and 15.5% in fiscal 2022. Adjusted EPS of $2.11 against fiscal 2024 GAAP EPS of $5.87 is a decline of roughly two-thirds. The write-off explains the headline; it does not explain the earnings reset.

**Tariffs are the dominant P&L variable.** Duties paid rose to $95M in fiscal 2025 from $60M, with the average effective rate on imported value going from ~19% to ~30%. Tariffs added $30M to fiscal 2025 cost of goods and roughly $11M to Q1 fiscal 2026 — $0.55 per share, larger than the entire $0.39 gap between GAAP and adjusted EPS that quarter. China fell from ~40% of production to 29% in fiscal 2025 and is running at a ~15% annualized rate. The important qualifier, stated plainly in the 10-K: the majority of fabrics still originate in China, and cotton used in non-China-manufactured product largely comes from Chinese mills. Country-of-assembly diversification does not fully insulate either the tariff exposure or the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act detention risk.

| Operating cash flow | $244.3M | $194.0M | $119.6M | $7.9M |
| Capex | ~$76M | $134.0M | $108.3M | $22.8M |
| **Free cash flow** | ~$168M | $60.0M | **$11.3M** | **−$14.9M** |

Fiscal 2025 distributions of roughly $97M against $11.3M of free cash flow were funded by an $85.3M increase in long-term debt. The 956,484 shares repurchased at an average $57.12 — including 842,007 at $59.38 in a single quarter — are approximately 32% underwater, a mark-to-market shortfall near $17.4M. Buybacks stopped entirely in Q1 fiscal 2026 with $95M still authorized.

Liquidity is adequate. The $325M asset-based revolver matures March 2028 at SOFR + 135-185bp (~5% weighted average), was drawn $142.7M at May 2 with $177M unused, and carries a fixed-charge coverage covenant of ≥1.0x that springs only if excess availability falls below the greater of $23.5M or 10% for three consecutive business days. It has never been triggered.

Screening output on this balance sheet reads worse than the flexibility warrants. A recomputed Altman Z of 1.83 (1.99 excluding the non-cash impairment) sits on the distress boundary, and a nine-signal Piotroski score of 3 of 9 looks alarming. Both understate the case: operating cash flow was positive $119.6M through a GAAP loss, $177M of revolver is undrawn, a $92M LIFO reserve means FIFO inventory would be roughly $257M against $165M carried, and the ~$45M tariff claim is off balance sheet. The Z-score also structurally penalizes a lease-heavy retailer carrying $449.7M of present-value lease liabilities. That said, four of the six failing Piotroski signals — current ratio 1.18 to 1.10, long-term debt from 2.4% to 8.9% of assets, gross margin 62.9% to 60.7%, asset turnover 1.176 to 1.129 — have nothing to do with the write-off. Financially stretched, not financially distressed: financial health scores 4.5, unchanged, with the capex step-down offsetting the debt build.

Shareholders' equity fell from $622.6M to $514.8M during fiscal 2025 and stood at $523.4M at May 2, or $35.06 per share. Price to book is 1.11x.

## Growth Analysis

There is no growth thesis here, only a stabilization thesis with one working engine.

Fiscal 2026 guidance of $1.475-1.505B sits on top of $1,477.8M delivered — flat, after declines of 3.5% and 2.6%. Q1 sales fell 0.4% to $391.4M with operating income down 38.2% and GAAP EPS down 41.2% to $1.00. Q2 is guided to $380-400M against $403M last year, a midpoint roughly 5.8% below the consensus that existed before June 10.

Segment divergence is the entire picture. **Tommy Bahama** grew 3.9% with gross margin up 110bp to 65.7% and EBITDA up 4.7% to $40.1M — a genuine inflection after a 4.7% decline in fiscal 2025, and the single most important positive datapoint in the file. Food and beverage was the fastest-growing channel at +14%. **Lilly Pulitzer** fell 8.8% with e-commerce down 17% and gross margin down 440bp. Two structural facts raise the stakes: LP e-commerce is 49% of LP sales and 44% of that is flash clearance, meaning roughly 21% of LP revenue depends on discount events. A brand structured that way has limited defense when the consumer softens, and a 440bp gross-margin decline is exactly what that vulnerability looks like in practice. **Johnny Was** fell 12.9% with wholesale down 34%, EBITDA at −$1.2M, sales per square foot down 20% year over year to ~$490, and store count cut from 78 to 73. **Emerging Brands** grew 12.8% but with gross margin down 570bp — growth purchased with price.

Estimate revisions do not show an inflection. The near quarter was cut 10% in thirty days to $1.30, which is simply consensus resetting to the company's own $1.20-1.40 adjusted guidance midpoint. The following quarter was raised 11.4% to −$0.59. Current-year consensus of $2.46 rose 2.7% in thirty days, consistent with the raised adjusted floor, and sits below the $2.50 guidance midpoint. Next year's $3.02 has drifted down 2.8% over ninety days. Nothing here resembles a genuine earnings inflection; this is a flat-to-marginally-negative revision picture with a mechanical guidance reset inside it.

The reported earnings "growth" from $2.11 adjusted to a $2.50 guided midpoint is tariff relief and cost control, not volume. Growth prospects score 3.5.

Peer medians in Apparel, Accessories & Luxury Goods sit at 16.7x with the group heterogeneous: two Under Armour share classes at negative earnings, Carter's at 16.7x, G-III at 12.5x, Capri at 32.1x. The relevant comparison is G-III at 12.5x — a low-growth apparel business with a similar demand profile. Oxford at 15.8x current-year adjusted earnings on a flat revenue line and 20.5x guided GAAP is not obviously cheap against that. The 7.5x EV/adjusted-EBITDA looks more supportive, but adjusted EBITDA excludes LIFO charges that represent real replacement-cost inflation passing through inventory, and excludes impairments the company itself has flagged as likely to recur.

Two things do support the valuation. The 7.18% yield is now plausibly covered: guided capex of ~$60M against an operating cash flow base of $110-120M implies free cash flow of roughly $50-65M against a $41.8M distribution, about 1.2-1.6x. That is a sensitivity rather than a forecast, and Q1 alone burned $14.9M of free cash flow while paying $10.6M of dividends funded by a $26.3M revolver draw — though Oxford's working capital systematically builds in Q1 as receivables rise ($24.3M in Q1), so no single quarter should be annualized. The second support is the tariff refund: ~$45M of IEEPA tariffs paid, ~$25M of Phase I claims filed, ~$5M received by June 11, nothing recognized in the accounts. Full recovery is roughly $3.01 per share pre-tax and $2.17 after tax, or 7.7% of market capitalization, with the caveat that ~$9M sits capitalized in inventory and would reduce carrying cost rather than income, and the 10-K explicitly warns refunds may not arrive in amounts or timeframes that meaningfully offset the impact.

Where the argument does not work: against fiscal 2024 GAAP EPS of $5.87 the stock trades at 6.6x, which is often presented as evidence of mispricing. The market is pricing a permanent reset in earning power, and the evidence supports the market — SG&A is 830bp higher as a share of a smaller revenue base, the store fleet and distribution center are sized for $1.5-1.6B of revenue that has not materialized, and a $61M write-off has already confirmed one acquisition's value destruction. Cheapness against a peak that included a 15.5% operating margin is not a catalyst.

Six analysts, all Hold, with targets from $36 to $44 and a mean of $40 — a 2.6% spread to the current price. Valuation scores 5.0: yield and refund optionality provide floor support, but there is no identified mechanism for multiple expansion and the GAAP multiple is above the peer median.

## Competitive Landscape

Tommy Bahama, at 65.7% gross margin and 17.9% segment EBITDA margin in Q1 with a restaurant-anchored store format no competitor has replicated at scale, is a defensible franchise. Its sales per square foot still fell to $715 from $770, but the brand returned to growth with margin expansion in the most recent quarter.

The rest of the portfolio is weaker than the brand count suggests. Lilly Pulitzer's dependence on flash clearance for roughly a fifth of segment revenue, combined with 51% Florida exposure in full-price retail, makes it a discount-dependent franchise in a weakening consumer environment; sales per square foot fell to $730 from $748. Johnny Was, after ~$168M of impairments in three years and a 20% single-year decline in productivity, has not demonstrated it belongs in the portfolio.

Two industry-level shifts are worth flagging because they are specific rather than generic. First, the contraction of the multi-brand and specialty channel — Saks Global's bankruptcy being the concrete instance — removes a low-cost brand-discovery mechanism and pushes customer acquisition toward paid digital and owned stores, both more expensive. Oxford spent $104M (7% of sales) on advertising and still cut it by $7M while total SG&A rose. Second, the 10-K now devotes multiple paragraphs to AI-mediated distribution: third-party shopping assistants and recommendation engines that can reduce brand visibility, and generative design tools that can approximate distinctive prints. For a company whose Lilly Pulitzer franchise is built almost entirely on proprietary prints and which derives 34% of revenue from e-commerce, that is a real rather than fashionable concern.

The cleanest external calibration is relative performance. Oxford outperformed its own comparator group in each of fiscal 2021 through 2024 and then returned −53% in fiscal 2025 against −15% for the peer index. That pattern argues the collapse was substantially company-specific — Johnny Was, the Lyons capex cycle, and a debt-funded buyback at $57-59 — layered on a difficult sector. Competitive position scores 4.5: one improving asset, two deteriorating, and brand concentration worsening despite a constant brand count.

## Risk Assessment

**Trade policy, two-sided and unresolved.** Guidance assumes current lower tariff rates persist. Section 122 tariffs imposed February 24, 2026 were ruled unlawful in May but remain under administrative stay pending appeal. Reinstatement would restore a burden comparable to the $30M that hit fiscal 2025 cost of goods and would break both the EPS guidance and the dividend coverage math. In the other direction, the $45M refund claim is real but unrecognized and administratively uncertain. A plausible bear path is partial refunds arriving slowly and being offset by Section 122 restoration.

**Further impairment is the company's own base case.** The 10-K identifies the Johnny Was indefinite-lived trademark and Jack Rogers goodwill as the units with the least excess of fair value over carrying value. Fiscal 2026 adjusted guidance embeds zero further impairment and only $0.22 of LIFO — precisely the Q1 actual — against $8.4M of LIFO charges in fiscal 2025 and $4.4M in Q1 alone. The gap between guided GAAP EPS ($1.70-2.10) and guided adjusted EPS ($2.30-2.70) is more likely to widen than narrow.

**Distribution center transition, happening now.** The Lyons facility was substantially completed in Q1 and most remaining brands transition during the first half of fiscal 2026. The 10-K added new language: if sales fall short of the levels assumed in the design projections, the facility may operate below planned capacity. The project was launched when revenue was $1.57B and growing; revenue is now guided at $1.49B. Depreciation steps up ($68-72M expected against $65.9M) against a flat sales base, and management has not quantified expected offsetting unit-cost savings in any filing.

**Geographic concentration into hurricane season.** With 34% of Tommy Bahama DTC and 51% of Lilly Pulitzer full-price retail in Florida, plus the distribution center in Georgia, the August-October window carries specific event risk. Helene and Milton hit the Sarasota store and the Lyons facility in 2024; the 2023 Lahaina fire destroyed a Marlin Bar.

**Litigation and governance overhang.** Four plaintiff firms announced investigations after June 10. These are solicitations, not filed complaints, and no matter appears in any Oxford filing. The theory advanced — that undisclosed cost pressures were eroding an outlook management maintained — sits awkwardly against a release that raised the adjusted EPS floor and disclosed tariff impacts in granular dollar terms. Separately, one widely circulated claim that executives "sold thousands of shares at $44.62" before the guidance revision is contradicted by the Form 4 record: every May 29 disposition carries transaction code F (shares withheld by the issuer for tax on RSU settlement) paired with same-date code M conversions. No code S open-market sale appears. On June 9 the board tightened advance-notice and Rule 14a-19 requirements and added exclusive-forum provisions two weeks before the annual meeting, following a −53% TSR year. That combination is defensively oriented, though it is equally consistent with routine bylaw modernization many issuers undertook in the same period.

**Dilution and share count.** Unvested awards total ~814,439 units, 5.45% of shares outstanding, which would dilute existing holders' ownership by 5.17% if all settled in new shares. Actual Q1 dilution was 113K shares, or 0.76%. The March 2026 grants were unusually large in share terms because the price had fallen — a mechanical consequence of value-denominated sizing that accelerates share creep if the price stays depressed. Offsetting this, diluted shares have fallen 8.0% since fiscal 2022.

Risk factors score 3.8, down slightly from 4.0: the tariff appeal, the named impairment candidates, the in-progress distribution center cutover and hurricane season all sit inside the next two quarters.

### Options Market Signal

Near-term ATM implied volatility of 64.6% against 64.4% one-year realized is fair — the market is not pricing near-term dislocation, and the 10th-percentile reading within the current chain says this is a quiet period for the name. The term structure is in contango, with 76.8% at the September 18 tenor implying a ±21.2% move, consistent with the next earnings report falling inside that window.

The informative change is positioning. Put/call open interest went from 0.70 on June 8 to 7.43 — a large build of put open interest across the period in which the stock fell 11.8%. Near-term skew is reported as elevated put demand. Yet recent put/call volume ratio is 0.25, meaning current flow is call-weighted against heavily put-weighted standing positions. Read alongside 34.0% short interest of float (3.29M shares, 5.6 days to cover), the most defensible interpretation is that bearish exposure is largely established and partially hedged, with recent flow leaning the other way. That configuration argues against initiating a short here and is one reason we do not carry this to AVOID. The September 11, 2025 session — a 27% single-day gain on 2.17M shares against 418K average volume — demonstrates what a good print does to a name with this float and this short base.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case — $50 (+28%)

Tommy Bahama's Q1 inflection (+3.9% sales, +110bp gross margin, +4.7% EBITDA) proves durable, and Lilly Pulitzer's corrective actions stabilize a brand that grew 4.3% at a 15.4% margin only a year ago. Section 122 tariffs are permanently vacated on appeal and the ~$45M IEEPA refund lands, adding roughly $2.17 per share after tax that no model currently carries. Capex at $60M against $110-120M of operating cash flow generates $50-65M of free cash flow, comfortably covering the $41.8M dividend and allowing debt to fall from $142.7M. Adjusted EPS moves toward $3.00-3.25 in fiscal 2027, and 16x on that plus the refund supports roughly $50. Note this bull case exceeds the $44 sell-side high because it embeds a refund no published target models.

### Bear Case — $27 (−31%)

Section 122 is reinstated on appeal, restoring roughly $30M of annual cost of goods and breaking the guidance on which coverage depends. Lilly Pulitzer's flash-clearance dependence proves structural rather than cyclical as Florida housing wealth compresses, and a further Johnny Was or Jack Rogers write-down arrives. Distribution center depreciation steps up against flat sales. Free cash flow fails to cover the distribution a second consecutive year, and the board halves the dividend rather than fund it on a revolver drawn to $180M+. Yield support disappears and the stock trades toward 0.75x book, roughly $27, against a 52-week low of $30.57.

### What the shares are actually pricing

At 15.8x current-year adjusted earnings and 1.11x book with a 7.2% yield, the market is pricing a permanent reset in earning power and assigning modest probability to either tail. The scenario spread of +28%/−31% is close to symmetric. That is a HOLD, and the classification is income: the dividend, now plausibly covered by the capex step-down, is what makes the position worth carrying while the tariff appeal and the Lilly Pulitzer trajectory resolve.

Two forward-looking signals lean mildly positive on management. The CEO bought 5,000 shares at $35.12 on December 12, 2025 and 2,500 shares at $36.90 on June 12 — two days after the guidance revision and the associated decline. Those are the only discretionary transactions in the record, and they are purchases. The amounts (~$268K combined) are small against $5.5M of annual compensation and two purchases by one executive is a thin sample, but the direction is unambiguous. Compensation actually paid to the CEO was −$1.01M in fiscal 2025, and NEO average actually-paid compensation fell to $157,645 from $1.20M — alignment demonstrated in outcome rather than design. Against that: the fiscal 2025 buyback was procyclical, revolver-funded and executed roughly 47% above today's price; the non-GAAP adjustment list expanded from one contemplated category to five in a single quarter, three of them new; and the distribution center appears to have been sized for revenue that did not arrive. Management quality scores 5.0.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Reassessment timeline:** The Q2 fiscal 2026 report, expected in the second week of September 2026 (the September 18 option tenor implies a ±21.2% move). Guided sales $380-400M, GAAP EPS $1.13-1.33, adjusted EPS $1.20-1.40. The hurricane season and the completion of the multi-brand distribution center cutover both fall inside the same window.

**Upgrade triggers (to BUY):**
1. Lilly Pulitzer segment sales decline narrowing to better than −3% year over year with gross margin recovering at least 200bp of the 440bp Q1 loss, confirming the weakness was promotional rather than structural.
2. Cumulative IEEPA tariff refunds received exceeding $20M with the Section 122 appeal resolved against the government, which would simultaneously de-risk guidance and convert an unrecognized asset into cash.
3. Free cash flow through the first half turning positive with revolver borrowings down from $142.7M, demonstrating the capex step-down is funding the dividend rather than the balance sheet.
4. Absent the above, a price near $30-32 (roughly 0.9x book, ~12x guided adjusted EPS, 8.7-9.3% yield) would restore enough asymmetry to justify capital independent of the operational triggers.

**Downgrade triggers (to AVOID):**
1. Any reduction in the $0.70 quarterly dividend, or a second consecutive quarter of negative free cash flow accompanied by revolver borrowings above $170M.
2. Section 122 tariffs reinstated on appeal without offsetting refund receipts, which would invalidate the guidance basis for coverage.
3. A further impairment at Johnny Was or Jack Rogers, or Johnny Was segment EBITDA remaining negative through the seasonally strong first half, which would confirm the unit is not stabilizable within the portfolio.
4. Tommy Bahama returning to negative comparable sales growth. With 70% of pre-corporate segment EBITDA in one brand, that single datapoint carries near-unlevered consequence for consolidated profitability.

**Position guidance:** Hold existing positions for the yield; do not add at $38.97. The two-sided tariff resolution and the Q2 print are the events that should determine direction, and both fall within ninety days.

## Conclusion

Oxford's score moves from 4.7 to 4.35, and the trajectory that ran 4.1 (AVOID) to 4.7 (HOLD) flattens rather than continues. The June 10 print delivered evidence on both sides: Tommy Bahama inflected positive with margin expansion, capex was halved in a way that mechanically restores dividend coverage, and the adjusted EPS floor was raised — against which Lilly Pulitzer deteriorated materially, Q2 revenue was guided 5.8% below the prior consensus, and the non-GAAP adjustment list expanded fivefold. The price decline from $44.12 to $38.97 is not the reason for the score change; the Lilly Pulitzer gross-margin collapse and the near-quarter consensus reset are.

What remains is a business with one healthy brand carrying 70% of segment profitability, a cost structure built for revenue that has declined for two consecutive years, $142.7M of debt where there was $31M fifteen months ago, and a 7.18% dividend whose coverage depends on trade policy currently under appellate stay. Offsetting that: $177M of undrawn revolver behind a covenant that has never sprung, positive operating cash flow through a GAAP loss year, an unrecognized $45M tariff claim worth ~$2.17 per share after tax, a CEO adding to a personal position two days after bad news, and 34% short interest that makes the downside crowded and the upside violent on any good print.

**HOLD** — income rationale. Collect the 7.2% while the Section 122 tail risks resolve; reassess at the September print. Sell on a dividend reduction or Section 122 reinstatement; add only near $30-32 or on confirmed Lilly Pulitzer stabilization.
