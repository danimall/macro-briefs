## Executive Summary

Millrose Properties is a land-banking vehicle spun out of Lennar in February 2025 that owns residential homesites, funds their horizontal development by builder counterparties, and collects a monthly option fee until the builder exercises and takes the finished lots down. As of June 30, 2026 it held $8,799M of Invested Capital across 19 counterparties, 30 states and 143,771 homesites at a 9.2% weighted average portfolio yield. The cost base is two lines: a 1.25%-of-tangible-assets management fee paid to a Kennedy Lewis subsidiary (~$29.9M in Q2) and interest expense (~$40.0M in Q2). Residual costs are roughly $1.1M per quarter. The model is therefore unusually transparent and depends on exactly two variables — portfolio yield and cost of debt.

Q2 2026, reported August 4, confirmed the earnings model rather than changing it: revenue $196.9M, net income $125.9M, diluted EPS $0.76, AFFO $0.77/share, dividend $0.77 (sixth consecutive increase), debt-to-capitalization ~30%, and $1.0B of net cash proceeds from homesite sales. The 10-Q discloses that no option agreements were terminated, forfeited, or expired without exercise in either the three or six months ended June 30, 2026 — the single most important disconfirming fact for the bear case, because it means carrying values are being validated by actual takedowns rather than by appraisal. Two new growth commitments landed since the last review: a JPI multifamily land-banking facility (July 30, size undisclosed) and, on August 7, support for Dream Finders Homes' agreed $2.2B all-cash acquisition of Beazer Homes, under which Millrose "plans to acquire homesites from the combined organization" alongside an acquisition-financing commitment of up to $1.25B attributed in the issuer's own release to "its manager," not to Millrose.

The constraint on the equity is arithmetic, not narrative. 1H26 dividends declared per share ($1.53) exactly equalled 1H26 AFFO per share ($1.53). With a ~100% payout, no retained earnings, a share price at 0.82x the $35.24 June 30 book value (making equity issuance dilutive to book value per share), and a contractual 1:1 debt-to-equity cap absent Lennar's consent, portfolio growth must be debt-funded — and it was: Invested Capital rose $329M in 1H26 while total debt rose $375M to $2,518M and equity was flat. At a ~7.84% post-fee asset yield against a ~6.5% cost of debt, each incremental turn of leverage buys about 130bp of net spread, which is thin compensation for balance-sheet risk. The result is an income instrument whose total return, absent re-rating, is roughly the 10.6% yield plus ~1% per quarter of decelerating dividend growth. That is a fair, not compelling, proposition. **HOLD, on income grounds.**

## Company Overview

Millrose owns raw and partially developed land through "Property LLCs" held under a taxable REIT subsidiary. A builder counterparty performs the horizontal development (grading, utilities, roads) on Millrose's land using its own contractors under a guaranteed construction agreement; Millrose funds within pre-agreed budgets but performs no development. The builder pays a monthly option fee for the right — not the obligation — to purchase finished homesites, and takes them down over time at pre-agreed prices. Millrose recycles proceeds into new land and separately makes development loans to third-party developers.

Millrose has no employees. Millrose Management LLC, a wholly owned subsidiary of Kennedy Lewis Investment Management, supplies all personnel, systems, the technology platform and public-company compliance functions for 1.25% of tangible assets annually. REIT status was elected beginning with the taxable year ended December 31, 2025; land activity sits in TRSs and option fee income is converted into qualifying REIT income at the parent through intercompany promissory note interest.

Two structural facts define the equity. First, Lennar accounted for 72% of Q2 2026 total revenues (down from 88% of FY2025 option fee revenue) and 74% of the ~$6.9B of future land development commitments, with the Master Program Agreement representing 67.6% of Invested Capital — and the agreements grant Millrose no exclusivity, no right of first refusal, no minimum volume and no expiration. Second, Lennar disposed of its 33,298,754 Class A shares in the November 2025 exchange offer (Form 4 dated 2025-11-26, leaving 1,794 shares) while retaining all Founder's Rights, including the Pause Period Designation, Enforcement Rights, the Applicable Rate Adjustment (a most-favored-nation on option pricing) and Capital Priority. Lennar's incentives with respect to Millrose are now those of a customer and contractual counterparty; the 20% equity stake that previously internalized part of the cost of exercising those rights is gone. The Miller Family retains ~43% of total voting power via Class B at 10 votes per share, so family-level interests remain partially aligned. No pause has been designated and no opportunistic behavior has been observed — this is a change in incentive structure, not in conduct.

A share-count note: the $4.47B market capitalization in the data implies ~154.2M shares, which corresponds to the Class A count alone. With 166,047,927 total Class A plus Class B shares outstanding at June 30, total equity value at $28.98 is approximately $4.81B. Per-share metrics below use the full 166.0M count.

## Financial Analysis

**FY2025 (successor period).** Option fee revenue $571.0M, development loan interest $29.5M, total revenue $600.5M; management fee $87.8M; net income $379.9M; diluted EPS $2.44; AFFO $427.9M ($2.58/share); Invested Capital $8,470M; total assets $9,258.1M; equity $5,856.3M; debt ~$2,143M gross. The $2.44 EPS implies a ~$405M numerator against $379.9M of reported net income; the difference is attributed to pre-spin predecessor-period items excluded from successor EPS. I treat the line-by-line reconciliation as unverified and do not use $405M interchangeably with net income.

**Q2 / 1H 2026.** Q2 revenue $196.9M, net income $125.9M, EPS $0.76, AFFO $0.77/share; 1H revenue $391.8M, net income $248.8M, EPS $1.50, AFFO $1.53/share, dividends declared $1.53/share.

**Unit economics, recalculated from the June 30 balance sheet.** Gross asset yield 9.2% (MPA 8.5%, Other Agreements 10.6%). Annualized management fee $119.6M on $8,799M of Invested Capital = 1.36%, leaving a post-fee asset yield of ~7.84%. A levered ROE check: ($8,799M × 7.84% − $2,518M × ~6.5%) ÷ $5,852M = ($690M − $164M) ÷ $5,852M ≈ 9.0%, against observed Q2 net income annualized of $503.6M ÷ $5,851.8M = 8.6%. The two converge, which validates the framework and produces the key conclusion: the management fee consumes ~14.8% of the gross portfolio yield and ~24% of annualized net income, while leverage at 30% of capitalization contributes only about 1.2 points of ROE.

**Balance sheet.** Debt at June 30 was $2,518M (revolver $485M, up from $110M at year-end; $1.25B 2030 notes; $750M 2032 notes). The March 25, 2026 amendment made the credit facility unsecured — existing liens released — sized at a $1.335B revolver plus a $500M delayed-draw term loan with a $2.5B accordion. Undrawn capacity at June 30 was approximately $1.35B. Covenants include a leverage ratio with tiers referenced at 0.30/0.40, an interest coverage test and a minimum tangible net worth test; debt-to-capitalization of ~30.1% sits at the first referenced tier. Cash interest paid in 1H26 was $70.1M versus $40.0M of Q2 interest expense, reflecting semi-annual coupon timing — quarterly interest expense is the better run-rate input.

**Capital recycling.** The Q2 release reports $1.0B of net cash proceeds from homesite sales. Annualized against $8,799M of Invested Capital that implies roughly 45% asset turnover and a ~2.2-year average holding period. I present this as a sensitivity, not a forecast: a single quarter's proceeds vary with builder takedown seasonality (spring is the heaviest), community-level timing, and the mix between Lennar's shorter-cycle assets and newer, longer-dated Other Agreements. Note also that reported operating cash flow (roughly $3.67B in FY2025 against $600.5M of revenue) is not a profitability measure — land purchases and homesite proceeds both run through operating activities. AFFO and the change in Invested Capital are the economically meaningful measures.

**Financial health assessment (6.3).** Positives: moderate and unsecured leverage, $1.35B of committed undrawn capacity, $2.0B of fixed-rate term debt maturing 2030/2032, and roughly $1.0B per quarter of contractual asset liquidation validating carrying values. Negatives: a ~100% payout means zero internal capital formation, so every dollar of growth is debt-funded; leverage rose ~330bp in six months; management is openly reconsidering the ~33% ceiling; and stated book value has never been independently appraised, so the equity cushion is a cost-basis measure derived substantially from a related party.

## Growth Analysis

Growth is decelerating and structurally constrained. Invested Capital progressed $8,470M (12/31/25) → $8,706M (3/31/26) → $8,799M (6/30/26): +$329M, or +3.9% in 1H26 (~7.9% annualized). The dividend sequence — $0.38 → $0.69 → $0.73 → $0.75 → $0.76 → $0.77 — shows sequential increases of +82%, +5.8%, +2.7%, +1.3%, +1.3%. At ~1% per quarter, dividend growth is now consistent with the ~4%/half rate of Invested Capital growth and a full payout.

The mechanism running against growth is the housing chain. Per management commentary (sourced to an AI-generated call summary, treated as unconfirmed paraphrase), mortgage rates have been above 6% for a fourth consecutive year with elevated incentives, and the large public builders have guided 2026 deliveries lower. Fewer starts mean fewer new parcels brought to Millrose, which is what the 3.9% half-year figure reflects. Offsetting this, slower takedowns extend the fee-earning period on existing assets, so near-term revenue is more insulated than the builders' own. The asymmetry is that near-term revenue is cushioned while growth and terminal risk are not.

Against that, two new platforms could matter if they land on Millrose's balance sheet at scale:

- **Dream Finders / Beazer.** DFH agreed on August 7 to acquire Beazer for $2.2B in cash, creating the sixth-largest U.S. builder. Millrose's release the same day announced "a commitment by its manager to provide acquisition financing of up to $1.25 billion," with Millrose planning to "acquire homesites from the combined Dream Finders and Beazer organization." Sizing sensitivity: $1.25B would be ~14% of current Invested Capital and would nearly exhaust June 30 committed capacity. If deployed at the 10.6% Other Agreements yield, funded with debt at ~6.5%, net of the 1.25% fee, the spread is ~2.85% → ~$35.6M pre-tax, or roughly $0.21/share (+7% on $3.08 of AFFO). At a 7.25% marginal debt cost the accretion falls to about $0.15/share. Crucially, how much of the $1.25B becomes Millrose Invested Capital is not established: the commitment is attributed to the manager, Kennedy Lewis manages competing land-banking vehicles, and the 10-K states there is no assurance opportunities will be allocated to Millrose fairly. The benign reading — that the manager signs as agent in the ordinary course — is plausible and the release does not say otherwise. The entire commitment is conditional on the merger closing.
- **JPI multifamily facility** (July 30). First extension beyond single-family. Management characterized yields and structures as similar to existing agreements. Size, tenor and whether Millrose or another Kennedy Lewis vehicle holds the assets were not disclosed.

Consensus estimates drift modestly lower: current year $3.08 (−1.9% over 90 days), next year $3.21 (+1.6%), on coverage of only one to two contributing analysts. Next-year growth of ~4% is consistent with debt-funded portfolio growth net of a higher marginal cost of debt. This is not an inflection profile, and the estimate-revision signal is mildly negative rather than supportive.

**Growth assessment (5.2).** Mid-single-digit per-share growth at best, with the only non-dilutive funding lever being leverage that earns ~130bp of net spread on existing assets. Two genuine new platforms, both unsized and one conditional.

The peer table supplied (AMH 26.8x P/E, ELS 32.1x, INVH 31.0x; median 31.0x, median yield 4.0%) is not a usable comparison. Those are rental platforms that compound through rent growth and asset appreciation with low payout ratios; Millrose is a full-payout vehicle earning a contractual spread on a self-liquidating asset pool it does not develop. The relevant comparison set is levered specialty finance: BDCs at roughly 0.85–1.0x NAV yielding 10–12%, and mortgage REITs at 0.75–0.9x book yielding 11–14%. MRP at 0.82x book and 10.6% sits inside that band — mid-range, arguably slightly rich versus mREITs given the concentration and governance defects, but with better asset characteristics (contractual takedown prices, no mark-to-market duration book).

The cleanest way to frame the price is as a required yield. At $28.98 the market demands ~10.6% on a vehicle earning ~8.7% ROE — you buy the ROE at 0.82x book, which converts to a 10.6% earnings yield on cost. Whether 10.6% is the right required yield is the whole question. Arguments that it is roughly right: 72% of revenue and 74% of forward development commitments tied to one counterparty; that counterparty holds a right to halve its option payment rate for up to two six-month periods (worth ~$126M, or ~$0.76/share, per six-month designation on the current MPA balance); Enforcement Rights that make disputing Lennar economically irrational; a most-favored-nation pricing right that structurally limits what Millrose can charge new counterparties; an asset-based external fee that diverts ~15% of gross yield with a substantial termination fee protecting it; a book value never independently appraised; and no compounding from retained earnings. Arguments for compression toward ~10%: non-Lennar Invested Capital reached $2,850M (32.4%), counterparties reached 19, no options were abandoned in 1H26, and the credit facility improved materially in March.

I set base fair value at **$31.00** — 0.88x June 30 book, 10.1x annualized AFFO, a 9.9% forward yield — which corresponds to modest compression in required yield as the non-Lennar mix advances and the DFH/Beazer homesite program begins deploying. That is +7.0% from spot, plus roughly $3.08 of dividends over twelve months, for ~17.6% total. Bull case $35.50 (1.01x book, 8.7% yield) requires the non-Lennar mix to cross ~50% of Invested Capital plus some long-end relief. Bear case $23.50 (0.67x book) requires a pause designation, disclosed option abandonment or impairment, or a dividend cut. Probability-weighting 25/50/25 gives roughly +4% expected price change and, after haircutting the dividend in the bear branch, ~13–15% expected total return against a ~10.5% required return. Positive, but the margin is thin relative to the unresolved verification gaps.

On the sell side: five analysts, all Buy or Strong Buy, targets $35–$40 with a $37.60 mean. The lowest target is 20.8% above spot. That coverage set has not marked to the derating, contributes only one to two estimates, and is revising downward, so I treat these targets as optimistic anchors rather than independent confirmation. The condition under which analyst targets function as floors — a cheap stock with rapidly rising estimates — is not present here; estimates are drifting lower.

**Valuation assessment (5.8).** Fair to modestly cheap for the risk profile, with a covered double-digit yield providing genuine absolute support, but no identified mispricing large enough to be asymmetric. A low multiple attached to a structurally capped payout is exactly the profile that requires a named catalyst; the two candidate catalysts here (DFH/Beazer and JPI) are real but unsized and, in one case, conditional.

## Competitive Landscape

The structural tailwind is observable: public builders continue shifting toward land-light balance sheets, controlling lots via options rather than owning them to raise inventory turns and returns on capital. Millrose is the only public pure-play vehicle serving that shift, and its permanent-capital structure is a genuine differentiator against finite-life private land banks — a builder can be promised funding continuity through a downturn that a fund with a defined investment period cannot offer. The DFH/Beazer arrangement extends the model to financing industry consolidation (Millrose's third such instance since founding), and JPI extends it to multifamily.

Three constraints qualify the moat. First, the Applicable Rate Adjustment Right lets Lennar reset its own option rate downward to match any lower rate Millrose grants a new counterparty. With Other Agreements already priced at 10.6% versus Lennar's 8.5%, Millrose's incentive is to keep non-Lennar pricing high, which limits how aggressively it can win share — the binding competitive constraint is contractual rather than market-based. Second, the 10-K concedes Millrose competes against private funds with longer operating histories, including land-banking vehicles managed by other subsidiaries of its own manager, with deal flow governed by an allocation policy that carries no fairness assurance. Third, Kennedy Lewis "does not have expertise as a homebuilder or real estate developer," which matters precisely in the scenario where options are abandoned and Millrose must have homes built on its behalf or sell to third parties.

Trajectory is mildly improving on mix (Lennar from 88% of FY2025 option fee revenue to 72% of Q2 revenue; 19 counterparties; $2.85B non-Lennar), but a falling revenue share does not establish that concentration risk has fallen proportionately — 74% of forward development commitments remain Lennar-related, and forward commitments are the better indicator of where capital is contractually destined. **Competitive position: 5.8.**

## Risk Assessment

**Lennar concentration with no offsetting equity interest.** 72% of Q2 revenue, 74% of ~$6.9B of forward development commitments, 67.6% of Invested Capital. No exclusivity, right of first refusal, first look or minimum volume; Lennar is free to use other land bankers. Since November 2025 Lennar holds no Millrose equity while retaining every Founder's Right.

**Pause Period Designation — the most quantifiable lever.** Lennar may suspend horizontal development and option windows for two periods of up to six months each and reduce its monthly option payment rate to 50%. Sensitivity: MPA Invested Capital $5,949M × 8.5% = $505.7M of annualized Lennar option fees; a 50% rate cut for six months forgoes ~$126M, or ~$0.76/share — roughly one quarter's dividend — with no offsetting expense relief because the management fee is asset-based. Assumes the full MPA portfolio is subject and balances hold. No pause has been designated.

**Enforcement Rights.** If Millrose fails to convey homesites after an exercised option and does not cure within ten days, Lennar may immediately stop all monthly option payments across all Lennar-covered properties without penalty. Millrose must convey first and litigate after. This makes disputing Lennar economically irrational.

**Book value is not appraisal-supported.** The 10-K states no independent appraisals or fairness opinions were obtained for the real estate, including spin-off and Rausch assets; Millrose relied on Lennar for spin-off asset values; Phase I environmental reports were not obtained on all assets. The 17.8% discount to book that anchors the bull case is a discount to a related-party cost basis. The mitigant is real: assets turn roughly every two years at contractual prices, and the 10-Q's disclosure that no options were terminated, forfeited or expired without exercise in 1H26 means takedowns are currently validating carrying value more directly than an appraisal would.

**Option abandonment.** Counterparties have no obligation to exercise. Remedies are to request the builder construct homes on Millrose's behalf (which the builder "cannot unreasonably deny") or to sell to third parties at prices the 10-K concedes "may be significantly less than the amounts of our investments." Pooling and cross-termination provisions deter selective abandonment, but pool composition was chosen solely by Lennar before Millrose had independent management, and adverse events can impair an entire pool.

**Manager conflicts, stated by the company.** The fee structure "may incentivize KL to recommend riskier or more speculative investments" and to "use higher leverage"; KL maintains a contractual, not fiduciary, relationship; competing Kennedy Lewis land-banking vehicles exist; termination without cause triggers a substantial fee; internalization would forfeit the technology platform.

**Growth box.** Third-party financing that would push combined debt-to-equity above 1:1 requires Lennar's consent. With ~100% payout and a share price below book, retained earnings are nil, debt is capped, and equity issuance is dilutive to book value per share.

**Interest-rate asymmetry (unresolved).** $2.0B of notes are fixed. A 10-Q footnote states the weighted average yield calculation "assumes SOFR rate as of March 27, 2026," implying some asset yields float. If a material share of option/loan yields floats against fixed coupons, falling short rates would compress spread. The alternative reading is that the SOFR reference applies only to the ~5%-of-revenue development loan book and the revolver, in which case the effect is immaterial. The floating share is not established. Separately, marginal debt for the DFH/Beazer and JPI programs may price above the ~6.5% blended cost, compressing the incremental leverage spread just as management raises the leverage ceiling.

**Governance and controls.** Dual class with Class B at 10 votes and a 35% voting floor; Miller Family ~43% of total voting power; Stuart Miller is Executive Chairman and CEO of Lennar; Class B veto over mergers and charter amendments. First-time SOX 404(b) auditor attestation applies to the FY2026 10-K, and the manager has no prior public-company management experience.

**Risk assessment (4.6).** Several of these are partially priced by the 0.82x book and 10.6% yield, but the binary levers — a pause designation, a first option abandonment, an adverse 404(b) outcome — are unpriced and asymmetric. The marginal improvement from 1H26's clean option record and the unsecured facility does not change the category.

### Options Market Signal

The chain is thin (31 contracts across four expirations), so read it lightly. Near-term ATM IV of 26.2% against 27.3% one-year realized is fair to marginally cheap, at the 19th percentile within the current chain, and ATM IV has fallen 5.3pp since July 22 — a routine post-earnings vol crush rather than a directional signal. Term structure is flat (26.2% near, 26.7% far), so uncertainty is evenly distributed with no dated catalyst priced. The 13.6% one-month put skew reads as elevated put demand, but it sits against a put/call volume ratio of 0.20 and a put/call open-interest ratio of 0.10, down from 0.40 — there is very little hedging in open interest, which contradicts the skew reading and argues the skew reflects thin quoting rather than positioning. Short interest is 6.0% of float with 4.2 days to cover: moderate and uncrowded. Nearest-expiry implied move of ±5.1%. Net takeaway: the options market is not corroborating either a bullish re-rating or a bearish break, which is consistent with the fair-value HOLD.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case — $35.50 (+22.5% price, ~33% total return with dividends)

The 10.6% yield is fully covered by AFFO and is earned on monthly contractual option fees that continue whether or not builders take lots down this quarter, so revenue is more insulated than the builders' own earnings in a fourth consecutive year of 6%+ mortgage rates. Non-Lennar Invested Capital has reached $2.85B (32.4%) across 19 counterparties, and two new platforms — DFH/Beazer and JPI multifamily — could add roughly $0.15–0.21 per share of AFFO per $1.25B deployed while cutting Lennar dependence toward 50%. Zero option terminations or forfeitures in 1H26 validate carrying values against actual takedowns, which is the strongest available answer to the unappraised-book objection. The CEO bought 235,000 shares for ~$6.4M at $27.31 in May, below stated book, in a structure where he receives no company equity grants; across the 25 most recent Form 4s there were no open-market sales. The March 2026 move to an unsecured $1.335B revolver plus a $500M delayed-draw term loan with a $2.5B accordion is a genuine credit-quality upgrade. At 0.82x a cost basis that turns over every ~2.2 years at contractual prices, a move to 1.0x book plus the yield is a mid-30s total return.

### Bear Case — $23.50 (−18.9% price, ~−11% total return after a reduced dividend)

The single counterparty supplying 72% of revenue and 74% of forward development commitments now holds no Millrose equity and retains the right to halve its option payment rate for up to two six-month periods (~$0.76/share per designation), to match down any concession Millrose grants a competitor, and to stop all payments if Millrose ever fails to convey within ten days of an exercised option. With ~100% payout, a sub-book share price and a 1:1 debt cap requiring Lennar's consent, there is no path to per-share compounding except leverage that earns ~130bp of net spread — and management is raising the leverage ceiling. Invested Capital growth of 3.9% in 1H26 was fully debt-funded and dividend growth has decelerated to +1.3% per quarter. A housing downturn deep enough to trigger option abandonment leaves Millrose owning land it cannot develop, managed by a firm the 10-K says lacks homebuilder expertise, with a book value no third party has ever appraised. The FY2026 404(b) attestation is a first-time test for an inexperienced manager, and the $1.25B DFH/Beazer commitment is attributed to that manager, not to Millrose, with Millrose's own share undisclosed and conditional on the merger closing.

### Trajectory Assessment

This cycle the decline in scores stops rather than continues, and the components move in opposite directions for identifiable reasons. Confirming improvements: Q2 delivered AFFO and dividend coverage in line, portfolio yield held at 9.2%, zero option terminations, counterparties to 19, non-Lennar capital to 32.4%, unsecured facility with $1.35B undrawn, and two new deployment platforms. Offsetting deterioration: dividend growth decelerating to +1.3%/quarter, debt-to-capitalization up ~330bp in six months against flat equity, an openly reconsidered leverage ceiling, and consensus drifting −1.9% for the current year. The price is 1.4% lower than mid-July on essentially unchanged fundamentals, which improves the entry marginally without changing the structural cap on total return. Net overall score unchanged at 5.6, with the recommendation held at HOLD because the binding constraint — a ~100% payout that makes the yield the return — has not moved.

## Conclusion

**HOLD — income rationale. Overall score 5.6.**

Millrose is a clean, transparent spread business: 9.2% gross portfolio yield, a 1.36%-of-assets manager fee, ~6.5% cost of debt, ~$1.1M of residual quarterly cost, ~8.7% ROE, and a 10.6% covered dividend at 0.82x stated book. Q2 2026 delivered on the model — a sixth consecutive dividend increase, stable yield, $1.0B of homesite sale proceeds, and, most importantly, no option agreements terminated, forfeited or expired without exercise in the first half. That last disclosure is the best available answer to the objection that book value has never been independently appraised, because it means the carrying basis is being validated at contractual takedown prices roughly every two years.

The reason this is not a BUY is arithmetic rather than any deterioration. Dividends declared per share in 1H26 exactly equalled AFFO per share. With no retained earnings, a share price below the $35.24 book value making equity issuance dilutive per share, and a 1:1 debt-to-equity ceiling that requires Lennar's consent, the only non-dilutive growth lever is leverage earning ~130bp of net spread — just as management reconsiders the leverage ceiling upward. Total return absent a re-rating is therefore approximately the yield plus ~1% per quarter of decelerating dividend growth. A re-rating toward book requires the market to lower its required yield on a vehicle where one counterparty supplies 72% of revenue and 74% of forward development commitments, holds a right to halve its payment rate for up to a cumulative year, holds a most-favored-nation right on option pricing, and no longer owns a single share of the equity. Expected total return of roughly 13–15% against a ~10.5% required return is positive but not asymmetric, and the two candidate catalysts — up to $1.25B tied to DFH/Beazer and an unsized JPI multifamily facility — are attributed in part to the manager rather than to Millrose, with the former conditional on a merger closing.

Existing holders should keep collecting a fully covered 10.6% yield. New capital should wait for either a better entry near $26.50 or, better, a filing that quantifies what share of the DFH/Beazer program actually lands on Millrose's balance sheet.
