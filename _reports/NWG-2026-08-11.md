## Executive Summary

NatWest delivered the strongest set of UK large-bank numbers of the H1 2026 season: total income £8.86bn (+11%), net interest income £6.89bn (+12.6%), operating profit before tax £4.3bn (+20.4%), attributable profit £3.0bn, EPS 38.1p (+23.3%), net interest margin 2.48% (+20bps), cost:income of 46.0% and return on tangible equity of 19.7%. Q2 pretax profit of £2.29bn beat company-compiled consensus of £2.04bn by 12%. Guidance was raised for the second consecutive quarter: FY2026 income to ~£17.9bn from a £17.2–17.6bn range, RoTE to >19%, capital generation to >240bps. The next buyback announcement was pulled forward six months to the February 2027 annual results. The interim dividend rose 26% to 12.0p. Three of the four 2028 targets set in February are already met or exceeded on H1 actuals, which is why Jefferies described those targets as "increasingly prudent."

The market has largely paid for this. The ADS closed at $19.19, within 1.2% of its 52-week high, after a 33% twelve-month advance, with the ordinary shares at their highest level since 2008. On derived tangible book (H1 average attributable profit of £6.0bn annualised divided by the 19.7% RoTE gives ~£30.5bn of average tangible equity, or ~387p per share on ~7.87bn shares) the stock trades at roughly 1.83x average TNAV, and higher on spot TNAV because Evelyn Partners' intangibles landed on 30 June. Dividing 19.7% RoTE by 1.83x book implies the market is discounting these returns at approximately a 10.8% cost of equity. That is a reasonable price for durable 19% returns and an expensive one if the returns are cyclical. The historic UK bank trade of buying below tangible book and waiting for the Treasury to sell has fully played out; HM Treasury exited during 2025.

Two things keep this a HOLD rather than a REDUCE. First, absolute earnings valuation remains undemanding: consensus of $1.95 per ADS for FY2026 and $2.17 for FY2027 puts the shares on 9.8x and 8.8x, with estimates for both years revised up ~3% in the last 30 days. Second, the cash return is large and mostly contractual: an estimated ~5% forward dividend yield on a stated ~50% payout, plus buybacks funded from capital generation that the company guides above 240bps pre-distribution. Two things keep it from being ACCUMULATE: the Q2 beat came disproportionately from a 13bps impairment charge that management itself declined to extrapolate (impairment guidance was left unchanged at <25bps while every other line was raised), and a UK bank windfall surcharge (unenacted, politically live, and made rhetorically cheaper by record profits) carries roughly 1.4% of attributable earnings per incremental percentage point.

## Company Overview

NatWest Group is an Edinburgh-headquartered UK banking group serving more than 20 million customers through three reporting businesses: Retail Banking (CEO Solange Chamberlain), Private Banking & Wealth Management (Coutts & Wealth, CEO Emma Crystal), and Commercial & Institutional (CEO Robert Begbie). Group CEO is Paul Thwaite; CFO Katie Murray; Group Treasurer Donal Quaid.

Scale as at 30 June 2026: customer assets and liabilities £986.9bn; customer deposits ~£448bn; assets under management and administration £130.6bn; total liquidity portfolio £224.6bn (£152bn primary, £72.6bn secondary); wholesale funding £93bn; loan-to-deposit ratio 90%; mortgage stock share 12.7%.

The economically important structural feature is the liability side: deposits are over 80% of group funding at a 90% loan-to-deposit ratio. That is a low-cost, short-duration funding base and it is the mechanical source of the high returns. NIM of 2.48% on a largely UK mortgage and corporate asset book only produces a 19.7% RoTE because the funding is cheap and the cost base is thin.

A methodological note: NatWest is a UK foreign private issuer and files Form 20-F (most recently 17 February 2026) and Form 6-K, not 10-K/10-Q. Standardised quantitative screens (Altman Z, Piotroski F, XBRL series) return nothing for this issuer, and the underlying 20-F text was not retrievable in this research pass. Those absences are filing-form artifacts, not signals; Altman Z and Piotroski are in any case conceptually inapplicable to a deposit-funded balance sheet where liabilities are the product. The practical consequence is real: no primary risk-factor text was read, so I cannot identify which risk factors were added or modified in the February 2026 filing. That is a stated gap in this report's evidence base.

The same structure means no Form 4s exist. US insider-transaction screens on NWG are empty by construction; the UK equivalent is the PDMR notification (one was released on 5 August 2026 and was not read). No inference in either direction should be drawn from the absence of insider data.

The quarterly cadence corroborates the half-year figures rather than masking a lumpy quarter: Q2 operating profit £2.3bn was up 12.4% on Q1, Q2 income ex-notables of £4.4bn up 5.4% on Q1, and Q2 cost:income of 45.5% a full point better than Q1. Implied Q1 operating profit of £2.05bn plus Q2's £2.3bn reconciles to the £4.3bn half-year figure.

Tax and the earnings bridge reconcile at a 26.0% effective rate, consistent with 25% UK corporation tax plus the existing 3% banking surcharge on profits above the £100m allowance, giving ~£3.18bn post-tax, less ~£0.18bn of AT1 coupons, arriving at the £3.0bn attributable to ordinary shareholders. One press outlet reported "pretax profit for the six months was £3.18bn," which appears to be the post-tax figure mislabelled; I treat £4.3bn operating profit before tax as load-bearing because it is internally consistent with the quarterly build.

This is a strong balance sheet, and the securities portfolio composition helps: 69% is held at FVOCI and 31% at amortised cost, so roughly two-thirds is already marked, limiting the hidden-loss problem that afflicted banks with heavy amortised-cost books in 2023. The corollary matters given the current rate environment: with only ~20bps of CET1 headroom over the 13.0% target, adverse long-end moves flow into CET1 through OCI rather than sitting invisible in a held-to-maturity bucket.

Capital generation deserves care. H1 CET1 generation of 197bps was flattered by 31bps from RWA management; organic lending growth consumed 61bps and investment spend 19bps, giving management's 137bps net figure. Annualising 197bps without deducting lending growth overstates distributable capital by roughly 45%. The FY2026 guide of ">240bps before distributions" is on the gross basis and explicitly excludes Evelyn's capital cost, a definitional carve-out that flatters the headline. Even so, working from a CET1 ratio of 13.2% and an estimated (not disclosed) RWA base of £190–215bn, 240bps equates to roughly £4.6–5.2bn, or about 8–9% of the ~£55.9bn derived market capitalisation, generated in a single year before distributions.

Three dated obligations sit in the next four quarters: Basel 3.1 on 1 January 2027 with a management-assumed ~€10bn RWA uplift (~56bps of CET1 on the estimated RWA base, material but under a quarter of guided generation); £5.2bn of TFSME repayable in March 2027; and a £1bn AT1 call in May 2027, after which the Treasurer expects capital issuance to be "quite light." A first NatWest Bank covered bond since 2024 was issued in June, with a second signalled for H2 and possible Q4 pre-financing.

### Earnings quality — the central caveat

Jefferies' post-results read is the one to internalise: "clean" pre-provision profits were **in line**, and NIM was "fractionally light." Q2 income beat consensus by only 2% while pretax beat by 12%. The gap is impairments. The Q2 charge of £140m equals 13bps, with H1 at 19bps and total post-model adjustments of £316m, roughly 7.5bps of cover on a loan book of approximately £420bn. Management raised income, cost, capital and returns guidance and left impairment guidance unchanged at <25bps, which is at least consistent with viewing 13bps as unsustainable.

The mechanism is straightforward: normalisation to a through-cycle 30–35bps costs roughly £0.5–0.7bn of pretax profit on the same loan base, or 6–8% of pretax. Management stated it saw "no new signs of stress across our 3 businesses," and a decade of balance-sheet repair may genuinely have produced a structurally lower-loss book. Both readings are live. What is not in dispute is that the earnings base carries negative convexity to UK unemployment with a thin overlay behind it.

## Growth Analysis

Gross customer lending grew £9.7bn in Q2. The composition matters more than the total. Commercial & Institutional added £5.7bn (+3.6%), concentrated in large corporates where management attributed demand to "digitization and decarbonization," with mid-market demand from manufacturing and social housing. Retail and Private Banking added £4.0bn (+1.7%), of which mortgages were £3.9bn and unsecured just £0.1bn. Deposits grew £2.8bn, of which C&I contributed £2.5bn; retail deposits were flat with continued migration into fixed and variable-rate ISAs.

So 59% of Q2 lending growth came from the corporate book, retail deposits did not grow, and the UK consumer added essentially no unsecured borrowing. The earnings trajectory is more sensitive to the corporate capex cycle than the "20 million retail customers" framing implies. Management's alternative explanation of ISA-season timing and rate-driven mix migration rather than consumer weakness is plausible, but the mix shift into ISAs itself compresses deposit spread even when balances hold.

Excluding Evelyn, customer assets and liabilities grew £23.5bn, or 2.6%, in H1, roughly 5.2% annualised, comfortably above the >4% 2028 target. The £95.2bn headline CAL increase is about 75% acquisition arithmetic and should not be read as organic momentum. Mortgage stock share rose to 12.7% with record applications in March, which is share gain in a competitive market rather than balance-sheet drift.

The wealth pivot is the strategic answer to the "what happens when rates fall" question. Evelyn Partners completed on 30 June at £2.7bn enterprise value, adding £71.7bn of AUMA and taking group AUMA to £130.6bn, with ~£275m of H2 income and a £100m run-rate cost synergy target. Fee income is capital-light and less rate-sensitive than NII. Whether it was bought well is a separate question, addressed under management.

Consensus supports continued earnings growth without requiring heroics: $1.95 per ADS for FY2026 and $2.17 for FY2027, implying ~11% growth, with 30-day revisions of +3.2% and +3.1% respectively. Coverage of the ADS is thin (one contributing estimate on the revision table; four on the recommendation table), so these should be treated as directional confirmation rather than a robust consensus. The four-quarter surprise record is three beats and one narrow miss (Q2 2026, −3.2%).

The supplied data carries two internal inconsistencies. The provider's trailing/forward P/E field of 19.39 equals the last trade price and is unusable. The earnings-surprise table (quarterly actuals of $0.17–$0.20) appears denominated on a different basis from the estimate-revision table (~$0.52 per quarter, $1.95 per year, USD per ADS). The revision-table figures reconcile cleanly with H1 EPS of 38.1p per ordinary share (76.2p per ADS at a 2:1 ratio, ~$1.04 at a derived 1.365 USD/GBP, or ~$0.52 per quarter), which also independently corroborates the ADR ratio.

## Valuation Assessment

**Earnings basis.** At $19.19, the ADS trades at 9.84x FY2026 consensus of $1.95 and 8.84x FY2027 consensus of $2.17. A bottom-up cross-check from guidance: income ~£17.9bn less opex ~£8.5bn gives ~£9.4bn pre-provision; less impairments of ~£0.8–1.05bn (19–25bps on ~£420bn) gives ~£8.4–8.6bn pretax; at the derived 26% effective rate, ~£6.3bn post-tax; less ~£0.4bn of AT1 coupons, ~£5.9bn attributable; on ~7.87bn shares, ~75p per ordinary share, or ~$2.05 per ADS. Top-down and bottom-up converge, which raises confidence in a high-single-digit forward multiple. On a normalised 30–35bps cost of risk, EPS would be 6–8% lower, putting the shares nearer 10.5x, still not demanding in absolute terms.

**Book basis.** This is where the cushion has gone. Annualised attributable profit of £6.0bn divided by 19.7% RoTE implies ~£30.5bn of average tangible equity, or ~387p per ordinary share, giving price/average TNAV of ~1.83x at 710p. Spot TNAV is lower because Evelyn's goodwill and intangibles were recognised on 30 June, so spot price/TNAV is plausibly above 1.83x. Both figures are derived, not disclosed, and I did not verify NatWest's RoTE denominator definition; if it uses a notional CET1 basis rather than actual tangible equity, the implied TNAV shifts. Peer context: Barclays and Lloyds also beat H1 2026 expectations, so this is a sector-wide re-rating in which NatWest sits at the premium end on the strength of a 46% cost:income ratio and a 19bps cost of risk.

**What the multiple implies.** 19.7% RoTE divided by 1.83x book equals a ~10.8% earnings yield on price. A bank compounding tangible book in the high single digits after distributions can sustain ~1.8x book indefinitely; a bank whose returns normalise toward its own 2028 target of >18% (or below it, once impairments normalise and Basel 3.1 inflates RWAs) cannot support much multiple expansion from here.

**Income support.** The provider-reported dividend yield of 3.30% appears to capture a single payment: 23p (the FY2025 final) doubled for the 2:1 ADS ratio and converted at ~1.365 gives ~$0.63, or 3.27% on $19.19. The FY2025 total of 32.5p per ordinary share equates to ~$0.89 per ADS, a 4.6% trailing yield. On the stated ~50% payout and ~75–76p of FY2026 EPS, a full-year dividend of roughly 38p implies ~$1.04 per ADS, or approximately a 5.3% forward yield, with buybacks on top. Treat this as an estimate: the payout ratio is a stated policy, not a declared number, and the interim is the only declared component.

**Analyst targets.** Mean $21.54, median $21.22, low $20.10, high $23.60; the low target sits 4.7% above spot, so the entire published range implies upside. Two qualifiers: coverage is thin, and the recommendation mix has softened over three months (from 2 strong buy / 1 buy to 1 strong buy / 2 buy / 1 hold). Targets on a name at 9.8x forward earnings can reasonably be read as lagging floors rather than ceilings; targets on a name at ~1.8x tangible book with a live fiscal risk are less reliably so. I weight them as corroboration, not evidence.

**Valuation score: 5.5.** Cheap on earnings, fair-to-full on book, with the re-rating leg of the thesis spent. There is no identified mispricing large enough to justify a high score, and the specific asymmetry that would create one, a lower regulatory CET1 target from the deferred Q4 2026 PRA work on domestic framework overlap, is unscheduled and explicitly not something management is encouraging investors to model.

## Competitive Landscape

NatWest competes with Lloyds, Barclays, HSBC UK, Santander UK and Nationwide. All three listed high-street peers reporting alongside NatWest beat H1 2026 expectations, and NatWest closed the season. The 2026 UK banking earnings upgrade cycle is therefore rate-driven and sector-wide, not idiosyncratic; the structural hedge reinvestment that drives it is the single most important determinant of UK bank NII durability and is entirely absent from my evidence base.

What appears company-specific is positioning rather than the cycle: a 46.0% cost:income ratio, a 19bps cost of risk, and 12.7% mortgage stock share taken with record March applications. Management's four-part claim of being "the most efficient large U.K. bank with the lowest cost of risk, delivering the strongest levels of capital generation and highest returns" is directionally consistent with the absolute figures but was not independently verified as a peer ranking on any of the four dimensions.

The wealth business is the second differentiator and the more contestable one. Post-Evelyn, £130.6bn of AUMA places NatWest as a top-tier bank-owned UK wealth platform but not the market's scale leader in a field containing St James's Place, Schroders, Rathbones, Quilter and the private arms of larger global groups. The strategic logic of capital-light, fee-based income counter-cyclical to NII is the correct answer to NatWest's structural rate exposure. Execution on the £100m synergy target determines whether it was the right price.

Trajectory across both engines is improving, with two qualifications: retail deposit mix migration into ISAs pressures spread even with flat balances, and C&I growth is concentrated in large corporates within themes ("digitisation") whose credit concentration is not publicly quantified.

## Risk Assessment

**1. UK bank taxation — the live asymmetric risk.** Externally reported and politically fluid, not a filing fact. Press reporting through July 2026 describes windfall-tax expectations, with the TUC modelling surcharge scenarios at 8% (£9bn over four years), 16% (£24bn) and 35% (£60bn), and CEO Thwaite publicly resisting ("You need strong banks") on the same day NatWest reported record profits. **No tax change has been enacted**, and I could not verify from any official source the identity of the current UK Prime Minister or Chancellor; those characterisations come from secondary press only and should not be treated as established.

The sensitivity is calculable. NatWest's blended effective rate of ~26% is consistent with 25% corporation tax plus the existing 3% surcharge. On ~£8.5bn of guided pretax profit, each incremental percentage point of surcharge is roughly £85m, or ~1.4% of attributable earnings. A move from 3% to 8% is roughly −7% to earnings; to 16%, roughly −18%. These are upper bounds, because the surcharge applies to UK banking profits above a £100m allowance rather than consolidated group pretax profit. The risk correlates positively with reported profitability: 19.7% RoTE, "highest profit since 2008," and post-crash-high bank bonuses are the headline set that makes a sector levy politically inexpensive. Resolution comes at the next UK Budget.

**2. Cost of risk at a cycle floor with thin cover.** 13bps quarterly, £316m of PMAs (~7.5bps of loans), and a 6–8% pretax sensitivity to through-cycle normalisation. Management stated it saw no new signs of stress, but guidance was not raised on impairments even as every other line was, which is consistent with some normalisation over the forecast horizon.

**3. Unquantified data-centre and AI-related credit exposure.** Asked directly on the H1 call for data-centre exposure as a percentage of the balance sheet, the CFO said "it's not a separate classification that we pull out," directed the analyst to Pillar 3 page 34, and said "it's not in itself a meaningful number as part of the total bank," while describing underwriting focused on "long-term contracted assets with… strong operators." The mundane explanation is almost certainly correct: banks do not maintain "data centre" as a reporting segment, and SIC-code tables are the standard disclosure route. I am not inferring concealment. The finding is that the exposure is unknown to an outside investor at a moment when AI-related credit concentration is a defining market concern, and it sits inside the fastest-growing part of the loan book. An investor should read Pillar 3 page 34 directly.

**4. Motor finance redress — peer risk, NatWest exposure unquantified.** The FCA published PS26/3 on 30 March 2026 establishing an industry redress scheme reported at ~£9.1bn; parts were suspended in July 2026 pending legal challenges with proceedings expected in December 2026. Peer provisions include Santander UK £461m and FirstRand $993m. I found no NatWest-specific provision, an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence. NatWest's consumer motor footprint is plausibly small relative to Lloyds/Black Horse, Santander and Close Brothers, which would make this a relative advantage, but December 2026 is a dated catalyst that could re-open scheme scope for all lenders.

**5. Evelyn integration and price.** £2.7bn of enterprise value consumed roughly 132bps of CET1 on the estimated RWA base (approximately the whole half's net capital generation) and leaves ~20bps of headroom over the 13.0% target. £2.7bn for £71.7bn of AUMA is ~3.8% of assets under management, at or above the top of the 1–3% band at which UK wealth transactions have more commonly cleared. On earnings, ~£550m annualised revenue gives EV/revenue ~4.9x; at an assumed (unverified) 25–30% standalone margin plus the £100m synergy target, EV/EBITDA is ~10–11x post-synergy and ~16–19x without. Synergy delivery is the whole valuation case for the deal.

**6. Regulatory capital reform — currently neutral, with deferred optionality.** Following the Bank of England's July 2026 Financial Stability Report, the Group Treasurer stated "there has been no change to our capital requirements to date." Leverage reform would cut NatWest's minimum ~40bps to 3.9% but brings "no day 1 benefit… given the risk weight framework is our binding constraint"; buffer usability "will probably evolve over a number of years"; O-SII releasability has "no impact on the way we manage capital or our CET1 target." The genuine upside, "the overlapping elements of the domestic capital framework… that has been deferred to Q4," would mechanically release capital for distribution if it lowered the CET1 target. Do not model a CET1 target reduction from the leverage or O-SII changes; management has been explicit.

**7. Items I could not assess.** Structural hedge reinvestment profile; conduct and litigation beyond motor finance; operational resilience and cyber; ring-fencing reform; pension risk; and any risk factors added or modified in the February 2026 20-F.

Short interest is 0.0% of float (1.31m shares, 0.2 days to cover): no crowding, no squeeze dynamic, and no informed bearish positioning visible. No options data was available for this security, so no options-market signal is presented.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull case — $24.50 (+28%)

No UK surcharge increase materialises; the current 13bps cost of risk proves to be franchise quality rather than deferred loss, holding H2 impairments near the low end of the <25bps guide. Evelyn's £100m of synergies land on schedule, adding fee income that reduces the sensitivity of the earnings base to the rate cycle and supports a higher multiple on the same book. The Q4 2026 PRA consultation on domestic framework overlap advances toward a lower CET1 requirement, freeing capital and enabling a larger-than-expected buyback at the February 2027 results. FY2027 EPS reaches ~$2.30 per ADS on continued organic CAL growth above 4% and further mortgage share gains, and the shares hold ~10.7x — $24.50, close to the $23.60 published high target.

### Bear case — $14.50 (−24%)

A UK Budget surcharge increase of 5pp cuts earnings ~7%; UK unemployment drifts and cost of risk normalises to 32–35bps, costing a further 6–8% of pretax; Basel 3.1 removes ~56bps of CET1 on 1 January 2027 against ~20bps of headroom over target, forcing the buyback to be trimmed rather than upsized. Evelyn synergies slip, leaving the deal at ~16–19x EBITDA and prompting the market to reprice management's acquisition discipline. On ~$1.90 of FY2027 EPS at 7.6x, or ~1.4x spot tangible book, the ADS trades to $14.50, roughly where it sat in March 2026, when it briefly traded at $13.89.

### Base case — $21.50 (+12%)

Guidance is met: FY2026 income ~£17.9bn, cost:income ~46%, RoTE >19%, impairments toward the middle of the <25bps range as the 13bps quarterly rate drifts up. FY2027 EPS reaches consensus of $2.17 per ADS. At 9.9x, unchanged from today's multiple so no re-rating assumed, that is $21.50, between the mean ($21.54) and median ($21.22) published targets. Add an estimated ~5% dividend for a ~17% twelve-month total return in the base case.

**Probability-weighted:** 50% base (+12%), 25% bull (+28%), 25% bear (−24%) gives an expected price return of roughly +7%, or approximately +12% including the estimated dividend. That is a positive expected return with a bear case 24% below spot: adequate to justify continued ownership for the cash return, insufficient margin of safety at ~1.8x spot tangible book to justify new capital at a 52-week high.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Recommendation: HOLD (income rationale).** Existing holders should keep the position for the ~5% estimated forward yield plus buyback, and reinvest dividends rather than adding at spot. Reassessment points are the Q3 2026 results on **30 October 2026** and the FY2026 results on **12 February 2027**, when the next buyback is announced.

**Upgrade triggers (to ACCUMULATE or BUY):**
1. A UK Budget that passes without an increase in the banking surcharge, removing the largest unpriced earnings risk. On unchanged fundamentals this alone would justify moving to ACCUMULATE.
2. A pullback to **$16.50–17.50** (roughly 1.55–1.65x estimated spot TNAV, ~8.0–8.5x FY2027 consensus), which would restore a double-digit margin of safety against the base case.
3. Q3 or FY2026 results showing pre-provision profit growth in line with or ahead of income growth (a beat that does not depend on impairment under-runs) combined with cost:income at or below 45% and confirmation that Evelyn synergies are tracking to the £100m target.
4. A Q4 2026 PRA outcome on domestic framework overlap that lowers NatWest's CET1 requirement, since that converts directly into distributable capital.

**Downgrade triggers (to REDUCE or AVOID):**
1. An enacted surcharge increase of 8pp or more (a ~−18% earnings event on the upper-bound sensitivity) without an offsetting derating already having occurred.
2. Quarterly cost of risk above 30bps, or any increase in the economic-uncertainty PMA above the current £284m, which would signal that the 13bps run-rate was a timing benefit rather than book quality.
3. CET1 falling below 13.0% without a stated path back, or the February 2027 buyback being deferred, reduced versus the £750m precedent, or funded by suspending the ~50% payout.
4. A NatWest-specific motor finance provision materially above peers, or a December 2026 legal outcome that expands scheme scope.
5. A further acquisition above ~£1bn announced before Evelyn's £100m synergy target is evidenced as delivered.

**Position management:** Trim on strength above $22.50, where the base case is fully discounted and the bear case is 35% below the price.

## Conclusion

NatWest is operationally the best-performing large UK bank in this cycle on the metrics that matter for a deposit-funded balance sheet: 46.0% cost:income, 19bps cost of risk, 19.7% RoTE, 13.2% CET1, 140% LCR and 90% loan-to-deposit ratio, with guidance raised twice in 2026 and three of four 2028 targets already achieved. Capital generation of 8–9% of market capitalisation per year before distributions is genuine, and Jefferies' description of free cash flow as a "standout" is independent corroboration.

The price has caught up. At $19.19 the ADS sits 1.2% below its 52-week high after a 33% twelve-month advance, at ~1.83x average and higher on estimated spot tangible book, with the ordinary shares at post-2008 highs. The re-rating from the Treasury-overhang discount is complete; what remains is a bet on the durability of ~19% returns. That bet is partly supported (9.8x FY2026 and 8.8x FY2027 consensus with rising estimates, ~5% estimated forward yield, buyback pulled forward to February 2027) and partly exposed: the Q2 beat came from impairments rather than pre-provision profit, the £316m overlay is thin at ~7.5bps of loans, Basel 3.1 arrives in January 2027 against ~20bps of CET1 headroom after the £2.7bn Evelyn deal, and a UK bank surcharge increase would cost roughly 1.4% of attributable earnings per percentage point at a moment when record profits make such a levy politically cheap.

Expected total return of roughly 12% over twelve months against a 24% bear case is a fair, not compelling, proposition. Own it for the cash return; wait for the Budget to clear or for a move into the $16.50–17.50 range before adding.
