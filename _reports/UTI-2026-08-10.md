## Executive Summary

Universal Technical Institute has spent FY2026 converting a high-return, cash-generative business into a cash-consuming expansion program, and the market repriced that decision sharply on 5 August. Nine-month revenue rose 7.8% to $661.2M while operating income fell 67% to $19.3M and net income fell 65% to $15.5M. Management cut FY2026 revenue guidance to $893–900M from $905–915M and guided net income to $32–36M against FY2025's actual $63.0M, adjusted EBITDA to $100–103M against $126.5M, and adjusted free cash flow to $(20)M–$0M. The shares fell 34.2% on 6 August and now sit 47% below the 8 July high of $51.34.

The central question is whether FY2026 margin compression is a self-funded, reversible pre-opening cost cycle or a structural increase in the cost of acquiring and financing students. The evidence cuts both ways. Supporting the reversible reading: UTI-division new starts rose 23.4% in Q3 and 14.4% over nine months, UTI-Atlanta's initial starts reportedly came in ~30% above internal expectation, and the profit decline is concentrated in the division doing the most campus opening (UTI segment nine-month operating income $25.8M versus $58.0M on the recast basis). Supporting the structural reading: advertising rose to 12.5% of revenue from 11.1% in *both* divisions simultaneously, the company is adding 20% more high-school admissions staff, tuition escalators have decelerated from +6.0% (FY2023) to +1.9% (FY2025) at the UTI segment, and the credit-loss provision has gone from 1.02% of revenue (FY2024) to 2.64% (FY2025) to 3.39% (9M FY2026). Provision growth does not reverse when campuses fill.

The call turns on valuation rather than the narrative. At $27.10 the stock is ~45x FY2026 consensus EPS of $0.60, ~37x FY2027 consensus of $0.73, and 24x FY2025 actual diluted EPS of $1.133, against an Education Services peer median P/E of 12.3x. On EV excluding capitalized leases the stock is 14.6x guided FY2026 adjusted EBITDA and 16.6x including the $199.4M of operating-lease liabilities. A complete restoration of FY2025 earnings power would still leave the shares at roughly twice peer multiples. Consensus EPS has been cut 22.2% for the current year and 13.7% for next year over 90 days — the opposite of the estimate-revision pattern that supports paying a premium. We rate the shares REDUCE with a base case of $23 (-15%), bull $36 (+33%) and bear $15 (-45%).

## Company Overview

UTI operates 34 campuses across two reportable segments. The **UTI segment** ran 16 campuses in nine states at 30 June 2026, reaching 17 in ten states with the July opening of UTI-Atlanta (Smyrna, GA; ~117,000 sq ft, designed for up to 1,500 students). Programs span automotive, diesel, collision, motorcycle, marine, welding, CNC, aviation, HVACR, electrical and robotics, with tuition from roughly $18,000 (Wind Power, 30 weeks) to roughly $69,000 (combined Automotive/Diesel, 90 weeks). Average annual revenue per UTI student was approximately $35,100 in FY2025. The **Concorde Career Colleges segment**, acquired December 2021, runs 18 allied-health campuses in eight states plus online, tuition from roughly $1,000 (Phlebotomy) to roughly $99,000 (Dental Hygiene), at approximately $30,000 average annual revenue per student.

Revenue is essentially all tuition and fees recognized ratably over program terms. On a cash basis, approximately 78% of FY2025 revenue came from Title IV programs and veterans' benefits — roughly 43% Direct Loans, 23% Pell, 11% VA. Individual institutions ranged from 67% to 82% federal funds against the 90% statutory cap. Headroom exists but is thin at the top of the range.

UTI functions as a subprime lender to its own students through three channels: a proprietary loan program (a single state-chartered bank originates, UTI purchases monthly and bears all credit and collection risk; 6–10% rates, terms to ten years, no repayment until six months post-completion; used by ~21% of UTI-segment active students), in-school cash payment plans (~49% of UTI-segment students), and Concorde retail installment contracts (~68% of Concorde students). Notes receivable totaled $52.1M at 30 June 2026 and are carried as Level 3 fair-value assets using collection assumptions derived from ten years of history that includes the federal payment pause.

Total operating expense rose to 97.1% of revenue from 90.5%, with educational services and facilities at 52.4% (from 50.3%) and SG&A at 44.7% (from 40.2%). The quarterly sequence within FY2026 — net income $12.8M → $0.4M → $2.3M — shows the compression arrived in Q2 and persisted.

Management attributes roughly $9.0M in Q3 and $27.6M over nine months to strategic growth expenses for campuses and programs launching over several years. That leaves roughly $11.6M of the $39.2M nine-month operating-income decline unattributed to growth spend in the MD&A language available. Two separately identifiable items cover most of the residual: the credit-loss provision rose $8.7M year over year, and stock-based compensation rose to $9.4M from $6.4M. The mechanism matters. Pre-opening cost at a campus that opens in 2027 is genuinely a timing item. A provision that has grown from 1.02% to 3.39% of revenue in two years is a repricing of collection assumptions on a loan book the company itself owns, and it does not reverse on utilization.

### Cash flow inverted

Nine-month operating cash flow fell to $17.4M from $40.2M while property and equipment purchases rose to $80.9M from $25.5M, plus $4.5M of capitalized intangibles. Nine-month free cash flow was therefore approximately negative $68.0M. Management's own guidance of $(20)M–$0M adjusted free cash flow for the full year corroborates the direction.

The operating cash flow decline is largely working capital rather than earnings quality: net income plus non-cash adjustments still generated $98.5M over nine months, with working capital absorbing $81.1M — receivables ($26.5M), deferred revenue ($20.8M), lease liabilities ($15.2M), prepaids ($11.7M), taxes ($7.1M), notes receivable ($4.4M). The deferred-revenue drawdown reflects where students sit in their programs at 30 June versus 30 September and is broadly consistent with the $25.5M drawdown in the prior-year period; it is not evidence of a booking collapse.

### Balance sheet: adequate, not comfortable

At 30 June 2026: cash $130.1M, short-term investments $40.1M, total debt $160.3M (revolver $95.0M at 5.47% maturing November 2027; Avondale term loan $26.8M; Lisle term loan $35.4M; finance lease $3.1M), producing net cash of approximately +$9.9M. Shareholders' equity $344.3M. PP&E net rose to $338.8M from $285.9M. Operating lease liabilities $199.4M present value on a 7.37-year weighted-average term, with approximately $50.1M of additional minimum payments on leases signed but not yet commenced.

Total liquidity fell $74.0M to $180.5M. At quarter-end the company had only $10.4M of revolver availability and $0.4M of letter-of-credit sub-facility capacity. In July it repaid the full $95.0M revolver with cash on hand and the Department of Education released the $19.6M letter of credit posted in July 2025, restoring the full $125.0M facility. Net leverage is unchanged by that sequence, but gross liquidity headroom is now roughly $75M of cash and investments plus an undrawn revolver.

Two industry-specific metrics matter more than conventional ratios. The ED composite score for FY2025 was 2.3 against a 1.5 threshold for financial responsibility — falling below triggers cash monitoring and letter-of-credit requirements, which is the channel through which weak profitability becomes a regulatory problem. The Altman Z-Score of 2.32 sits in the grey zone; reported debt-to-equity of 1.52 is dominated by capitalized leases, with financial debt to equity under 0.5x. All financial covenants were in compliance at 30 June 2026.

**Financial Health: 4.5.** Net cash positive, covenant compliant, ED score above threshold, and a restored revolver. Against that: 9M FCF of −$68M with guided negative full-year FCF, liquidity down $74M, an escalating provision on a self-held subprime loan book valued on Level 3 inputs, and $249M of committed and uncommenced lease obligations added ahead of revenue.

## Growth Analysis

Volume growth is real and better than the earnings suggest. Q3 FY2026 consolidated average full-time active students rose 5.8% to 25,131, total new starts rose 10.9% to 6,342, and end-of-period students rose 9.1% to 24,408. The UTI segment drove it: new starts +23.4% in Q3 and +14.4% over nine months. Concorde's starts fell 1.4% in Q3 (+4.2% over nine months) even as its end-of-period students rose 15.4% and revenue rose 11.1% — the stock of students is growing while the inflow has stalled, plausibly because twelve newly launched Concorde programs (radiologic technology, diagnostic sonography, surgical technology) are longer duration.

The capacity pipeline is aggressive. FY2026 execution: UTI San Antonio opened March 2026 (the first skilled-trades-only campus), UTI Dallas expanded by ~1,000 student capacity, UTI Atlanta opened July 2026, Concorde relocated Aurora to Denver. Announced for 2027, all pending regulatory approval: UTI Salt Lake City (~113,000 sq ft, 3,000+ students), Concorde Houston, Concorde Glendale AZ, Concorde Atlanta, and a North Hollywood-to-Burbank relocation. North Star Phase II contemplates multiple campuses per year through 2029 and 10–20 new programs annually.

Three things temper the growth case.

First, **growth is volume-only**. UTI-segment tuition increases decelerated from +6.0% (FY2023) to +3.0% (FY2024) to +1.9% (FY2025), below wage inflation over the period. The entire margin thesis therefore rests on utilization and cost control, with no pricing contribution. The FY2025 10-K separately carries a risk factor on underutilized capacity at existing campuses; the company is adding capacity while acknowledging it has not filled what it has.

Second, **the divergence between UTI starts (+14.4% nine months) and UTI revenue (+6.6%)** is unresolved. Ratable recognition over program length produces this mechanically when starts surge late in a period, but so does a mix shift toward shorter, cheaper skilled-trades programs. San Antonio is skilled-trades-only, with programs priced well below the $69,000 Automotive/Diesel combination. If mix is the driver, revenue per student falls permanently and the utilization math changes.

Third, **estimate revisions are moving the wrong way and moving fast**. Current-quarter consensus fell 37.1% over 30 days to $0.33; current year fell 22.2% to $0.60; next year fell 13.7% to $0.73. Rapid negative revisions of this magnitude are as informative on the downside as large positive revisions are on the upside, and they are the single most direct rebuttal to the argument that FY2026 is a discrete investment year with a clean FY2027 snap-back.

**Growth Prospects: 5.0.** Revenue and enrollment growth are durable and supported by a genuine secular reallocation of student demand toward skilled trades and allied health, reinforced by white-collar displacement from AI. Earnings growth has inverted, pricing power is eroding, and OBBBA federal loan limits threaten the funding available to students for $40,000–$99,000 programs.

A note on the supplied data: the profile lists trailing and forward P/E identically at 47.46x alongside EPS of $1.13. Those are inconsistent — $27.225 ÷ $1.13 = 24.1x. The 47.46x figure corresponds to EPS of roughly $0.57, consistent with FY2026 consensus. We use the reconciled set above.

Against peers, the premium is the decisive fact. The Education Services comparables show a median P/E of 12.3x: STRA 14.2x, PRDO 12.3x, LRN 11.6x, GHC 9.6x, LOPE 18.5x. UTI at 37.1x on FY2027 consensus trades at three times the peer median. Even granting a full recovery to FY2025 earnings power of $1.133 per share, the stock is at 23.9x, roughly twice the peer median and above the highest-multiple comparable (LOPE at 18.5x).

Work the normalization case forward explicitly. Suppose FY2027 revenue reaches ~$960M (+7% on the FY2026 guidance midpoint) and the operating margin recovers to FY2025's 10.0%, giving $96M of operating income. Less approximately $8M of net interest and 26% tax yields net income of roughly $65M, or $1.16 diluted. At 20x — a substantial premium to peers, justified by faster growth — that is $23. At 25x it is $29. To reach $36 on that earnings base requires roughly 31x, or requires margins meaningfully above the pre-expansion peak.

A longer-dated bull path: FY2029 revenue of $1.15B at an 18% adjusted EBITDA margin (versus FY2025's 15.1%) gives $207M; at 10x EV/EBITDA ex-leases that is $2.07B of enterprise value, or roughly $36–37 per share three years out. That is approximately a 10% annualized return from $27.10 before any allowance for the regulatory or credit tails, and it requires margins to exceed the prior peak by nearly 300 basis points while the current data shows acquisition cost and credit provisioning both rising.

The 14.6x EV/EBITDA on depressed FY2026 EBITDA is the strongest bull argument on a multiple basis. It becomes 11.7x if FY2027 merely returns to the FY2025 absolute EBITDA level. That is not expensive in isolation. It is expensive relative to a peer group trading at single-digit-to-low-teens P/Es, and it is expensive given negative free cash flow, no capital return, and a program-level regulatory testing regime being written now.

Analyst targets require an explicit caveat. The supplied consensus of $41.83 mean, $37 low and $48 high, and the 6 Buy/Strong Buy ratings with zero holds, all predate the 5 August guidance cut. Barrington's $42 target dates to 7 May 2026 and the $42.69 consensus average to 14 May 2026. No post-print revisions were located. These are stale inputs from before net income guidance was cut roughly 43–49% versus FY2025 actual, and they should not be treated as independent confirmation of value. This is the configuration where analyst targets are not floors: the stock is not cheap relative to improving estimates; estimates are falling.

**Valuation: 3.5.** No identifiable mispricing. The multiple embeds a full margin restoration plus continued premium rating, while revisions, free cash flow and the provision trend all point the other way.

## Competitive Landscape

Named competitors in the FY2025 10-K: Adtalem, American Public Education, Legacy Education, Lincoln Educational Services, Perdoceo, Strategic Education. Lincoln (LINC) is the closest analogue — the other large campus-based skilled-trades operator, also in an aggressive expansion cycle.

The durable competitive assets are physical and regulatory rather than commercial: 34 campuses with specialized labs and $338.8M of net PP&E, ED-recognized accreditation across 15 institutional OPE IDs (ACCSC for all UTI institutions and 13 Concorde institutions; COE for three), and state authorizations in every operating and recruiting state. These take years and regulator consent to replicate, and they are the reason a new entrant cannot simply outspend UTI on advertising.

Management's stated moat is its OEM relationship network — Mercedes-Benz DRIVE, Peterbilt PTI, Porsche PTAP, Tesla START Collision, dealer-technician training, instructor staffing. The FY2025 10-K materially qualifies that framing: relationships with American Honda, Mercury Marine (Brunswick), Volvo Penta and Yamaha Motor USA are verbal and unwritten, certain written manufacturer agreements are terminable without cause, and some expire within six months of filing. No OEM loss has been disclosed, and arrangements that have persisted for years with manufacturers dependent on UTI's dealer pipeline may be sticky in practice. But the contractual foundation is weaker than the Item 1 language implies.

Two trajectory signals argue the competitive position is flat to slightly deteriorating rather than improving. Pricing power has decelerated for three consecutive years. And student-acquisition cost is inflating in both divisions simultaneously — advertising rose from 11.1% to 12.5% of revenue over nine months, UTI segment 11.2% → 12.6% and Concorde 10.6% → 12.2%. When two divisions serving entirely unrelated end markets see acquisition cost rise in the same period, the likely driver is cross-cutting: competitive bidding for the same leads as LINC and others expand, a tight entry-level labor market reducing urgency to enroll (the 10-K notes enrollment "tends to be counter cyclical"), or unbranded new-market advertising. Management's own attribution is the third explanation, which is plausible for Atlanta, San Antonio and Salt Lake City. The Q3 disclosure of a multi-phase restructuring whose stated purpose includes "improve student acquisition," plus a 20% increase in high-school admissions headcount, suggests the funnel is the binding constraint.

**Competitive Position: 5.0.** Real, capital- and regulator-gated barriers; deteriorating price and acquisition-cost trajectory; contractually fragile OEM layer.

## Risk Assessment

**Regulatory reform is the dominant unpriced risk.** With approximately 78% of revenue from Title IV and VA on a cash basis, federal rulemaking is existential rather than incidental. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, enacted 4 July 2025, introduces a "Do No Harm" earnings benchmark comparing median completer earnings against working adults holding lesser credentials, with Title IV ineligibility for failing programs. UTI's entire value proposition is wage uplift versus a high-school diploma, and it is now to be measured on exactly that basis. OBBBA also imposes federal loan limits and reductions, which directly constrain student funding for $40,000–$99,000 programs and would push more of the burden onto UTI's own balance-sheet lending — a book already showing provision escalation. The RISE and AHEAD negotiated-rulemaking committees are writing implementing regulations with no visible resolution date. Separately, the Gainful Employment / Financial Value Transparency rule (effective 1 July 2024) tests programs on debt-to-earnings *and* an earnings premium; failing either in two of three years costs that program's Title IV eligibility. With dozens of programs across 34 campuses, the outcome is a distribution, and the longest, highest-tuition programs (Dental Hygiene ~$99,000, Automotive/Diesel ~$69,000) carry the most debt-to-earnings risk.

**Credit quality is opaque and probably understated.** Reported cohort default rates of 0% for the 2020, 2021 and 2022 cohorts fall entirely inside the COVID-era payment pause and should be disregarded as a credit indicator. Meanwhile the provision has risen from $7.5M (FY2024) to $22.1M (FY2025) to $22.4M in nine months of FY2026, and FY2025 net receivables grew 48.2% against 14.0% revenue growth. The proprietary loan program depends on a single small-cap state-chartered bank to originate and a single servicer terminable on 90 days' notice. A January 2022 CFPB letter regarding supervisory authority over UTI's student lending remains unresolved. A mitigating data point: nine-month FY2026 contract receivables including notes rose only 5.8% to $104.5M, below revenue growth, though the filing does not state whether that figure is gross or net of allowance.

**Contingent legal exposure is unquantified.** ED provided approximately 2,500 borrower-defense claims from former Concorde students in February 2024, all pre-acquisition, unadjudicated as of the FY2025 10-K. Concorde is one of 153 named schools in the Sweet settlement; claims filed between 22 June and 15 November 2022 are recoupment-eligible against the school. The 10-Q states no material legal proceedings, so there is no accrual and no disclosed range of loss.

**Execution and commitment risk.** Four campuses are slated for 2027 pending approvals, with approximately $50.1M of minimum payments on leases signed but not commenced over 11–15 years, on top of $199.4M of existing lease liabilities. Each campus is a long-dated fixed cost incurred well ahead of revenue. Several Concorde accreditations sit in "In Process" status with nominal expiry dates passed — routine in the sector, but a campus-level Title IV tail.

**Near-term guidance risk.** The FY2026 adjusted EBITDA guide is heavily back-end loaded. Reconstructing nine-month adjusted EBITDA as EBITDA plus stock compensation plus restructuring gives roughly $58.2M versus roughly $89.5M a year earlier, a ~35% decline. The full-year guide of $100–103M therefore requires Q4 of roughly $42–45M against roughly $37M in Q4 FY2025, on implied Q4 revenue of $231.8–238.8M versus $222.4M. That is 150–220bp of year-over-year Q4 margin expansion immediately following a 35% nine-month decline. Q4 is genuinely UTI's seasonal peak, and San Antonio, Dallas and Atlanta transitioning from pre-opening cost to revenue supports the case. But the reconstruction is an approximation of the company's undefined adjusted EBITDA measure — a build-up from the implied Q4 net income of $16.5–20.5M yields roughly $37–42M instead — so treat this as a sensitivity rather than a forecast. Either way, the bar is high.

**Governance items unresolved.** An 8-K dated 17 March 2026 under Item 5.05 (amendment to or waiver of a provision of the code of ethics) was not read. That specific item type warrants review before any upgrade.

### Options Market Signal

Positioning is defensively skewed while the price of protection is low. Put/call open-interest ratio is 6.67 and put/call volume ratio 3.79, the heaviest put tilt in any name we currently follow; the 38-day tenor shows a 7.69 put/call volume ratio and −4.0% skew. Near-term ATM implied volatility of 60.5% sits 11.2 points below one-year realized volatility of 71.7%, at the 30th percentile of the current chain, with a flat term structure (60.5% near versus 60.4% far). The nearest expiry prices a ±8.4% move; the November tenor prices ±26.6%.

Participants hold substantial downside protection in size but are not paying up for it, and uncertainty is spread evenly across tenors rather than concentrated on a dated catalyst. For a holder reducing exposure, listed puts are cheap relative to what the stock has actually done. Short interest of 9.2% of float at 4.8 days to cover is moderate — enough to produce squeeze rallies on good news, not enough to make the bearish view crowded.

**Risk Factors: 3.0.** Multiple live binary regulatory processes with no resolution date, an opaque self-held subprime loan book with escalating provisions, unquantified borrower-defense contingencies, and a Q4 guide requiring sharp margin inflection. Little of this is compensated at 37x FY2027 consensus.

## Management Quality

The record through FY2025 was strong: cumulative TSR of $641 on a $100 FY2020 base by FY2025 against a peer group at $283, and adjusted EBITDA compounding from $34.2M (FY2021) to $126.5M (FY2025). CEO Grant's FY2025 total compensation was $5.15M with compensation actually paid of $15.23M, the gap reflecting equity mark-to-market in a year the stock performed exceptionally. Performance measures are adjusted EBITDA, stock price and revenue.

Two forward-looking observations, which is what matters here.

First, **the insider record is uniformly on the sell side with no offsetting purchases.** Coliseum Capital Management sold 3,000,000 shares at $41.40 on 8 June 2026 for $124.2M gross, cutting its position to 971,440 shares — roughly a 76% reduction — approximately two months before a print that took the stock down 34%. Nine other officers and directors sold between $30.08 and $44.42 in the February–June 2026 window, including the CEO (60,040 shares at $34.95 in February; 94,500 shares at $41.15–$41.63 in June, leaving 335,715). No open-market purchases by any officer or director appear in the eighteen-month Form 4 record. Fair caveats apply: Coliseum's stake dated to the retired convertible preferred and had appreciated roughly 6.4x, making concentration management a complete and ordinary explanation requiring no information advantage; several executive sales sit adjacent to disclosed vesting and tax-withholding events; the Chairman's activity is explicitly under Rule 10b5-1. Whether the other executive sales were under pre-existing plans is not established, so we read this as weak aggregate conviction rather than informed trading. Even on the charitable reading, an insider group with zero open-market buying at $27 after selling at $34–44 provides no support.

Second, **capital allocation returns nothing to shareholders while consuming cash.** A $35.0M repurchase authorization has been in place since December 2020 with no shares repurchased in FY2023, FY2024, FY2025 or the nine months of FY2026. No dividend. All capital goes to campuses, programs, technology and student receivables, against guided negative free cash flow. That is internally consistent, and it means shareholders bear the full duration risk of the expansion with no cash return and no valuation support from buybacks.

A point in management's favor deserves stating: the FY2026 growth spend is expensed *within* adjusted EBITDA, which is the company-selected compensation metric, and guided FY2026 adjusted EBITDA of $100–103M falls below FY2024's $102.9M. A team optimizing near-term payout would more likely have deferred or capitalized the spend. We cannot exclude that the compensation committee adjusts targets or excludes growth spend from the plan-specific definition, and we did not read the plan mechanics, so this is modest rather than decisive credit.

**Management Quality: 3.5.** No open-market buying, a large anchor holder exiting at nearly 1.5x the current price, no capital return against a six-year-old authorization, and a guidance cut that damaged credibility on a plan the market had been underwriting.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case ($36, +33%)

FY2026 is a discrete, self-funded investment year. The profit decline is concentrated in the UTI division precisely because that division is opening the most campuses — nine-month segment operating income fell to $25.8M from $58.0M while starts grew 14.4%, which is the signature of pre-opening cost drag rather than demand weakness. UTI-Atlanta's initial starts came in reportedly ~30% above internal expectation; San Antonio and the Dallas expansion are ramping; advertising spent in 2026 for campuses opening in 2027 is pre-revenue by construction and mean-reverts as those campuses fill. The multi-phase restructuring funds the growth plan out of overhead rather than out of shareholder returns. The secular demand backdrop is real and independently corroborated: technician and healthcare-worker shortages, projected 5–24% job growth through 2034 in Concorde's twelve new program fields, and AI-driven displacement of office work creating a reskilling cohort. If FY2028 revenue reaches ~$1.05B at a 16–18% adjusted EBITDA margin, adjusted EBITDA is $170–190M, and 10–11x ex-leases supports $34–38 per share. The balance sheet is net-cash positive with a fully available $125M revolver, so the company can fund the plan without equity issuance, and the ED composite score of 2.3 plus the July release of the $19.6M letter of credit removes the near-term regulatory funding constraint.

### Bear Case ($15, -45%)

Margin compression is structural. Advertising rose to 12.5% of revenue in both divisions simultaneously and the company is adding 20% more admissions staff, which is a rising customer-acquisition cost, not a timing item. Tuition escalators have fallen from 6.0% to 1.9%, so there is no price offset. The credit-loss provision has tripled as a share of revenue in two years on a loan book UTI owns, valued on Level 3 inputs anchored to a collection history that includes the payment pause; reported 0% cohort default rates are non-informative and normalization pressures both the CDR eligibility test and loss experience. OBBBA federal loan limits reduce the funding available to students for $40,000–$99,000 programs, forcing more onto that same balance sheet. The "Do No Harm" benchmark and the gainful-employment debt-to-earnings tests threaten the longest, highest-tuition programs specifically. Meanwhile the company has committed $199.4M of lease liabilities plus $50.1M of uncommenced leases and four 2027 campuses ahead of revenue, with guided negative free cash flow and liquidity down $74M. If FY2027 EPS lands near the current $0.73 consensus and the multiple converges toward the 12.3x peer median, the equity is worth $9–11; at 14x on $1.00 of FY2027 EPS with a slightly negative net-cash position, roughly $14–15. A further guidance reduction would compress the funding window and put the ED composite score in play, which is the mechanism by which weak profitability becomes a regulatory problem.

### Base Case ($23, -15%)

Utilization improves through FY2027 as Atlanta, San Antonio and Dallas fill, but the 2027 campus slate keeps growth expense in the P&L and acquisition cost normalizes only partially. FY2027 revenue of roughly $960M at a 10% operating margin gives $96M of operating income, roughly $65M net income and $1.16 diluted EPS. At 20x — a 60% premium to the peer median, warranted by faster growth and a stronger balance sheet — that is $23. Provisioning stays elevated at 3%+ of revenue, free cash flow returns to modestly positive rather than to the $55M of FY2025, and no capital is returned.

Probability-weighting 30% bull / 45% base / 25% bear gives approximately $24.9, or roughly −8% from $27.10 before carrying cost. That is a below-fair expected return with unfavorable asymmetry: 45% downside against 33% upside.

## Conclusion

UTI is a legitimate operator in a sector with genuine secular demand, executing an expansion program that its balance sheet can currently afford. The enrollment data is not the problem: consolidated starts +10.9%, UTI-division starts +23.4%, end-of-period students +9.1%. The problem is that the price still requires the entire margin structure to be restored and then some, at a time when consensus estimates are being cut 22% for the current year and 14% for next, free cash flow is guided negative, the credit-loss provision has tripled as a share of revenue on a loan book the company itself carries at Level 3 fair value, and the federal rules that determine program eligibility and student funding capacity are actively being rewritten against a company drawing 78% of revenue from those programs.

The 47% decline from $51.34 does not make the stock cheap. At 37x FY2027 consensus against a peer median of 12.3x, and 24x even on a full restoration of FY2025's $1.133 of EPS, the market is still awarding a large growth premium to a business whose pricing power has decelerated three years running and whose customer-acquisition cost is rising in both divisions at once. A complete operational recovery gets a holder to roughly $23–29 on defensible multiples; a structural read on acquisition cost and credit gets to $15. Insiders sold heavily at $34–44 and have bought nothing at $27, and the $35M repurchase authorization has gone unused for six years.

**REDUCE.** Trim into rallies toward $31–33, hedge with cheap November optionality if maintaining exposure, and revisit on the late-November FY2027 guide or a price near $19–21.
