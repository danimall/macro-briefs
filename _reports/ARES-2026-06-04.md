**Research Date: June 4, 2026**

## Executive Summary

Ares Management is one of the largest dedicated alternative credit managers in the world, with $622.5B of AUM as of year-end 2025 (+29% YoY) and a fee base where 93% of management fees come from perpetual or long-dated capital. The franchise is high quality: fee-related earnings grew 30% to $1.775B in 2025, the firm raised a record ~$30B in Q1 2026, and it sits on roughly $158B of dry powder it can deploy into a dislocating credit market. On the operating metrics that matter most for an asset manager (fundraising momentum, fee durability, FRE growth) Ares is performing well.

The problem is positioning relative to the cycle. Roughly 65% of AUM is in credit and 44% is in direct lending, which makes Ares the single most credit-cycle-exposed name among the scaled alternatives managers. The private-credit asset class is undergoing its first genuine stress test after a decade of explosive growth, and while Ares is not directly implicated in the marquee blowups (First Brands, Tricolor), it is the bellwether the market sells when private-credit anxiety rises. The stock has fallen from ~$180 in September 2025 and a $161.63 close on 12/31/25 to $123.10, a ~37% drawdown from the 52-week high of $195.26. That decline tracks three consecutive earnings misses (Q1'26 -9%, Q4'25 -16%, Q2'25 -7%) and consistently falling consensus estimates (current-year EPS revised down 8.3% over 90 days).

The drawdown improves the entry but does not resolve the core uncertainty. At ~57x trailing/forward GAAP P/E (and ~21x on the ~$5.94 current-year consensus EPS), Ares trades at a steep premium to a sector median P/E of 16.2, a premium justified only if FRE growth durability survives a credit downturn that has not yet arrived. With estimates falling, fee-related performance revenue down ~30% YoY, an ACOF VI fee step-down of ~$40M coming in 2026, founders having sold heavily near the peak, and the firm at the center of a building credit-stress narrative, the risk/reward is balanced. This is a HOLD with a genuine uncertainty designation: the quality is real, the cyclical exposure is real, and the resolution depends on whether private credit deteriorates materially over the next two to three quarters.

## Company Overview

Founded in 1997 and headquartered in Los Angeles, Ares Management operates as a global alternative asset manager across five segments. The Credit Group ($406.9B AUM, ~65% of total) is the largest dedicated credit platform globally, spanning US and European direct lending, liquid and opportunistic credit, and Ares Capital Corp (ARCC), the largest publicly traded BDC. Real Assets ($139.1B) nearly doubled in 2025 via the GCP acquisition and now spans real estate equity and debt, infrastructure, and digital/data-center infrastructure ("Ada Infrastructure"). Secondaries ($42.2B), Private Equity ($25.3B), and Other (~$9.1B) round out the platform, alongside ~$25.9B of related Ares Insurance Solutions/Aspida assets.

AUM has compounded at roughly 26% over five years and 21% over ten years. The structural advantages are genuine: 76% of AUM is institutional direct, and the embedded growth pipeline of $156-158B of not-yet-fee-paying AUM represents an estimated ~$730M of incremental annual management fees if deployed (~23% on the 2025 base). The firm is founder-controlled, with Antony Ressler (Executive Chairman) and Michael Arougheti (CEO) and the founding group holding over 70% of voting power; Class A holders are effectively non-voting.

The market cap of ~$28.7B reflects only the Class A float; total enterprise value is materially larger once the ~105M AOG units held by insiders are included. This matters for interpreting per-share metrics, since the headline market cap understates the economic size of the franchise.

## Financial Analysis

FY2025 GAAP results were strong on the surface. Total revenue rose 44% to $5.60B. Management fees, the durable core, grew 25% to $3.68B. Carried interest allocation swung up 196% to $1.15B, a large but inherently volatile line tied to realization timing. Incentive fees were $362M and administrative/transaction/other fees rose 119% to $356M, largely GCP-driven. Fee-Related Earnings, the key valuation metric for an asset manager, grew 30% to $1.775B, and Realized Income rose 26% to $1.848B. Net income attributable to ARES was $527M, with $426M to Class A common after $101M of Series B preferred dividends. ROE was ~25.5%.

The balance sheet is appropriate for a fee-based asset manager. Total assets were $28.6B and equity $4.28B, with long-term debt of $3.94B (senior notes $2.15B, sub notes $450M, $1.38B credit facility). Goodwill jumped to $3.45B from $1.16B post-GCP. Corporate cash was ~$489M and company-only operating cash flow was ~$2.11B. The Altman Z-Score of ~0.5 ("distress") is a false signal: it was built for industrial manufacturers and penalizes asset managers for balance-sheet leverage and Consolidated Fund gross-ups that have nothing to do with bankruptcy risk. The real financial-health indicators (FRE growth, fundraising, fee durability) are all solid.

Two cracks deserve emphasis. First, fee-related performance revenues fell ~30% YoY (per the Seeking Alpha sell-side note), a direct read on softening transaction and realization activity. Second, ACOF VI management fees step down ~$40M in 2026 as ACOF VII activates, a known headwind to the management-fee line. The earnings track record has also deteriorated: the firm missed consensus in three of the last four quarters (Q1'26 actual $1.24 vs $1.36 estimate, -9.0%; Q4'25 $1.45 vs $1.73, -16.0%; Q2'25 $1.03 vs $1.11, -7.0%), with only a marginal Q3'25 beat (+0.9%). The Q4'25 miss on after-tax realized income sent shares down 8.4% on the day. For a stock priced at a premium multiple, a 1-of-4 beat rate undermines confidence in forward consensus.

## Growth Analysis

The growth engine is functioning. Q1 2026, reported May 1, swelled dry powder to ~$158B and produced a record investment pipeline. Management said the firm is on track for a record fundraising year and that institutional investors are "not anxious," commentary that explicitly eased private-credit doomsday fears and stands as the most important recent positive datapoint.

The qualifier is that growth is increasingly deployment- and realization-dependent in a way that is sensitive to the credit cycle. Raising capital is one thing; deploying it at attractive vintages and realizing carried interest at historical pace is another, and management itself flags realization timing as the source of earnings volatility. The three consecutive misses are the practical consequence. Estimate revisions confirm the cooling: current-year EPS has been cut 8.3% over 90 days (to $5.94), next-year EPS cut 6.3% (to $7.32), and the current quarter trimmed 6.6%. Consensus is moving against the name, not toward it, the opposite of the rising-estimate pattern that signals an undervalued momentum stock.

Falling rates cut both ways and are underappreciated. Roughly 86% of Credit Group debt assets are floating-rate; the Fed's 2025 cuts compress portfolio-level net investment income and can pressure ARCC Part I fees. The market often frames rate cuts as a positive for asset managers via lower discount rates and easier fundraising, but for Ares's specific asset mix, the near-term income effect is a headwind. The secular optionality in digital/data-center infrastructure (post-GCP) and insurance (Aspida) is genuine but will not move the needle on a 12-month horizon.

## Valuation Assessment

On headline metrics, Ares is expensive. The trailing and forward P/E of ~57x reflects GAAP EPS of $1.76, which is depressed by preferred dividends and the Class A structure. On the consensus of ~$5.94 current-year EPS, the multiple is ~21x, and on next-year's $7.32 it is ~17x. Even on the favorable framing, Ares trades at a meaningful premium to the asset-management peer median P/E of 16.2 (NTRS 17.8, STT 16.2, BEN 23.7, TROW 11.2, AMP 11.1). The premium has historically been justified by faster AUM growth, higher-quality perpetual fee base, and superior FRE compounding, and those attributes remain intact.

The valuation case is genuinely two-sided. The bull argument is that the drawdown has compressed the multiple toward the low end of Ares's own range while FRE growth, record fundraising, and dry powder remain robust, and that the analyst target distribution (mean $145.35, median $142.00, high $190, low $125) sits entirely above the $123.10 price, implying ~15% to median and ~54% to the high. The bear argument is that the premium multiple prices in continued FRE durability that a credit downcycle would impair, that estimates are being cut not raised, and that a "cheap relative to its own history" multiple on peak-cycle fee-related performance revenue is the classic value trap for a cyclical-at-the-margin business.

The estimate-revision guardrail is decisive here. The strong-BUY estimate-revision signal applies to stocks where consensus is rising; Ares is the inverse, with estimates falling. Treating analyst targets as a floor is appropriate only for stocks cheap relative to rising estimates. With falling estimates and a premium-to-peer multiple, the targets are more likely stale-high than stale-low, and proximity to the low target ($125) is a more reliable signal than the mean. I score valuation at 5.5: the drawdown helps, but there is no asymmetric mispricing at $123 with consensus deteriorating.

## Competitive Landscape

Ares competes with Blackstone, Apollo, KKR, Blue Owl, and other scaled alternatives managers. Its competitive position within private credit is strong and arguably improving on a structural basis: it is the largest dedicated credit manager globally, with scale and incumbency advantages that compound as institutional allocators consolidate relationships with the largest, most diversified platforms. The GCP acquisition broadened Real Assets and added digital-infrastructure exposure as data-center demand grows. Direct lending share gains and the dry powder position the firm to deploy into a credit dislocation at attractive vintages, which historically widens the gap between scaled survivors and sub-scale competitors during stress.

The competitive qualifier is cyclical, not structural. Q4 industry reviews ranked Blackstone the standout and screened Ares as a relative laggard this cycle, reflecting Ares's heavier credit/direct-lending tilt at a moment when that exposure is exactly what the market fears. The moat is intact and the trajectory is favorable on a multi-year view, but the same concentration that builds the moat in private credit also concentrates the cyclical risk. I score competitive position 7.0: a genuinely advantaged franchise, with the caveat that its differentiation is also its vulnerability this cycle.

## Risk Assessment

The dominant risk is private-credit cycle exposure, which makes Ares the bellwether for the asset class. Industry-wide stress is building per multiple Tier-1 and sell-side sources through April-May 2026: rising defaults, software-sector exposure, liquidity-mismatch concerns, and high-profile warnings about hidden credit problems. There is no "Lehman moment" yet, but sentiment and potential credit losses are the swing factor for both the multiple and forward fees. This sits against a macro backdrop where sophisticated credit positioning is pricing material deterioration on a 6-12 month horizon even as near-term spreads stay tight and primary access remains open.

Secondary risks compound. ARCC single-fund concentration means a "significant portion" of management fees derives from one vehicle whose advisory agreement is terminable on 60 days' notice. Retail/wealth redemption risk in non-traded REITs, ASIF, and semi-liquid vehicles could surge during stress, cutting fees and forcing concessions. GCP integration risk includes emerging-market exposure (Japan, Brazil, Vietnam) and large one-time charges ($227.6M equity comp, $301.1M contingent-consideration revaluation). Governance is founder-controlled with a ~$2.4B TRA paying 85% of tax benefits to insiders, capping any takeover premium. Regulatory overhangs include potential non-bank SIFI designation, SEC private-fund scrutiny, AIFMD II (effective April 2026), insurance-capital changes affecting Aspida, and carried-interest tax risk. I score risk factors 4.5: the credit-cycle concentration is a specific, non-trivial downside catalyst that is only partially priced, and the earnings-miss record confirms visibility is deteriorating.

### Options Market Signal

The options market is pricing imminent event stress. Near-term ATM IV of 66.9% is rich against 40.7% one-year historical vol (84th percentile within the current chain), and the term structure is in backwardation (near-term 66.9% > long-term 50.9%), which signals the market expects a near-dated catalyst. IV has collapsed from 112.1% on May 22 to 66.9% on June 4 (down 45.2pp) as the prior event window passed and price firmed +3.3%. The put/call skew is call-heavy (-42.6%) and the P/C OI ratio is benign at 0.66 and falling, which argues against an acute bearish-positioning read at the very front end. Layered against this, short interest is elevated at 14.8% of float (4.9 days to cover), meaningful institutional bearish positioning that creates two-sided risk: a positive surprise could squeeze, while continued credit anxiety validates the shorts. The options structure does not strongly confirm either direction; it confirms uncertainty and the proximity of catalysts.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull Case.** Ares is a fallen high-quality compounder. FRE growth, the Q1'26 fundraising record, and dry powder can be deployed into a credit dislocation at attractive vintages, historically the best environment for scaled credit managers. The drawdown to $123 has compressed the multiple toward the low end of its range, director open-market buying (Bhutani 10,000 shares at $126.61 in February, Olian at $124.43) signals insider confidence near current levels, and the full analyst target distribution sits above the price. If private credit avoids a hard landing, FRE durability reasserts and the stock re-rates toward $145-160 (~18-30% upside) as record fundraising converts to fee-paying AUM.

**Bear Case.** Ares is the most credit-cycle-exposed name among scaled alternatives at the moment the credit cycle is most fragile. Estimates are falling, the firm missed three of four quarters, fee-related performance revenue is down, ACOF VI fees step down in 2026, and 86% floating-rate credit assets mean Fed cuts are a near-term income headwind. Founders sold $200M+ near the peak ($150-181) and January 2026 C-suite grants are already underwater at $149.67. If private-credit defaults accelerate into a visible event, the premium multiple compresses toward the peer median and earnings estimates fall further, taking the stock toward $95-100 (~19-23% downside, retesting the 52-week low of $95.80).

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Reassessment timeline:** Next earnings (Q2 2026, expected early August) is the primary checkpoint, with the credit-cycle narrative and rate path monitored continuously.

**Upgrade triggers (to BUY):**
- Two consecutive quarters of earnings beats with stabilizing or rising consensus estimates, confirming FRE durability through the credit-stress window.
- Evidence that record fundraising is converting to fee-paying AUM (fee-paying AUM growth re-accelerating and management-fee growth re-accelerating despite the ACOF VI step-down).
- A pullback toward $100-105 with fundamentals intact, restoring asymmetric upside to the $142 median target.

**Downgrade triggers (to AVOID):**
- Materialized private-credit stress hitting Ares's own portfolio — rising direct-lending non-accruals/defaults, an ARCC NAV decline, or redemption surges in retail/semi-liquid vehicles.
- A fourth consecutive earnings miss or current-year EPS estimates cut below ~$5.50.
- Confirmation that fee-related performance revenue declines are structural rather than cyclical, alongside continued estimate cuts.

## Conclusion

Ares Management is a genuinely high-quality alternative asset manager whose franchise advantages (scale in private credit, perpetual/long-dated fee base, FRE growth, record Q1'26 fundraising, dry powder) are intact. The ~37% drawdown from the September peak improves the entry materially, and the asset-class concentration that creates the cyclical risk also builds the structural moat. If private credit avoids a hard landing, the stock offers ~15-30% upside to consensus.

The reasons this is a HOLD rather than a BUY are concrete and current. The stock trades at a premium to a peer median that is justified only by FRE durability the cycle has not yet tested; consensus estimates are falling across every period; the firm has missed three of its last four quarters; fee-related performance revenue is down; founders sold heavily near the top while the stock now sits below their exit prices and below the January grant strike; and the name is the market's first sell when private-credit anxiety rises into a building stress environment. The director open-market buying and record fundraising are real counter-signals, but they do not offset the falling-estimate momentum and the binary credit-cycle exposure. The risk/reward is balanced, not asymmetric. I rate ARES a HOLD with an uncertain rationale: the resolution hinges on a credit-cycle outcome that is not yet decided, and the appropriate posture is to wait for either a cleaner entry near $100-105 or two quarters of confirmed FRE durability before committing fresh capital.
