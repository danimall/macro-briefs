## Executive Summary

Coterra Energy is no longer a separately listed security. Devon Energy announced an all-stock acquisition on February 2, 2026, shareholders of both companies approved it on May 4, 2026, and the transaction closed on approximately May 7–8, 2026. Each Coterra share converted into **0.70 Devon shares**, leaving Devon legacy holders with 54% and Coterra legacy holders with 46% of the combined company. Any position carried under the CTRA ticker is now a Devon position at that fixed ratio; the ticker itself carries no independent economics.

The data infrastructure corroborates this. EDGAR retrieval failed on CIK resolution for 8-K and Form 4 pulls while the historical XBRL series (FY2022–FY2025) and the March 20, 2025 DEF 14A remained intact — the pattern expected when a registration is terminated but the historical filing record persists. None of these tool behaviors is proof of deregistration on its own; taken with six independent news sources confirming the close, the conclusion is not in serious doubt.

That resolves a score trajectory that had been drifting lower for the wrong reason. Prior reports moved 6.5 → 6.1 → 6.0 → 5.8 → 5.8 → 5.5 with four consecutive HOLDs, with the declines concentrated in valuation (-1.5) and management quality (-1.5). The correct present finding is categorical rather than incremental: there is no CTRA instrument to hold, so a HOLD is not a coherent action. The underlying asset base is reasonably good — a Delaware Basin oil position, the legacy Cabot Marcellus dry-gas position, and Anadarko acreage, generating $1,733M of FY2025 free cash flow on 0.72x net debt to an EBITDA proxy and 12.0x interest coverage — but investors can now only access it inside Devon, at Devon's price, under Devon's capital allocation, with Kimmeridge and TOMS Capital both pressing publicly for asset sales. The recommendation is AVOID on the ticker, and the appropriate action for anyone still carrying a CTRA line item is to re-underwrite it as Devon.

## Company Overview

Coterra was a large-cap U.S. independent exploration and production company headquartered in Houston, formed by the 2021 merger of Cabot Oil & Gas and Cimarex Energy. Thomas E. Jorden, formerly Cimarex CEO, led it through the Devon transaction.

The narrative disclosure set for the most recent 10-K was not retrievable — Items 1, 1A, 2, 7, 7A and 8 were unavailable, and the section parser saw only Items 10–15. Consequently there are no verified proved reserves, reserve life, acreage, well counts, basin-level realizations, per-unit costs, hedge positions, debt maturity schedule, or covenant terms in this analysis. That is a substantial gap, and it materially limits what can be asserted about asset quality beyond what the financial statements and external reporting establish.

What the external record does establish is a three-basin structure: a Delaware Basin position that Siebert Williams Shank described as among the largest in the play post-merger; the legacy Cabot Marcellus dry-gas position in northeast Pennsylvania, corroborated indirectly by Reuters reporting that Stone Ridge Asset Management approached Devon on June 1, 2026 with an approximately $8 billion offer for "Devon Energy's Marcellus shale assets" acquired via Coterra; and Anadarko Basin acreage in Oklahoma, referenced in market context but unverified from a filing here.

Commodity mix is the defining structural characteristic. Devon's first combined-company guidance (Reuters, June 9, 2026) is roughly 1.38 MMboe/d of total production including approximately 500 kbbl/d of oil, or about 36% oil by volume. Devon's prior standalone guidance was 835–855 kbbl/d of total output. Legacy Coterra therefore contributed the bulk of the gas weighting. This was a gas-heavy portfolio with a growing oil overlay, and its equity behaved accordingly: MarketWatch noted CTRA and DVN each rose about 10% in January 2026 as cold weather drove natural gas prices higher.

The governing fact is the 40.3% single-year revenue decline from 2022 to 2023 against DD&A that rose slightly, meaning volumes were flat to higher and the earnings swing was almost entirely realized price. Operating margin traversed nearly 30 percentage points in four years on an asset base that did not shrink. Any assessment of this portfolio starts from that price sensitivity, not from company-specific execution.

FY2025 revenue of $7,294M was 33.6% above FY2024. Without MD&A, the price/volume/mix decomposition is unavailable, but the balance sheet points to acquired Permian volumes plus firmer gas realizations as the primary drivers.

**FY2025 deployed the balance sheet.** Cash fell from $2,038M to $114M. Net PP&E rose $4,168M to $22,058M. Total assets rose $2,616M to $24,241M. Long-term debt was essentially flat at $3,568M versus $3,535M. Stockholders' equity rose $1,716M to $14,838M. Interest expense nearly doubled to $205M from $106M.

Reconciling property additions: net PP&E up $4,168M with $2,370M of DD&A charged implies gross additions of roughly $6,538M against reported capex of $2,288M, leaving approximately **$4,250M of non-capex property additions** — acquisitions and purchase-price allocation. Trade press reported Permian acquisitions from Franklin Mountain Energy and Avant Natural Resources closing in early 2025, broadly consistent in magnitude, but the counterparties, price, and consideration mix were not verifiable from any filing here. The existence and approximate scale of a large 2025 property acquisition is established by the balance sheet; the specific transaction identification is not.

Funding was conservative. Retained earnings rose $1,037M against $1,717M of net income, implying roughly $680M of distributions; the equity roll-forward then implies approximately $679M of net new equity. So a roughly $4.25 billion acquisition was funded principally by spending a $2.0 billion cash balance, issuing modest stock, and assuming approximately $900M of additional liabilities, with long-term debt untouched. An alternative reading cannot be excluded: part of that $900M liability increase may be a revolver draw sitting in current liabilities rather than acquired deferred taxes, in which case debt funding was higher than the long-term-debt line suggests. Without the balance-sheet notes these cannot be distinguished.

**Leverage stayed conservative; liquidity did not.** Derived net debt rose from $1,497M to $3,454M, up 131%. Against an EBITDA proxy of $4,822M (operating income plus DD&A), net debt/EBITDA is **0.72x**, and interest coverage is **12.0x**. Total liabilities to equity is 0.63x, and long-term debt to total assets *improved* to 14.7% from 16.4%. But the current ratio fell from 2.92x to 1.19x. A screen reading only debt-to-assets would score FY2025 as a deleveraging year; economically, net debt more than doubled and the buffer was consumed. This is a reduction in optionality rather than an approach to distress, and undrawn revolver capacity may have fully substituted for the cash — there is no credit-facility disclosure to check.

The interest-expense flow (up from $73M in 2023 to $205M in 2025) against a flat ending balance most likely reflects higher average intra-year balances, less capitalized interest, or a higher blended coupon after refinancing. The debt note was unavailable, so this remains unresolved and is a genuine capital-structure gap.

**Cash flow.** FY2025 operating cash flow was $4,021M, capex $2,288M, free cash flow $1,733M, a derived 23.8% FCF margin. Operating cash flow at 2.3x net income is normal for a depleting E&P given $2,370M of DD&A. One line deserves attention: capex ran at 96.5% of depletion, $82M below DD&A. For an E&P, sustained capex below depletion implies harvesting rather than organic replacement. The most likely benign explanation is mechanical — the DD&A base was inflated mid-year by acquired producing reserves, and acquired drilled-but-uncompleted inventory converts to production at below-normal capital. The reserve-replacement ratio would settle it and is unavailable. FY2022–FY2024 cash flow fields were empty in the XBRL pull, so no multi-year FCF trend has been established.

**Screens.** The financial-health scoring tool returned insufficient data, so both screens were computed manually. Altman Z ≈ 2.45 using the January 30, 2026 market capitalization of $21,970M with year-end 2025 balance-sheet data; the original five-factor model penalizes the 0.30x asset turnover that is structural to owning $22 billion of oil and gas properties, and at 0.72x net debt/EBITDA with 12x coverage this is not a solvency signal. Piotroski F-Score is 7 of 9, with both failed signals (current ratio, share issuance) direct consequences of the acquisition rather than deterioration.

**Earnings delivery was weak into the deal.** Three misses in four quarters: -1.4% (Mar-25), +4.1% (Jun-25), -9.0% (Sep-25), and -20.6% (Dec-25, $0.39 versus $0.49 estimated). The deterioration in the surprise pattern across the second half of 2025 is consistent with realized prices and costs running behind consensus, and with the relative equity underperformance that preceded the merger — CTRA returned about 4% in the twelve months to January 30, 2026 versus DVN +18%, gas futures +17%, and the S&P 500 +15%.

## Growth Analysis

For the CTRA instrument, forward growth is zero — there is no security to which growth accrues. The relevant question is what the legacy assets are now growing into.

Devon's combined-company outlook implies roughly 1.38 MMboe/d, versus prior standalone Devon guidance of 835–855 kbbl/d of total output, alongside up to 70% of free cash flow returned to shareholders and $1.25 billion of debt repayment. Targeted synergies are $1 billion annually, with secondary sources indicating a late-2027 timeline. Devon reported Q2 2026 net earnings of $1.9 billion, its highest quarterly profit since 2022, with production at the upper end of forecast; Reuters attributed the strength to a surge in crude prices, which places it in the same category as the January 2026 gas-driven rally — a price outcome, not a run-rate. Seeking Alpha reported $1.7 billion of Q2 free cash flow, more than double Q1; that figure is tier-3 and should not be annualized. First-full-quarter purchase accounting, merger costs, derivative and working-capital settlements, and cash-tax timing all distort it. If a run-rate framing is used at all, roughly $6.8 billion annualized is an upper-bound sensitivity at Q2 pricing, not a forecast.

Two growth-relevant capital events cut against each other. On May 21, 2026, Devon paid approximately $2.6 billion for 16,300 net undeveloped acres in the core Delaware Basin at a federal lease sale — a derived **~$159,500 per net acre** — with Reuters reporting analysts calling the per-location price "eye watering," while noting favorable lease terms and proximity to Devon's best assets. Per-undeveloped-acre metrics overstate cost where multiple benches are prospective, so this may prove accretive. Two weeks after closing a merger justified by synergies and payout, however, it competes directly with the 70% FCF-return commitment and the $1.25 billion of debt repayment for the same dollars. On June 1, Stone Ridge approached Devon with an approximately $8 billion offer for the legacy Coterra Marcellus assets, and Devon has a portfolio review underway.

Growth prospects score at 3.5. The legacy assets have credible mid-cycle growth inside a larger vehicle, but the CTRA holder captures none of it directly, the mix remains roughly 64% gas by volume into a market where price sets earnings, and the confirmed FY2025 organic reinvestment ran marginally below depletion.

This rests on a single Reuters report of an unsolicited approach relayed via MSN, is unconfirmed by any filing, and the asset scope may include Devon's own Appalachian interests. Without the Properties section there are no reserve volumes against which to test whether $8 billion is high or low.

Valuation scores 2.5. Cheapness on the last clean pre-deal metrics is not investable, and a compressed historical multiple with no accessible instrument is the purest form of value trap — the asset is unavailable at any price under this ticker.

## Competitive Landscape

Coterra's outcome is a chapter in post-2023 shale consolidation: Exxon/Pioneer ($60 billion, closed 2024), Chevron/Hess ($53 billion, closed 2025), Devon/Grayson Mill ($5 billion, 2024), and now Devon/Coterra at a $58 billion combined enterprise value. Wedbush framed the merger as giving both parties the scale needed to compete with Exxon and Diamondback, which is a statement about the pre-merger position: at roughly $22 billion of equity value Coterra was large but sub-scale against the new peer set, and its board accepted that standalone scale was insufficient.

Two structural implications follow. First, core Delaware inventory is the binding industry constraint, not production volume — Devon's willingness to pay approximately $159,500 per net undeveloped acre three months after arguing that scale and synergies justified a merger is the clearest available evidence. Second, gas exposure is contested. Morningstar's July 16, 2026 note states plainly that "bears frown on its multibasin, multiresource story" while arguing Devon's gas exposure helps over the long term. That disagreement is a disagreement about the legacy Coterra Marcellus and Anadarko assets specifically, and the portfolio review plus the Stone Ridge approach mean the market will get price discovery on it.

Competitive position scores 5.0: a genuinely good Delaware position and a scale Marcellus asset, now diluted to a 46% interest and blended with Devon's Anadarko, Eagle Ford, Williston, and Rockies exposures, with the asset mix itself under activist challenge.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull case (for the residual Devon exposure, not for CTRA).** The legacy Coterra assets entered Devon at conservative leverage (0.72x net debt/EBITDA, 12x coverage) with a 23.8% FY2025 FCF margin, and at 5.3x EV/EBITDA on the last clean pre-deal metrics they were not richly valued. The Stone Ridge approach at roughly 36% of Coterra's entire pre-merger equity value for one basin suggests the public market undervalued the gas position; a portfolio-review divestiture at or near that level would convert that gap into cash, fund the $1.25 billion of debt repayment and buybacks, and de-risk the gas weighting. Two activists pushing in the same direction raise the probability of that outcome. Devon's Q2 2026 print — $1.9 billion of net earnings, its best since 2022, production at the high end — indicates the combined operation is functioning. Add $1 billion of targeted synergies, 46% of which accrues to legacy Coterra holders, and the exchange ratio's lack of premium becomes defensible.

**Bear case.** For the CTRA ticker the bear case is definitional: there is nothing to own, no price, no coverage, and no participation. For the residual exposure, the asset base remains a price-taker whose revenue can fall 40% in twelve months without any operational failure, now roughly 64% gas by volume into a market where Devon itself frames strength as commodity-driven. The company drained a $2.0 billion cash buffer to buy Permian assets in 2025, then Devon spent $2.6 billion on undeveloped acreage at a per-acre price analysts called "eye watering" two weeks after closing a synergy-justified merger, while simultaneously promising 70% FCF return and $1.25 billion of debt paydown. Those promises are not jointly fundable at mid-cycle prices without divesting something, which is precisely what the activists are demanding and what management has not committed to. Coterra's own delivery record into the deal was three misses in four quarters, capped by a 20.6% Q4 2025 shortfall, and five-year relative TSR of 187.80 versus a peer 185.30 shows no durable outperformance.

**Management quality.** Coterra's management is no longer in control of these assets, which limits how much forward weight the dimension can carry. What the record shows: CEO total compensation ranged only $11.06M–$15.30M across FY2020–FY2024 while net income ranged $201M–$4,065M, with compensation actually paid ranging $6.10M–$32.09M — a stable grant-date architecture with equity-linked realization, which is design working as intended rather than misalignment. Performance was the weaker point: five-year cumulative TSR 1.3% ahead of a company-selected peer group, with underperformance in 2021, 2022, and 2023. No Form 4 data was obtainable, so there is no insider-transaction evidence in either direction, and Kimmeridge's explicit demand to revamp executive pay targets the combined board rather than Coterra's plan specifically. Score 4.5.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Re-entry criteria for the CTRA ticker**: none exist, and none will. Confirmation of deregistration and delisting should remove the ticker from the coverage universe. **Action for holders of a legacy CTRA position**: the position is 0.70 Devon shares per former Coterra share. Re-underwrite it as Devon on Devon's price, balance sheet, and capital plan. The specific items to resolve, none of which can be answered from Coterra's record:

1. **Portfolio review outcome.** Does Devon transact on the legacy Marcellus at or near the reported $8 billion approach level, and what does the disclosed reserve and cash-flow detail imply about whether the public market was mispricing that asset? This is the single most informative event for anyone assessing what Coterra contributed.
2. **Capital-allocation reconciliation.** Verify from an actual Devon 8-K or press release the size, term, and conditions of the reported buyback authorization and dividend increase, and confirm whether the 70% FCF-return commitment, $1.25 billion of debt repayment, and further inventory acquisition are jointly fundable at strip prices. A second large undeveloped-acreage purchase at a per-acre price comparable to the May lease sale, absent a divestiture, would indicate the return commitment is subordinate to inventory building.
3. **Synergy verification.** $1 billion annually with a late-2027 target; look for quantified realization in Devon's quarterly disclosure, not restated guidance. Legacy Coterra holders' 46% share is roughly $460 million per year, the substitute for the cash premium they did not receive.
4. **Activist resolution.** Kimmeridge and TOMS Capital outcomes — board changes, divestitures, compensation redesign, or a sale process — will determine near-term capital-allocation discipline more than commodity prices will.

**Downgrade/upgrade thresholds for the successor position**: reassess if Devon's net debt rises while both buybacks and acreage purchases continue, if the portfolio review concludes with no transaction and no explanation of why the Marcellus is worth more inside the company, or if realized gas differentials in Appalachia widen materially against the FY2025 baseline. Reassess constructively if a divestiture at or above the reported approach level funds debt reduction and buybacks simultaneously.

**Timeline**: the portfolio review and activist campaigns are live now; synergy verification runs through late 2027.
