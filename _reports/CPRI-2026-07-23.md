**Research Date: July 23, 2026**

## Executive Summary

Capri Holdings enters fiscal 2027 as a fundamentally different company than the one that agreed to sell itself to Tapestry in 2023. The December 2025 divestiture of Versace to Prada for $1.395 billion (gross) funded roughly $1 billion of debt prepayment, collapsing long-term debt from ~$1.47 billion to ~$343 million and leaving the company with net interest income, a $1.16 billion undrawn revolver, and modest solvency risk. What remains is a two-brand house: Michael Kors (~83% of revenue and essentially all of the profit) and Jimmy Choo (structurally unprofitable). The balance sheet has been de-risked; the earnings-power question has not been resolved.

Revenue has declined for three consecutive years, from $5.62 billion (FY23) to $3.47 billion (FY26), and continues to fall on a continuing-operations basis even after stripping out Versace. FY26 net income of ~$137 million is flattered by the discontinued-operations disposal gain; continuing-operations operating income was only ~$23 million, a 0.7% margin, once Jimmy Choo's ~$22 million operating loss and corporate overhead offset Michael Kors' ~$312 million segment profit. The thesis, as management frames it, is a margin-led EPS rebound driven by store rationalization (139 Michael Kors closures underway), reduced promotional intensity, product elevation, and buyback accretion against a share base of ~115 million, rather than top-line growth.

We rate CPRI a HOLD with an overall score of 4.5. The setup has improved materially from the distressed profile of early 2026, and management is putting capital and personal money behind the turnaround. But the core problem, a structurally over-distributed brand that has lost pricing power, is unproven to be fixable, near-term estimate revisions have rolled over, and Jimmy Choo's return to profitability remains an assumption rather than a track record. Consumer discretionary is among the weakest sectors for stock selection in the current regime, and the two-phase consumer-cliff dynamic argues for patience. We would not chase, and we would want to see evidence of the margin rebuild converting to reported results before upgrading.

## Company Overview

Capri Holdings is incorporated in the British Virgin Islands and now operates two brands following the Versace sale:

- **Michael Kors** — accessible-luxury accessories, footwear, and apparel, ~$2.87 billion of FY26 revenue and the profit center (~10.9% margin). Sold through owned retail, wholesale/department stores, and e-commerce (~21% of net revenue). Sourced primarily from Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia, and Bangladesh, the center of the tariff exposure.
- **Jimmy Choo** — luxury footwear/accessories, ~$600 million revenue, structurally low-margin, and unprofitable.

About 47% of revenue is international. The strategic core issue, implicit throughout the filings, is that Michael Kors became over-distributed and over-promoted, eroding brand equity and pricing power. The standalone strategy is a deliberate shrink-to-fix: store count fell from ~1,003 (FY24) to ~884 (FY26), with the 139 further closures reported in mid-2026. The company competes against Tapestry (Coach/Kate Spade), its failed acquirer and now a stronger standalone rival, on the accessible-luxury tier; against European true-luxury houses on the aspirational end; and against promotional department stores on the value end.

## Financial Analysis

**Revenue trajectory is the central issue.** Total revenue fell from $5.62 billion (FY23) to $5.17 billion (FY24) to $4.44 billion (FY25) to $3.47 billion (FY26). On a continuing-operations basis (ex-Versace), the decline continues, from ~$4.14 billion (FY24) to ~$3.62 billion (FY25) to $3.47 billion (FY26), though the rate of decline is moderating. In the December 2025 quarter (Q3 FY26), continuing-ops revenue of ~$1.03 billion was down ~4% YoY, with Michael Kors off ~5-6% but Jimmy Choo up ~5%, an early stabilization signal that has not yet compounded into a trend.

**Profitability is transitional and messy.** FY25 posted a net loss of ~$1.18 billion, driven by ~$524 million in deferred-tax valuation allowances (a full allowance booked after a three-year cumulative loss), goodwill/intangible impairments, and Versace losses. FY26 returned to net income, but that figure is materially flattered by the Versace disposal gain in discontinued operations. Gross margin remains high (~62%), consistent with a branded model, but Michael Kors gross margin compressed ~290 bps in the December quarter, partly from US import tariffs. The consolidated business is barely profitable once Jimmy Choo's losses and corporate costs eat into Michael Kors' contribution.

**The balance sheet has been de-risked, but the equity base is thin.** Total debt is now ~$357 million; cash ~$135 million; plus $1.16 billion of undrawn revolver capacity on a $1.5 billion facility. The company earns net interest income (~$77 million in FY26), a function of its large net-investment hedge book. Stockholders' equity is only ~$80 million after years of losses, impairments, and buybacks. The resulting Altman Z-Score of ~1.22 screens as "distress," but that reading is a false positive on solvency: it is driven almost entirely by the tiny equity denominator, not by leverage. With modest debt, net interest income, and a large undrawn revolver, near-term bankruptcy risk is low. The real risk is earnings power. We reflect this in a financial-health score of 5.0: de-risked balance sheet, adequate liquidity, but near-zero core operating margin and a thin equity cushion that limits absorption capacity if the turnaround stalls.

**Cash flow and capital allocation.** FY26 operating cash flow was ~$197 million (continuing ops) against ~$63 million capex; FY27 capex is guided up to ~$125 million for store refreshes. A new $1.0 billion, three-year buyback was authorized November 4, 2025, with ~$79 million (3.99 million shares) repurchased in Q4 FY26 and ~$921 million remaining. There is no dividend. Repurchasing stock at ~$16-20 against a ~$80 million equity base and a ~$1.8 billion market cap is aggressive: it signals board confidence and concentrates the upside per share if the plan works, but it also concentrates risk if the turnaround does not.

**Earnings track record is volatile.** Over the last four reported quarters, Capri beat three times and missed once, with wide dispersion (+277% in June 2025, -122% in September 2025, +93% in March 2026). This variance reflects a business in transition and reduces confidence in any single forward estimate.

## Growth Analysis

There is no organic growth thesis here. This is a restructuring and self-help story. The explicit management target is a margin-led EPS rebound driven by cost discipline, store rationalization, reduced promotion, and mix, not top-line expansion. Revenue is still declining, and the plan is to shrink the store base to improve profitability per unit.

The estimate-revision picture is mixed. Over the trailing 90 days, consensus EPS moved up meaningfully (current quarter +26.9%, current year +14.1%, next year +10.8%), consistent with the margin-recovery narrative gaining credibility after the Versace close. But over the trailing 30 days, the trend has rolled over across every window (current quarter -0.5%, next quarter -1.1%, current year -0.5%, next year -0.3%). The 90-day move alone would ordinarily qualify as a meaningful positive-revision signal, but the strongest version of that signal requires acceleration and revenue growth above 30%. Neither condition holds: revisions have decelerated to slightly negative in the most recent month, and revenue is contracting. The signal is therefore weak, and we treat it as a caution about visibility rather than a catalyst.

Jimmy Choo is the swing factor. Its return to profitability is an explicit pillar of the thesis but has no track record. Its ~5% revenue uptick in the December quarter is encouraging, but the segment still posted a ~$22 million operating loss for the year. If Jimmy Choo stays in the red, the consolidated margin-recovery math becomes considerably harder. We score growth prospects at 3.8: a plausible margin-recovery path exists, but there is no revenue growth, the revision trend has softened, and the profitability of the second brand is unproven.

## Valuation Assessment

At $16.16, CPRI trades at ~24.9x trailing earnings on $0.65 trailing EPS. That trailing multiple is not the right anchor for a stock at a transitional earnings trough. On forward consensus, the picture looks less demanding: current-year EPS of $2.13 implies ~7.6x forward, and next-year $2.58 implies ~6.2x. On those numbers, the stock is cheap.

The problem is that "cheap on forward estimates" is precisely the value-trap pattern that has historically sorted the wrong way in consumer discretionary. The forward multiples are only meaningful if the margin-led rebound converts to $2.13, then $2.58, of EPS, and the recent negative 30-day revision trend, the continuing revenue decline, and the unproven Jimmy Choo turnaround all cut against that certainty. Peer multiples reinforce the ambiguity: the accessible-luxury/apparel median P/E is ~16.7x (GIII 12.5x, CRI 16.7x), while several peers (Under Armour, Oxford) carry negative earnings and trade at deep 52-week discounts. Capri's trailing multiple looks rich versus that group; its forward multiple looks cheap. The dispersion tells you the sector is being priced on execution risk, not on stable multiples.

Analyst targets span an unusually wide range: low $16.00, high $45.50, mean $25.09, median $23.00. The width itself communicates that this is a binary turnaround, not a consensus re-rating. The low target sits at the current price; the high implies a full margin recovery the market does not yet believe. We do not treat analyst targets as a floor here because that principle applies to stocks with rising estimates and reasonable multiples, and Capri fails the "rising estimates" test on the 30-day window.

We assign valuation a 4.5. The forward math is genuinely inexpensive if the plan works, but a low multiple on trough earnings with no confirmed revenue stabilization and decelerating near-term revisions is a value-trap warning, not a buy signal. The score reflects real optionality tempered by the requirement for an execution catalyst that has not yet arrived.

## Competitive Landscape

Capri's competitive trajectory is stable-to-deteriorating rather than improving. Michael Kors' problems are partly self-inflicted (over-distribution and over-promotion diluted brand equity and pricing power), and the fix (fewer stores, less promotion, product elevation) mechanically shrinks the revenue base before any brand-equity benefit shows up. There is no proof yet that elevation restores growth; the December-quarter Michael Kors decline of ~5-6% suggests the erosion has slowed but not reversed.

The competitive environment has tightened. Tapestry is now a stronger standalone rival in the same accessible-luxury tier. The US department-store channel remains consolidating and promotional, pressuring both volume and wholesale margin. Jimmy Choo competes at the high end of footwear where scale and profitability are hard to achieve. The broader luxury backdrop of cautious discretionary spending, weaker tourist/luxury spend, and tariff and supply-chain uncertainty is a headwind, but it affects peers similarly; Capri's underperformance is more brand-specific than sector-wide. We score competitive position at 3.8: the moat is compressing, share is not clearly being regained, and the trajectory is toward stabilization at best rather than gains.

## Risk Assessment

The idiosyncratic risks here are numerous and mostly company-specific:

1. **Brand fatigue / revenue erosion at Michael Kors** — the entire thesis rests on stabilizing a brand that has declined for years, with no confirmed inflection.
2. **Tariff exposure** — heavy sourcing from Vietnam/Cambodia/Indonesia/Bangladesh. The February 2026 Supreme Court ruling that IEEPA tariffs were unlawful entitles Capri to a ~$65 million refund (recorded as a receivable, timing uncertain), but new Section 122 tariffs (150 days, effective 2/24/2026) reimpose margin pressure. Policy is live and unpredictable.
3. **Jimmy Choo profitability** — an explicit thesis pillar with no track record.
4. **July 2027 debt maturity / refinancing risk** — flagged as a new risk factor; a June 25, 2026 8-K citing a new material agreement and direct financial obligation may be an early refinancing step worth monitoring for terms.
5. **Wholesale channel decline** — department-store consolidation and markdowns.
6. **Large FX hedge book** — $3.5 billion notional Swiss Franc net-investment hedges (to 2045) and $2.364 billion EUR cross-currency hedges, with credit-support annexes requiring collateral posting from June 2030, a latent, underappreciated liquidity risk.
7. **Securities class-action litigation** tied to Tapestry-merger statements (names CEO Idol and former CFO Edwards); motion to dismiss granted without prejudice 3/31/2026, refiled 4/30/2026, still pending.
8. **Thin equity / valuation-allowance overhang** — the full valuation allowance means little P&L benefit from prior losses until sustained profitability is demonstrated.
9. **BVI incorporation** — weaker minority-shareholder protections.

Several of these are already reflected in the depressed valuation and the improved risk profile post-Versace. On balance, we score risk factors at 4.5: the balance-sheet tail risk has been materially reduced, but the concentration of the thesis on a single unproven turnaround, plus live tariff, litigation, and refinancing overhangs, keeps this below neutral.

### Options Market Signal

The options market is pricing imminent stress. Near-term ATM IV is 67.1% versus 49.7% historical volatility, rich, at the 85th percentile within the current chain, with a backwardated term structure (67.1% near-term versus 56.9% at six months). ATM IV jumped from 49.4% to 67.1% over the past month while the stock fell ~20%, and the put/call OI ratio rose from 0.23 to 0.65. This is consistent with the market pricing a binary event (the next earnings print, expected in the August window) and elevated near-term downside hedging. The implied one-month move is ±15.4%. Put/call skew is roughly balanced (-1.9%), so this is broad uncertainty rather than a directional put pileup, but the rich IV and backwardation confirm that the market views the near term as unresolved, aligned with our HOLD. Short interest is moderate at 8.8% of float with 3.2 days to cover, so squeeze mechanics are not a dominant factor either way.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull case.** Versace is gone, debt is down, and the company now earns net interest income with an undrawn revolver; the solvency scare was a Z-Score artifact of a thin equity base, not real leverage. The margin-led rebound is real: 90-day EPS estimates rose double digits, the CEO bought ~$989K of stock in the open market at $17.98 near the lows, and the board authorized a buyback against the market cap. Store rationalization and reduced promotion lift Michael Kors margin; Jimmy Choo crosses into profitability; buyback accretion compounds against a small share base. If EPS reaches the $2.58 next-year consensus, the stock at ~6x forward is deeply mispriced, and the ~$23-25 median analyst target, let alone the $45.50 high, becomes reachable.

**Bear case.** The margin plan requires stabilizing a brand that has declined for three straight years, and the shrink-to-fix approach mechanically cuts revenue before any equity benefit appears. Continuing-ops operating margin is 0.7%, Jimmy Choo has never been sustainably profitable, and the 30-day estimate trend has already rolled over to slightly negative. Tariffs are a live margin drag, the July 2027 maturity needs refinancing at higher rates, and the consumer discretionary backdrop is weakening. If revenue keeps falling and Jimmy Choo stays in the red, the forward multiple is a mirage: the "cheap" 6x reverts to a much higher number on lower delivered EPS, and the stock retests the low-teens. The aggressive buyback into a stalling turnaround becomes value-destructive.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

This is a HOLD. The recommendation history has moved from three consecutive AVOIDs to HOLD, tracking the improving score trajectory (3.6 → 4.7) as the balance sheet de-risked. The current price (~$16.16) is roughly 17% below the level at the prior review (~$19.47), but that decline coincides with the 30-day estimate rollover and a rich, backwardated IV structure; the risk/reward has not clearly improved because the fundamental turnaround remains unconfirmed and near-term visibility has, if anything, softened.

**Upgrade triggers (to BUY):**
- Two consecutive quarters of Michael Kors revenue stabilization (YoY decline narrowing to flat or better) alongside gross-margin expansion, confirming the elevation strategy is working.
- Jimmy Choo posting a positive segment operating result, removing the largest single drag on consolidated margin.
- 30-day estimate revisions turning positive again to confirm the 90-day trend, ideally with the current-year EPS estimate holding or rising above $2.13.

**Downgrade triggers (to AVOID):**
- Continuing-ops revenue decline re-accelerating, or a guidance cut that signals the margin-recovery plan is stalling.
- Jimmy Choo losses widening, or a material Michael Kors gross-margin miss from tariffs.
- Adverse refinancing terms on the July 2027 maturity (materially higher rate), or a negative development in the pending securities litigation.

**Reassessment timeline:** The next earnings report (expected in the August window, given the late-March fiscal year and the ~August-September options concentration) is the natural checkpoint for whether the margin-led EPS strategy shows up in reported results.

## Conclusion

Capri Holdings has done the hard, unglamorous work of de-risking: shedding Versace, cutting debt, and refocusing on two brands. The solvency scare was always overstated. What remains is a turnaround bet on stabilizing a structurally challenged Michael Kors and coaxing Jimmy Choo to profitability, executed through store closures, reduced promotion, and buyback accretion against a small share base. The CEO's open-market purchase near the lows and the authorization are credible signals of conviction.

But conviction is not proof. Revenue is still falling, core operating margin is near zero, the second brand is unprofitable, and the encouraging 90-day estimate momentum has already decelerated to slightly negative over the past month. The forward multiple looks cheap only if the margin rebuild delivers, the classic value-trap setup in a weakening consumer discretionary tape. We rate CPRI a HOLD, overall score 4.5, with the recommendation contingent on the next print showing that margin recovery is converting to reported results rather than remaining a plan. We would want confirmation before paying up, and we would exit on any evidence the top-line erosion is re-accelerating.
