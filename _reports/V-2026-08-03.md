## Executive Summary

Visa's fiscal third quarter (ended June 30, 2026) delivered net revenue of $11,633M, up 14%, and non-GAAP diluted EPS of $3.32. The share price fell roughly 4% in the session following the release, on operating expense growth of 19% GAAP (including a $563M severance charge) and 17% adjusted, against 14% revenue growth, plus sequential revenue deceleration from 17% in Q2 to 14% in Q3. The stock has since recovered and now sits 1.4% below its 52-week high of $373.97 (reached July 29, 2026).

The fiscal 2026 interim data resolves an important question left open by the FY2025 10-K. FY2025 showed net revenue up 11.3% to $39,976M while GAAP operating income grew 1.7% to $23,994M — a 570bp operating margin contraction to 60.0%. Nine months into FY2026, net revenue is up 15% to $33,764M, net income up 17% to $17,502M, and GAAP diluted EPS up 20% to $9.14, with the quarterly litigation provision stepping down from $615M a year ago to roughly $237–253M. The underlying engine is intact; the FY2025 GAAP margin compression was dominated by a legal charge that is now smaller. Value-added services reached $3.8B in the quarter (+33%) and roughly $10.3B for nine months (+32%), taking VAS to about a third of net revenue at twice the consolidated growth rate.

What keeps this a HOLD for a fifth consecutive review is the combination of price and two legal exposures that a standard data pull does not surface. At $368.775 the stock trades at 27.9x FY2026 consensus of $13.21 and 24.7x FY2027 consensus of $14.95 — a PEG near 1.9 on 13% expected earnings growth, and roughly in line with Mastercard's 28.1x trailing multiple. Estimate revisions are positive but shallow (+1.0% over 90 days on both current and next year), which is not the earnings inflection that justifies paying a premium multiple into a high. Meanwhile, a class action filed April 21, 2026 (the *Potayto-Potahto* matter) directly attacks the validity of the forward-looking release on which the entire U.S. interchange settlement architecture — and the Class B share-release mechanism — depends, and the UK Competition Appeal Tribunal's February 18, 2026 ruling that interchange was not passed on by merchants removes one of Visa's principal quantum defenses in a jurisdiction with no Class B absorber. Neither is priced. Base case fair value of $395 offers 7.1% upside plus a 0.73% dividend, against 18.9% downside if the legal architecture reopens or expense growth persists above revenue growth.

## Company Overview

Visa operates the largest of the open-loop payment networks under what the company calls the four-party model: consumers, issuers, acquirers, and sellers. The 10-K is explicit that Visa "is not a financial institution," does not issue cards, extend credit, or bear credit risk. It sets default interchange reimbursement fees paid by acquirers to issuers but states its own fees to issuers and acquirers are not derived from interchange or merchant discount rates. Visa is a transaction-flow toll-taker with negligible balance-sheet credit exposure — a materially different risk character from most of the Financials sector in which it is classified.

FY2025 scale: 329 billion Visa-branded payments and cash transactions, of which 258 billion (78.4%) were processed on VisaNet; $17 trillion of total payments and cash volume; approximately 5 billion payment credentials; 175 million merchant locations; 14,500 financial-institution clients; 200-plus countries. Tap-to-pay penetration is 79% of global face-to-face transactions but only 66% in the U.S. Workforce grew from roughly 31,600 to 34,100 in FY2025, up 8%.

Revenue is reported in five lines — service, data processing, international transaction (the highest-yield line, tied to cross-border and currency conversion), other, less client incentives as contra-revenue. Growth is organized around consumer payments, commercial and money-movement solutions (Visa Direct processed more than 12.5 billion transactions for more than 650 partners in FY2025), and value-added services. The company's stated addressable markets (>$40T consumer spend, ~$200T of commercial flows, ~$520B of VAS revenue opportunity) are company-sourced estimates built on third-party studies, some dated to 2022. They frame ambition, not contracted revenue, and carry no weight in the valuation work below.

Two capitalization details matter for any per-share work. First, Visa's reported market capitalization of roughly $629.5B reflects Class A shares only (1,704.1M × $368.775 = $628.6B), while reported and consensus EPS use a diluted base of approximately 1.9 billion Class A-equivalent shares including converted Class B and C. On that base, equity value is closer to $700B, and enterprise value — adding roughly $24.1B of debt, netting $12.4B of cash — is near $712B. The internally consistent read is price per Class A share against EPS per Class A-equivalent share, which is what the multiples in this report use. Second, the May 2026 exchange offer (approximately 3M Class B-1 and 120M Class B-2 exchanged for 61M Class B-3 and 23M Class C) reduced the as-converted count from about 1,930M to 1,880M, a 2.6% reduction that a conventional buyback screen will not capture. Whether the exchange ratios were accretive or dilutive to Class A holders is not something I can assess from available disclosure.

## Financial Analysis

**Revenue and margin.** FY2025: net revenue $39,976M (+11.3%), operating income $23,994M (+1.7%), income before tax $24,194M (+1.2%), tax $4,136M, derived net income $20,058M (+1.6%). Operating margin fell from 65.7% to 60.0%. Nine months FY2026: net revenue $33,764M (+15%), net income $17,502M (+17%), GAAP diluted EPS $9.14 (+20%), non-GAAP diluted EPS $9.79 (+15%).

Two forces explain the FY2025-to-FY2026 pattern. The litigation provision, which Visa carries through operating expense, was the dominant swing factor in FY2025 — accrued litigation stood at $3,033M at September 30, 2025, and the quarterly provision has since fallen from $615M to roughly $237–253M. I have not read the specific FY2025 income-statement litigation line, so this attribution is a supported inference rather than a verified figure. The second force is structural: client incentives were $4.7B in Q3 FY2026 against $11,633M of net revenue, implying gross revenue of roughly $16.3B and incentives at approximately 28.8% of gross. Incentives are what Visa pays issuers, acquirers, merchants, and partners to direct volume to its network. They have grown faster than gross revenue and compress net take rate independently of any legal outcome.

**Expenses.** Q3 GAAP operating expense rose 19% to $4.8B including $563M of severance; adjusted expense rose 17%; personnel expense rose approximately 40%; marketing rose approximately 54% on FIFA World Cup 2026 and Milano Cortina Winter Olympics activations. The marketing step-up is calendar-driven — both events fall inside FY2026 — and should not recur at the same magnitude in FY2027. The severance charge is non-recurring by nature and follows an 8% FY2025 headcount build, which is at least consistent with a correction to over-hiring, though the 10-Q does not attribute it that way and AI-driven role redesign or footprint changes are equally plausible. The number I cannot dismiss is 17% adjusted expense growth against 14% revenue growth. Whether that gap closes in FY2027 is the single most important near-term variable for the multiple, and I have no management guidance in hand to resolve it.

**Cash flow and balance sheet.** Nine-month operating cash flow was $16,342M, down 2.8% despite net income up 17%. The bridge is litigation cash: roughly $3.0B was paid to opt-out merchants and the injunctive relief class, drawing accrued litigation down from $3,033M to $1,274M and the escrow account from $2,990M to $888M. These are settlement payments against previously provisioned amounts, not fresh expense. Cash fell from approximately $17.2B to $12.4B. Total debt is roughly $24.1B; Visa repaid $5.6B and issued $3.0B in February 2026 at coupons of 3.80%–4.70%, materially above the legacy stack, so interest expense will drift higher as pre-2022 maturities roll. In July 2026 the commercial paper authorization was raised from $3.0B to $7.0B — most plausibly liquidity flexibility around the litigation payout schedule and the pending $1.5B Argentina acquisition, though it is also consistent with larger near-term cash needs. I would not infer management intent from the authorization.

Nine-month GAAP effective tax was approximately 15.6%, helped by a $351M discrete deferred benefit. Do not extrapolate that rate.

Retained earnings fell from $17,289M to $15,106M in FY2025 and stockholders' equity declined to roughly $35.2B by June 30, 2026, consistent with returning more than 100% of net income through dividends and repurchases. For a business with minimal capital intensity this is a deliberate posture, not distress, but it means conventional equity-based leverage ratios will deteriorate mechanically over time. [The free-cash-flow yield comparison in this paragraph rested on a capital-expenditure figure and a full-year free-cash-flow estimate not present in the research brief or Phase 1 data, and a specific Treasury yield that is also not in the sources; those figures have been removed.] Full-year free cash flow is substantial given negligible capital intensity, but a precise free-cash-flow yield is not derivable from the provided sources.

Financial health scores 7.5: exceptional margin and cash generation, negligible credit risk, ample liquidity, offset by shrinking book equity, litigation cash outflows suppressing reported operating cash flow, and a rising cost of debt.

**Earnings delivery.** The four-quarter record is two beats and two misses: −1.5% (Sept 2025), −1.0% (Dec 2025), +4.8% (Mar 2026), +0.8% (June 2026). One caveat on the most recent quarter — the surprise table shows $3.32 against a $3.29 estimate (+0.8%), while secondary coverage cited a $3.23 consensus (+2.8%). The magnitude of the beat depends on which consensus you use; either way it was modest, and this is not a company that reliably clears estimates by 5–10%. That argues for treating current consensus as approximately fair rather than conservative.

## Growth Analysis

The reacceleration in FY2026 is real but decelerating within the year: 17% net revenue growth in Q2 to 14% in Q3. Consensus has FY2026 at $13.21 and FY2027 at $14.95, implying 13.1% EPS growth. Revisions are positive and consistent — current quarter −0.3% over 90 days, next quarter +0.8%, current year +1.0%, next year +1.0% — but shallow. This is not the estimate-revision inflection that would justify paying up; it is a stable compounder whose consensus is grinding higher by a point a quarter.

The mix shift is the more interesting growth story. VAS at $3.8B in Q3 (+33%) and roughly $10.3B for nine months (+32%) is running at about a third of net revenue and roughly 2.3x consolidated growth. The strategic logic is that Visa sells risk, identity, tokenization, and orchestration services to the same real-time-payment and account-to-account networks that compete with its card rails. The 10-K supports this framing directly: RTP networks "can compete with Visa on consumer payments and other payment flows... but can also be customers for value-added services, such as risk management."

Two measurement disciplines apply. First, rising VAS mix demonstrates deeper monetization per client and diversification of revenue *type*; it does not establish diversification of *customer* concentration, because VAS is largely sold to the same large issuers and acquirers that already generate core payment revenue. Visa's risk factors state that a significant portion of net revenue is concentrated among its largest clients; the specific disclosure is not verified here, and I do not treat the VAS mix as evidence of reduced concentration. Second, remaining performance obligations of $5.6B at June 30, 2026 are not backlog. Under ASC 606 they exclude wholly unperformed cancellable contracts and variable consideration tied to future volume, which is the overwhelming majority of Visa's economics. RPO is not a demand indicator for the network.

Agentic commerce is the most-discussed growth vector and the least investable near term. Visa and OpenAI expanded a partnership announced June 10, 2026 under which Visa supplies tokenized credentialing and real-time fraud monitoring into OpenAI's agent products; Visa has launched an Agentic Directory, Agent Score, Large Transaction Model, and Trusted Agent Protocol, with Cleverbridge and eDreams ODIGEO among early adopters. Management cited $3–5T of addressable volume on the Q3 call. Against that, secondary reporting indicates only 14% of consumers trust AI to execute purchases without verification, 42% refuse above $25, and OpenAI scaled back Instant Checkout in March 2026 with in-chat conversion around a third of on-site levels and roughly a dozen live Shopify merchants. Those adoption datapoints come from a single secondary aggregation and are unverified, but the direction is consistent with a standards-setting phase rather than a volume phase. I assign agentic commerce no weight in the FY2027 numbers and treat it as a long-dated option.

Latin American processing M&A is the more tangible strategic move: $1.5B for Prisma and Newpay in Argentina (February 2026, pending competition review), the pending Prosa majority interest in Mexico, plus completed Featurespace and Pismo. Buying the processing layer in cash-heavy, RTP-exposed markets is a coherent defense against PIX-style disintermediation, and the sizes are immaterial to the balance sheet.

Growth prospects score 6.5: durable low-to-mid-teens EPS compounding with a genuine VAS mix tailwind, tempered by sequential deceleration, incentive-driven net take-rate compression, and a macro backdrop that pressures discretionary volume and cross-border travel.

Visa therefore trades at parity-to-slight-premium versus the single relevant comparable, with no growth differential in its favor.

Analyst positioning is constructive — 9 strong buy, 29 buy, 3 hold, mean target $413.45 (+12.1%), median $420 (+13.9%), range $330 to $450. On genuinely inflecting stocks with rapidly rising estimates, consensus targets tend to lag and function as floors. That does not apply here: +1.0% ninety-day revisions on a stock 1.4% from its high is not an inflection, and at 25–28x forward the target and the price largely reflect the same extrapolation. I treat the target distribution as corroborating context, not independent evidence.

The base case: 26.5x FY2027 EPS of $14.95 equals $396, rounded to $395, or 7.1% above the current price. The multiple choice reflects the low end of Visa's recent trading band, which is where I think a business with 28.8%-of-gross incentive drag, an open expense-leverage question, and two unresolved legal binaries should clear. Bull case: 30x FY2027 equals $449 (+21.8%), consistent with the top of the analyst range and requiring FY2027 operating leverage to reappear (adjusted expense growth below revenue growth) plus final settlement approval surviving the release challenge. Bear case: 20x FY2027 equals $299 (−18.9%), reached if the forward-looking release is invalidated, or if incentives and expenses cap EPS growth in the high single digits while long-duration multiples compress.

Valuation scores 5.0. This is a high-quality asset at a fair-to-full price. There is no identified mispricing, and cheapness is not the issue — the issue is that 7% upside to a reasonable base case against 19% downside on identifiable binaries is not an asymmetry worth new capital.

## Competitive Landscape

The 10-K's own competitive table (Nilson Report 1288, calendar 2024) puts Visa payments volume at $13,433B against Mastercard's $8,014B, transactions at 311B versus 204B, and cards at 4,805M versus 3,146M. Against American Express ($1,750B), JCB ($319B), and Diners/Discover ($253B) the gap is 8–50x. On level, the position is dominant.

Trajectory is where the score comes down from where this franchise historically sat. Four observations:

**Routing leakage is quantified and rarely cited.** Of 329B Visa-branded transactions in FY2025, 258B ran on VisaNet — roughly 71B, or 21.6%, were processed elsewhere, where Visa may earn service revenue but not data processing revenue. Part of that gap is mechanically unavailable (cash and ATM transactions on other networks), so 21.6% overstates the contestable pool. But this ratio is the precise variable on which the Credit Card Competition Act, DOJ debit remedies, and further Interchange Fee Regulation enforcement operate. Tracking it year over year is a cleaner leading indicator of regulatory damage than headline volume.

**Bargaining power is eroding at the margin.** Incentives at 28.8% of gross revenue, growing faster than gross revenue, is the clearest quantifiable expression of large issuers and acquirers extracting Visa's economics as the price of distribution.

**The contest is increasingly not against card networks.** Four of the seven competitor categories the 10-K names are non-card: local and regional debit networks (often government-mandated), alternative payment providers including BNPL and stablecoins, RTP networks live in at least 80 countries with government sponsorship (FedNow, PIX, UPI), and wallets fundable off non-card rails. Visa explicitly concedes that emerging cross-border RTP linkages such as PayNow–UPI "will compete with our cross-border business" — the highest-yield revenue line. In Europe, the IFR has already permitted processors to process Visa transactions directly with issuers and acquirers, unbundling scheme from processing economics. Capital One's ownership of Discover creates a vertically integrated U.S. issuer-plus-network that could migrate its own volume off Visa rails over time; the filing does not quantify this.

**Stablecoin exposure is real and currently immaterial.** More than $100B of crypto/stablecoin purchases facilitated since 2020, more than $35B of spend through crypto-linked credentials, four stablecoins across four blockchains, and stablecoin settlement at a $2.5B annualized run rate as of September 30, 2025 — against $17T of total volume.

Competitive position scores 6.9: an unassailable installed base and a credible strategy of selling infrastructure to disintermediators, with measurable erosion in processed-transaction share and net take rate. Government restrictions in China, India, Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, and South Africa, plus the exclusion of Russia and China from Visa's own addressable-market framing, cap the geographic runway.

## Risk Assessment

**1. The challenge to the forward-looking release (highest-weighted, unpriced).** The FY2025 10-K discloses approximately $39.4B of interchange at issue in unresolved claims, down from roughly $49.6B — the metric that governs Class B share release. A superseding amended settlement with the Injunctive Relief Class was filed November 10, 2025, preliminary approval was granted June 9, 2026, and a final approval motion was filed July 15, 2026; Visa has settled with opt-out merchants representing roughly 95% of the opt-out group's Visa-branded volume. Against that progress, a class action filed April 21, 2026 on behalf of merchants from January 25, 2019 forward specifically challenges the validity of the forward-looking release. The settlement's economic logic and the Class B release mechanism both depend on that release holding. The case is at the earliest stage, such challenges frequently fail, and a court has already granted preliminary approval to a structure containing the release — I assign no probability, and no court has ruled on the merits. But this is a low-probability, high-impact item that carries no visible discount in the price.

One clarification on widely reported figures: media descriptions of a roughly "$38 billion" settlement most plausibly describe estimated aggregate merchant savings from interchange reductions over the settlement term, not a Visa cash payment. The verifiable cash items are the roughly $3.0B paid in nine months FY2026, $1,274M of accrued litigation, and $888M in escrow at June 30, 2026.

**2. European interchange litigation, structurally worse than U.S.** On February 18, 2026 the UK Competition Appeal Tribunal ruled that interchange was not passed on by merchants (with limited exceptions), which removes Visa's pass-on defense and is adverse on damages quantum; Visa has sought permission to appeal. New UK High Court claims from European merchants were filed April–June 2026. Europe sits outside the Class B mechanism, so European liabilities fall directly on Visa Inc. common shareholders rather than being absorbed through Class B conversion-rate adjustments. Investors who net all interchange litigation against the Class B absorber are making an error. No European provision amount is verified here.

**3. U.S. debit regulation.** DOJ scrutiny of debit dominance and the Credit Card Competition Act operate on the routing ratio above. I found no 10-Q update on the DOJ civil suit's procedural posture; the separate U.S. Debit Class Actions saw amended complaints in February 2026. Status is a verification gap.

**4. Expense leverage.** Adjusted operating expense growth of 17% against 14% revenue growth is the variable most likely to move the multiple in the next two quarters. If FY2027 guidance shows the severance reset producing leverage, the bull case opens; if it does not, 24.7x forward is difficult to defend.

**5. Brazil settlement guarantees.** A new regulatory requirement to extend settlement guarantees to sellers increases Visa's settlement exposure, moving a business built on minimal balance-sheet risk incrementally toward counterparty risk. Small today; worth watching for precedent.

**6. Macro.** Cross-border and travel volumes are the highest-yield line and the most cyclical, and they are the most exposed to a consumer slowdown. [The specific mortgage-rate, crude-price, recession-probability, and Treasury-yield figures previously cited here are not present in the research brief or Phase 1 data and have been removed.] A macro backdrop of a consumer slowdown and elevated long-term rates would pressure discretionary volume and be a discount-rate headwind for a premium-multiple compounder, but the specific figures cannot be sourced.

**7. Resolved favorably.** The U.S. securities class action was dismissed without leave to amend on June 29, 2026 — a favorable and largely unremarked development.

Risk factors score 5.3: two live legal binaries with asymmetric downside and no visible discount, quantified routing leakage, unverified client concentration, and three circulating share-count constructs that complicate per-share modeling.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case — $449 (+21.8%)

Visa compounds mid-teens EPS on a network that no competitor can replicate at scale, and the market is under-appreciating three things. First, the FY2025 GAAP margin compression was a litigation artifact now stepping down from $615M to roughly $240M per quarter, while nine-month FY2026 GAAP EPS grew 20%. Second, VAS at 33% growth and roughly a third of revenue converts the disintermediation threat into a revenue line — Visa sells risk, tokenization, and orchestration to the RTP and A2A networks competing with its card rails, and the sell side is beginning to reframe VAS as offensive rather than defensive. Third, the FY2026 expense base contains identifiable non-recurring items: $563M of severance and a marketing step-up from two once-per-cycle sporting events, both of which reverse in FY2027. If final settlement approval holds and the release challenge fails, Class B releases proceed, the litigation overhang narrows, and the as-converted share count continues shrinking (already down 2.6% via the May 2026 exchange offer) on top of conventional repurchases. Thirty times FY2027 EPS of $14.95 yields $449, at the top of the analyst range.

### Bear Case — $299 (−18.9%)

The margin question is not just litigation. Client incentives at 28.8% of gross revenue and growing faster than gross revenue are a structural transfer of Visa's economics to large issuers and acquirers, and adjusted expense growth of 17% against 14% revenue growth suggests the cost base is compounding faster than the toll. Sequential deceleration from 17% to 14% arrives just as the consumer chain tightens. On the legal side, the *Potayto-Potahto* challenge to the forward-looking release, if successful, reopens the $39.4B interchange framework and the Class B mechanism simultaneously, and the UK's no-pass-on ruling raises European damages exposure that falls entirely on common shareholders. Visa's own proxy shows five-year TSR of $176.94 versus $245.77 for its selected peer group — the compounder has already lost roughly 69 index points of relative ground, which is what incentive erosion and litigation overhang look like in returns. Derating to 20x FY2027 yields $299, still above the 52-week low.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Recommendation: HOLD (growth hold)** — the 12-month thesis is positive (13% EPS growth, VAS mix, litigation provision stepping down), but the entry price does not compensate for the legal binaries.

**Upgrade to BUY on any of:**
1. Price in the low $320s, approximately 21.5x FY2027 consensus and roughly 13% below the current level — the point at which base-case upside exceeds 20%.
2. Final approval of the amended settlement granted with the *Potayto-Potahto* release challenge dismissed or resolved in Visa's favor. This removes the largest unpriced binary and clarifies the Class B release path.
3. FY2027 guidance (expected with the fiscal Q4 print in late October 2026) showing adjusted operating expense growth below net revenue growth, with client incentives stabilizing at or below approximately 29% of gross revenue for two consecutive quarters.

**Downgrade to AVOID on any of:**
1. A court ruling invalidating the forward-looking release, or denial of final settlement approval.
2. Client incentives exceeding roughly 31% of gross revenue for two consecutive quarters with net revenue growth below 10%.
3. UK appeal permission denied combined with a disclosed European litigation provision above $2B.
4. Visa-processed share of branded transactions falling below approximately 76% in the FY2026 10-K (from 78.4% in FY2025), which would indicate routing mandates are biting.

**Reassessment timeline:** fiscal Q4 2026 results and FY2027 guidance in late October 2026 is the primary checkpoint — it resolves the expense-leverage question and provides the first management framing of FY2027. The settlement final-approval ruling is an unscheduled catalyst that could arrive sooner.

**Position guidance for existing holders:** hold. Dividend coverage is strong (roughly 20% payout), the balance sheet is unencumbered, and the compounding is intact. New capital should wait for $340 or below, with the low $320s the preferred zone.

## Conclusion

This is the fifth consecutive HOLD on Visa, and the score has drifted from 7.2 in late April to 6.2 now. The drift is not driven by earnings deterioration, since fiscal 2026 growth accelerated relative to FY2025. It reflects three specific pieces of evidence accumulated across those reviews: quantified erosion in Visa's share of processed transactions and net take rate (21.6% of branded transactions off VisaNet, incentives at 28.8% of gross revenue), the emergence of a legal challenge that attacks the architecture on which the U.S. settlement and Class B release depend, and an adverse UK ruling in a jurisdiction with no Class B absorber. Each argues for a lower multiple than a franchise of this quality would otherwise command, and none of the three is visible in the price at 24.7x FY2027 earnings, 1.4% below the 52-week high.

The offsetting case is real. Visa generates substantial free cash flow with almost no capital intensity, carries no credit risk, is shrinking its as-converted share count through both conventional repurchases and the Class B exchange mechanism, and has a VAS business growing 33% that converts the disintermediation threat into revenue. Consensus estimates are rising, if only by a point a quarter, and the analyst distribution sits 12–14% above spot. I am below that consensus because the multiple already embeds continued execution, because the two beats and two misses in the last four quarters argue current estimates are approximately fair rather than conservative, and because the asymmetry at this price — 7% to base case against 19% to bear — is not attractive enough to add. Own it, monitor the release challenge and FY2027 expense guidance, and put new capital to work in the low $320s.

**Thesis:** Visa remains a toll-taking network with negligible credit risk compounding low-to-mid-teens EPS, with VAS at roughly a third of revenue growing 33% and the litigation provision stepping down from $615M to about $240M a quarter. The problem is price and legal architecture: at 24.7x FY2027 the multiple embeds continued execution while a challenge to the forward-looking release and adverse UK pass-on ruling carry no visible discount.

**Variant view:** Consensus treats US interchange litigation as converging and nets it against the Class B absorber. Europe sits outside that mechanism, and the April 2026 release challenge attacks the settlement's own architecture. Also, incentives near 29% of gross revenue are a structural take-rate leak, not litigation noise.
