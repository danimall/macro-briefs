**Research Date: July 21, 2026**

## Executive Summary

D.R. Horton is the largest U.S. homebuilder by volume — number one every year since 2002 — operating in 126 markets across 36 states with a structural tilt toward the entry-level and first-time move-up buyer. The buyer segment DHI dominates is the most rate-sensitive in housing, and with a higher-for-longer Fed path now the base case, DHI is sustaining volume by spending heavily on mortgage rate buydowns. The cost of that demand shows up in two places: homebuilding gross margin fell to 20.1% in Q2 FY2026 (from 21.8% a year earlier, versus mid-20s peak-era margins), and the captive mortgage arm's pre-tax margin dropped to 26.8% from 34.3%. Reading only the homebuilding line understates the cost of moving inventory.

The franchise is not in question. DHI carries an Altman Z-Score around 3.3, is investment-grade rated by all three agencies, runs a land-light model with 77% of lots controlled via options rather than owned, and has retired roughly 20% of its share count since FY2022. This is a demand-and-margin downturn, not a balance-sheet or solvency problem. The July 21, 2026 Q3 print crystallized the debate: DHI beat on EPS but cut full-year revenue guidance to roughly $32.75B midpoint (about a 3% miss to consensus), and press reports cited a cancellation rate jumping to ~20% from 16-17% in Q2. The strong order and backlog growth (net orders +11%, backlog +19% units) is being bought, not organically driven, and the guidance cut confirms it is not converting to revenue as hoped.

At ~$145 and roughly 13.5x trailing earnings — in line with the homebuilding peer median of 13.6x — DHI is neither cheap nor expensive for where it sits in the cycle. Consensus current-year EPS of $10.58 has drifted up only modestly (+2.5% over 90 days) and next-quarter estimates are flat, so there is no earnings-inflection signal to lean on. The buyback provides a per-share floor, but it is being funded by drawing down cash (net debt/total capital rose from 11.0% to 16.4% in six months) while spec inventory builds. We rate DHI HOLD. The risk/reward is roughly balanced: genuine mortgage-rate relief would re-rate the group, while a deeper affordability squeeze plus rising cancellations and a sunsetting tax credit keeps near-term earnings under pressure.

## Company Overview

D.R. Horton operates four reportable segments:

- **Homebuilding (~92% of revenue):** Single-family detached (85% of home sales) plus townhomes and duplexes, at an average closing price of ~$363,500 in the first half of FY2026. The product spans roughly $200K to $1M+, skewed to the affordable end.
- **Financial Services:** DHI Mortgage plus title, financing ~81% of DHI's homebuyers. This is both a profit contributor and the mechanism through which DHI delivers rate buydowns.
- **Forestar (62%-owned, publicly traded as FOR):** Residential lot developer, the core of the land-light strategy. DHI controls 77% of its lots via option contracts with $26.7B remaining purchase price controlled against only $2.5B of earnest money at risk.
- **Rental:** Single-family build-to-rent and multi-family, built and sold in bulk to investors.

The competitive edges are durable: scale-driven purchasing leverage with subcontractors and suppliers, geographic diversification, the land-light model that limits impairment risk in a downturn, captive mortgage/title vertical integration that lets DHI control the rate-buydown lever directly, and an affordable product focus that keeps volume flowing. The trade-off is that the affordable-buyer skew makes DHI more rate-sensitive than luxury-tilted peers, so it must spend more per unit on incentives.

## Financial Analysis

DHI is in a demand-driven downturn off the 2022 peak. Revenue fell from $36.8B in FY2024 to $34.25B in FY2025 (-6.9%), and net income dropped from $4.81B to $3.62B (-24.6%). Pre-tax income declined to $4.74B from $6.28B. The trajectory is driven by affordability and the cost of buying demand.

The Q2 FY2026 quarter (ended March 31, 2026) showed revenue of $7.56B (-2% YoY), net income to DHI of $647.9M (-20%), and diluted EPS of $2.24 versus $2.58. The margin detail:

- Home sales gross margin 20.1%; the six-month figure was 20.3% versus 22.3%. About 200 bps of compression came from rising average cost plus falling ASP, partly offset by a favorable litigation outcome and lower warranty costs. Sales incentives, including mortgage rate buydowns, are the primary driver.
- Financial Services pre-tax margin fell on "reduced profit margin on loans sold" — the same buydown strategy showing up a second time.
- Homebuilding SG&A rose to 9.2% of revenue from 8.9% as revenue fell faster than fixed cost — operating deleverage.

The leading indicators are encouraging in isolation: net sales orders +11% (24,992 homes), order value +10% to $9.2B, backlog +19% units and +17% value ($6.4B). But the Q3 guidance cut and the cancellation rate indicate this order strength is not converting cleanly.

The balance sheet is a clear strength. Debt/total capital is 21.7%, net debt/total capital 16.4%, against a ~20% long-term target. Total notes payable are $6.56B against stockholders' equity of $23.6B; book value is $82.91/share. The company repurchased ~30.7M shares for ~$4.3B in FY2025 and another 10.4M for $1.6B in the first half of FY2026 ($1.7B remaining on the $5.0B authorization), taking share count from ~354M in FY2022 to ~285M today. The dividend was raised to $0.45/quarter from $0.40.

The watch item: cash fell to $1.92B from $2.99B at September 2025, with homebuilding cash just $1.1B, as buybacks and inventory build were partly debt-funded. Homes in inventory rose to 38,200 from 29,600, including ~22,900 unsold and ~5,500 completed specs. This is strategic (start specs to capture demand), but it thins the buffer and adds impairment exposure if demand weakens. FCF conversion is being deliberately traded for share-count reduction; the balance sheet can absorb it, but it is not free.

## Growth Analysis

The growth picture is negative in the near term and cyclically dependent thereafter. Net income has declined for three consecutive years off the 2022 peak, and FY2026 will mark a fourth down year: consensus current-year EPS sits at $10.58, and the guidance cut points to continued pressure. Estimate revisions are stable rather than rising — current-year EPS up just +2.5% over 90 days, next-quarter flat, next-year +1.4%. There is no earnings-inflection signal here; the strong-BUY revision pattern (>30% in 90 days) that would flag a cyclical trough is absent.

The order and backlog growth is the one bullish thread, but it is manufactured with incentives and partially offset by rising cancellations. Two structural headwinds compound the cyclical drag:

- **The 45L energy-efficient home tax credit sunsets June 30, 2026.** It provided $39.5M of benefit in FY2025 and $70.4M in FY2024. The effective tax rate already rose to 24.4% (6mo FY26) from 23.2% and will step up further — a structural EPS drag independent of operating performance that consensus may under-weight.
- **Tariffs on steel, aluminum, and lumber** are a newly emphasized cost risk in the FY2025 10-K, naming the current administration. This layers input-cost pressure onto the affordability squeeze.

The upside case for growth is the rate cycle, not company execution. If mortgage rates fall, incentive spend eases, margins recover toward the mid-20s, and volume normalizes without subsidy. That is a macro bet, not a DHI-specific catalyst, and the current higher-for-longer path argues the near-term direction is the wrong way.

## Valuation Assessment

At ~$145, DHI trades at ~13.5x trailing EPS of $11.57 and roughly the same on forward, essentially in line with the homebuilding peer median of 13.6x (PHM 12.0x, LEN 13.6x, NVR 15.8x, TOL 11.1x). It is not cheap relative to the group, and where it screens slightly below NVR is explained by its higher rate-sensitivity and thinner margin cushion, not mispricing.

I resist treating the multiple as a value signal in either direction. A homebuilder at trough-ish margins can look optically cheap on trailing earnings that are about to fall further — the classic value trap — and DHI's forward consensus EPS of ~$10.58 is below the trailing $11.57, so the "13.5x" understates the forward multiple. Consensus targets (mean $168, median $173, high $206, low $123) sit well above spot, and the low-$120s target roughly matches book value plus a cyclical cushion. But with estimate revisions flat rather than rising and a fresh guidance cut, I do not treat the consensus mean as a floor here; the analyst-target-as-floor logic applies to inflection stocks with rising estimates, which DHI is not.

The valuation case is best framed as: normalized mid-cycle earnings power (if rates ease and margins revert to mid-20s) supports something closer to the mean target, while a prolonged affordability squeeze with incentives "elevated through FY2026" per management supports the low-$120s. That is a symmetric setup around the current price. The dividend yield of ~1.2% is not a support. On balance, valuation is fair, not compelling — a middling score reflecting that the multiple is neither stretched nor offering asymmetric upside without a rate catalyst the company does not control.

## Competitive Landscape

DHI's competitive position is stable-to-strong and largely insulated from the cyclical downturn, which is sector-wide rather than company-specific. Its structural edges — scale (best subcontractor/supplier leverage), the land-light model (77% optioned lots, lowest impairment exposure among large builders), captive mortgage (direct control of the buydown lever), and affordable product mix (keeps orders +11% even as ASPs compress) — remain intact. DHI has held its number-one volume position every year since 2002.

The affordable-buyer skew delivers volume resilience but requires more incentive spend per unit than luxury-tilted peers like Toll Brothers. In this rate environment, DHI's cost of holding share is visibly higher, which is why gross margin sits at 20.1% while less rate-exposed peers hold up better on margin. This is a share-preservation trade, not share erosion — DHI is spending margin to protect volume, the rational move for the volume leader.

The prior score trajectory marked competitive position down from 8.5 to 6.8 over recent reports, which appears to conflate cyclical margin compression with competitive deterioration. The moat itself has not narrowed; the cycle is forcing DHI to monetize less of its structural advantage. I hold competitive position at a moderate level — the franchise is intact, but the current environment prevents it from translating into superior returns.

## Risk Assessment

The dominant risk is affordability and higher-for-longer mortgage rates, the swing factor for earnings. Management explicitly expects incentives to remain elevated through FY2026, capping margins. Secondary risks that the market may under-weight:

- **The margin cost hidden in two segments.** The rate-buydown strategy compresses both homebuilding gross margin and financial services margin. A segment-blind read misses the full cost.
- **Rising cancellations.** Order strength is bought, not organic; the guidance cut confirms the conversion gap.
- **Tariffs** on steel/aluminum/lumber — a fresh 2025 cost risk.
- **45L tax credit sunset** — a structural EPS drag pushing the tax rate above 24.4%.
- **Construction-defect self-insurance** since June 2021, with reserves rising to ~$1.14B (~860 pending claims, 99% construction-defect related) and "high variability" in estimation — a slow-burn tail unique to a builder of DHI's scale.
- **Forestar related-party litigation.** A Mississippi PERS derivative suit in Delaware Chancery (April 2025) alleges breach of fiduciary duty in lot-sale transactions between DHI and its own 62%-owned subsidiary — the structural conflict at the heart of the land-light model. Not yet deemed material, but it puts governance of the core capital-efficiency strategy under scrutiny.
- **Mortgage repurchase facility renewal** ($1.4B, matured May 6, 2026; management expected to renew) — routine but a live near-term item.
- **Thinner cash buffer** as buybacks and inventory build draw down cash.

Offsetting these is balance-sheet resilience (Z ~3.3, IG-rated, expanded $3.295B revolver extended to 2029/2031), which removes solvency from the risk equation. The affordability squeeze and cyclicality are permanently priced into homebuilders. What is less priced is the 45L step-down and the cumulative margin cost of sustained buydowns.

### Options Market Signal

The options market is pricing near-term stress around the July print. Near-term ATM IV of 49.0% sits rich to 1-year historical vol of 38.7%, at the 69th percentile of the current chain, and the term structure is in backwardation (49.0% near vs 40.9% at six months). The put/call volume ratio of 5.13 and OI ratio of 2.05 (up from 0.93 five weeks ago) show meaningful put demand, consistent with hedging into the guidance cut. Skew is roughly balanced (-0.0% near-term), so this reads as event hedging rather than a strong directional bearish bet. IV has already collapsed from 91.4% on June 12 to 49.0%, so the event premium is largely priced. For a HOLD, the takeaway is that the market expects volatility but is not signaling directional conviction that contradicts the fundamental read.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull Case ($195-206):** Mortgage rates fall — whether from a dovish Fed pivot or softening inflation — and incentive spend rolls off. Homebuilding gross margin recovers toward the mid-20s, financial services margin normalizes, and the +11% order growth converts to revenue and earnings without subsidy. Continued aggressive buybacks amplify EPS recovery. On normalized earnings power at a 14-15x multiple, DHI approaches the high analyst target.

**Bear Case (~$120-123):** Higher-for-longer rates persist, cancellations climb further, and spec inventory (38,200 homes, ~5,500 completed) forces impairments or deeper discounting. Margins grind below 20%, the 45L sunset and tariffs add cost pressure, and EPS falls further. At ~13x, that supports the low-$120s, roughly the analyst low and a modest premium to book value plus cyclical cushion.

**Base Case (~$150-155):** Rates hold roughly where they are through FY2026, incentives stay elevated as management guides, and earnings continue their gradual decline into the guidance-cut trajectory. Buybacks provide an EPS floor around consensus $10.58. The stock trades at a market multiple, range-bound near current levels until a rate catalyst resolves the setup in either direction.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

This is a HOLD with an uncertain rationale — the evidence does not support a confident directional call in either direction, and the setup is symmetric around ~$145. Reassessment follows the rate cycle and the next earnings prints.

**Upgrade triggers (to BUY):**
- 30-year mortgage rate falls meaningfully and holds, or estimate revisions turn positive (+10% or more over 90 days on current-year EPS), signaling margin recovery ahead.
- Home sales gross margin stabilizes and inflects back above 21% for two consecutive quarters, confirming incentive spend is rolling off.
- A pullback toward the low-$120s (analyst low / book-plus-cushion) with fundamentals intact, which would shift risk/reward asymmetrically favorable.

**Downgrade triggers (to AVOID):**
- Cancellation rate rises above ~25% or net orders turn negative YoY, indicating demand is deteriorating faster than incentives can offset.
- Gross margin falls below 19% alongside a further guidance cut, signaling the margin vise (buydowns + tariffs + 45L) is overwhelming volume gains.
- Cash buffer erodes further (homebuilding cash below ~$0.8B) while buybacks continue, or spec inventory impairments begin — signs the balance sheet is being stretched to defend EPS.

**Reassessment timeline:** Next reassessment at Q4 FY2026 earnings (fiscal year-end September 30, 2026) and around any material shift in the Fed rate path. The 45L tax-credit sunset (June 30, 2026) is already in the data and should be watched for its full-year tax-rate impact in the FY2026 close.

## Conclusion

D.R. Horton is a best-in-class, fortress-balance-sheet homebuilder navigating a cyclical, macro-driven affordability downturn. The debate is cycle timing and margin trajectory, not solvency or franchise quality, both of which are strong. Earnings are declining (net income -25% off peak, four straight down years likely) because DHI is buying orders with rate buydowns that compress both homebuilding and mortgage margins. The guidance cut, rising cancellations, tariff cost pressure, and the sunsetting 45L credit all point to continued near-term earnings pressure, while aggressive buybacks provide an EPS floor funded by a thinning cash cushion.

At ~13.5x on a market multiple in line with peers, with flat estimate revisions and no earnings-inflection signal, the stock offers neither compelling upside nor obvious downside without a rate catalyst DHI does not control. The overall score of 5.65 reflects a high-quality operator in a difficult environment, priced fairly for that reality. We rate DHI **HOLD**. The most attractive entry would be a pullback toward the low-$120s or a confirmed rate-relief catalyst that reverses the margin compression; the most concerning developments would be accelerating cancellations, sub-19% gross margins, or further balance-sheet strain to defend EPS.
