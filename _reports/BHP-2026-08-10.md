## Executive Summary

BHP is a high-quality, low-leverage, first-quartile mining business whose share price has moved further than its cash flows. The ADS has appreciated 73.5% over the trailing twelve months and now sits 3.1% below its 52-week high. On the reconciled per-ADS basis, that price equates to roughly 22.6x trailing earnings ($4.03 TTM EPS per ADS), 22.5x trailing free cash flow, 4.3x sales, and a 2.9–3.0% dividend yield. Every one of those metrics is at the extreme end of BHP's own five-year distribution: the P/E has ranged 4.6x–18.4x, P/FCF 4.4x–13.2x, and the dividend yield 4.8%–12.6% across FY21–FY25. The re-rating is real and partly justified by a copper price cycle that Rio Tinto's +43% first-half underlying earnings corroborates as sector-wide. The current price leaves no room for error.

Three specific things happened during the period that make the current price harder to defend. First, on 16–17 July 2026 BHP guided FY27 copper production lower because ore grades are falling at Escondida, a geological constraint rather than a price or cost problem, and it lands on the asset that now drives over half of underlying EBITDA. Second, on 18 June 2026 BHP raised the Jansen Stage 2 investment estimate from $4.9bn to $6.9bn, pushed first output to late FY2031, and booked a ~$2.3bn impairment; the write-down roughly equals the cost increase, which implies the incremental capital is not expected to earn its cost of capital on current potash assumptions. Third, on 6 May 2026 the Court of Appeal refused BHP permission to appeal the November 2025 English High Court finding that BHP is liable for the 2015 Fundão tailings dam failure on both strict-liability and fault-based grounds. Liability is settled; quantum is not, and BHP's booked provision could not be verified from any source available in this research.

Against that, capital intensity is rising while operating cash flow is not. Capex has gone $5,670m (FY21) → $9,448m (FY25) → $9,513m (TTM), with FY27 guided at ~$11bn, a 94% increase from FY21. Operating cash flow over the same span fell from $27,234m to $19,747m. Free cash flow therefore compressed 57% from FY21 to FY25, and at ~$11bn of capex against flat OCF the FY27 free cash flow run-rate is roughly $8.7bn against a trailing dividend cost of about $6.7bn — coverable, but with less cushion than the multiple implies. The sell side reflects this: 1 buy, 5 hold, 1 sell, 1 strong sell, with a mean target of $73.64 and a median of $63.00 against a spot price of $90.88. We rate BHP REDUCE: trim into the mid-August FY26 result, retain a reduced core position for those who want diversified-miner exposure, and look to re-engage in the low-to-mid $60s.

## Company Overview

BHP is the largest diversified miner by market capitalization — approximately $229.9bn at $90.50 per ADS on a derived 2,540m ADS (≈5,081m ordinary shares; 1 ADS = 2 ordinary shares). Enterprise value is approximately $243.8bn including $13.95bn of net debt at 31 December 2025.

A methodological point governs the rest of this report. BHP is an Australian foreign private issuer that files Form 20-F and Form 6-K, not 10-K, 10-Q, DEF 14A or Form 4. The most recent annual filing is the 20-F of 22 August 2025 covering FY2025 (year ended 30 June 2025); FY2026 full-year results are not yet published as of the 10 August 2026 information cutoff. There is therefore no US proxy compensation data, no insider transaction record, and no Altman/Piotroski-style screen for this issuer, and none should be inferred. The financial series below is drawn from a structured third-party aggregation of BHP's reported figures, cross-checked where possible: derived H1 FY26 revenue growth of +10.8% reconciles against the independently reported "+11%," which raises confidence in the series.

One data conflict must be flagged before any valuation work. The market-data profile shows trailing EPS of $1.774 and a trailing P/E of 44.91. The $1.774 figure is on a **per-ordinary-share** basis (2 × $1.774 = $3.55, exactly FY25 per-ADS EPS), while the price is per ADS. The resulting 44.9x P/E is roughly double the true figure and is not used here. All per-share arithmetic in this report is per ADS.

The portfolio's defining structural change is the copper/iron-ore crossover. Segment revenue (US$m, fiscal years to 30 June):

| Segment | FY21 | FY23 | FY25 | FY25 share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | 15,726 | 16,027 | **22,530** | 43.9% |
| Iron Ore | 34,475 | 24,812 | **22,919** | 44.7% |
| Coal | 5,154 | 10,958 | **5,046** | 9.8% |
| Other | 1,566 | 2,020 | 767 | 1.5% |
| **Total** | 56,921 | 53,817 | **51,262** | |

Copper revenue rose 43.3% from FY21 to FY25 while iron ore fell 33.5%. Coal collapsed 67.6% from the FY22 coking-coal peak. On an earnings basis the crossover completed in H1 FY26, when copper contributed over 50% of underlying EBITDA. Western Australia Nickel has been under divestment review since July 2025 and BHP has flagged that some Queensland metallurgical coal production could be shut on low prices and high royalties.

Two observations follow. The commodity mix shift is not diversification: copper plus iron ore was 88.6% of FY25 revenue versus 88.2% in FY21, so BHP has rotated out of coal and nickel into copper and is now a two-commodity business whose earnings swing factor is a single mine. And that mine is entering grade decline.

**Margins and the earnings trough.** FY24, not FY25, was the operating trough: the margin bottomed at 31.51% and recovered to 37.97% in FY25 on revenue that was 7.9% lower, with net income rising 14.2%. That combination is only explicable by large non-recurring charges depressing FY24 (nickel and Brazil provisions are the plausible candidates, alongside the coal price collapse), but the composition was not verifiable from primary filings and should not be treated as established. The practical consequence is that a naive FY24→FY25→FY26 earnings sequence will misread the trend, particularly once the $2.3bn non-cash Jansen charge lands in FY26 reported net income.

**H1 FY26 was genuinely strong.** Revenue $27.9bn (+11%), attributable profit $5.64bn (+28%), underlying EBITDA +25% at a 58% margin, copper above 50% of EBITDA, interim dividend +46%. Derived payout on the interim (≈73 US cents per ordinary share × 5,081m shares ≈ $3.71bn against $5.64bn attributable profit) is roughly 66%, comfortably above the stated 50%-of-underlying policy floor.

**Leverage is comfortable; the direction is not.** Net debt of $13.95bn at Dec'25 is ~6.1% of market capitalization and ~0.71x TTM operating cash flow. Applying the disclosed 58% H1 FY26 EBITDA margin and the +25% year-on-year comparison implies TTM underlying EBITDA near $29bn, giving net debt/EBITDA of roughly 0.48x. Treat that as a derived sensitivity: the 58% margin may be struck on a revenue base excluding third-party trading, and the FY25 half-year EBITDA split is inferred. Even allowing wide error bars, leverage is low for a diversified miner. But gross debt rose $7.3bn in eighteen months (FY24 $20.7bn → Dec'25 $28.0bn) while cash rose only $1.2bn, and net debt has moved from +$1.4bn net cash in FY22 to −$13.9bn. Total equity, interest expense and covenant terms were not obtainable, so no interest-coverage or covenant-headroom assessment is presented.

**The capital-intensity problem is arithmetic, not interpretive.** Between FY21 and FY25, free cash flow fell $12,320m: the capex increase accounts for $3,778m and the operating cash flow decline for $8,542m. FY27 capex guidance of ~$11bn adds roughly $1.5bn more of drag against a flat OCF base. At ~$8.7bn of FY27 free cash flow, the trailing dividend cost of ~$6.7bn ($2.63/ADS × 2,540m) absorbs about 77%. The dividend is not at risk in the base case; the buffer above the 50% policy floor is thinner than the H1 increase suggested.

**Earnings-delivery record.** Three misses in the last four reported quarters, including −11.6% on the December 2025 quarter against a $1.26 estimate. That pattern argues for discounting rather than extrapolating consensus forward estimates, and it sits awkwardly with a multiple at the top of the five-year range.

Financial health scores 6.5: strong absolute cash generation, first-quartile margins, low leverage, offset by falling FCF conversion, rising gross debt, an unquantified Brazil obligation, and genuine disclosure gaps on equity, coverage and covenants.

## Growth Analysis

Near-term volume growth is negative, and the growth that shows up in reported revenue is price-driven.

**Copper.** The FY26 sequence deteriorated within the year: a record H1 with guidance raised (January 2026), then Q3 FY26 copper −3% quarter-on-quarter and −7% year-on-year, then Q4 at 491.9kt against 490.6kt consensus. FY27 is guided lower on Escondida grade decline. Tier-1/2 reporting (WSJ, MarketWatch, Morningstar, all 16 July 2026) states FY26 copper output fell; tier-3 outlets described "record copper," a conflict left unresolved here with weight given to the tier-1/2 view. Grade decline can only be offset by throughput expansion, new tonnes, or acquisition, all of which cost capital and time.

**Iron ore.** Record FY26 volumes, Q4 68.1Mt (−3% year-on-year, +8% quarter-on-quarter), FY27 guidance 286–298Mt. This is a maintain-and-optimise business rather than a growth engine, and BHP achieved higher realised quarterly prices in FY26 despite Chinese purchasing restrictions before an April 2026 supply agreement.

**Potash.** Jansen Stage 1 first production slipped to mid-2027 (with an up-to-$1.7bn Stage 1 overrun flagged in July 2025); Stage 2 rose from $4.9bn to $6.9bn with first output pushed to late FY2031 and a ~$2.3bn impairment. The June 2026 CN and CPKC rail agreements de-risk logistics, since dual-carrier access to Vancouver reduces single-railroad hold-up risk, but a write-down before the first Stage 1 tonne is sold is a poor opening. Headlines citing "$9.9bn" are AUD/CAD conversions of the US$6.9bn Stage 2 figure and must not be added to Stage 1.

**Coal and nickel.** Both are being shrunk deliberately: coal revenue down 67.6% from FY22, Queensland met coal closure flagged, WA Nickel under divestment review.

The forward earnings path therefore depends almost entirely on realised copper and iron ore prices. That can be a perfectly good reason to own a miner, but it is a commodity view rather than a company growth thesis, and it is being expressed at 22.6x trailing earnings rather than at the 11–14x that prevailed when the same assets were priced for cyclical risk. Growth prospects score 4.5.

## Valuation Assessment

At the authoritative price of $90.88:

| Metric | Current | FY25 | FY24 | FY23 | FY22 | FY21 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P/E (per ADS) | **22.6x** | 13.6x | 18.4x | 11.7x | 4.6x | 14.6x |
| P/FCF | **22.5x** | 13.2x | 12.3x | 12.7x | 5.4x | 4.4x |
| P/S | **4.3x** | 2.4x | 2.6x | 2.8x | 2.2x | 1.7x |
| Dividend yield | **2.9%** | 5.3% | 5.3% | 8.8% | 12.6% | 4.8% |
| FCF yield | **4.4%** | 7.6% | 8.1% | 7.9% | 18.5% | 22.5% |

Prior appreciation on its own is not a valuation argument, and it is not the argument here. The argument is that the current multiple requires the copper price regime to be permanently higher while BHP's copper volumes decline and its capex rises. Work through the cycle-adjusted case:

- **Underlying FY26 earnings power.** H1 FY26 attributable profit was $5.64bn. A comparable second half implies roughly $11.0bn underlying, or $4.33 per ADS. Reported FY26 EPS will be lower (~$3.43) because of the non-cash Jansen charge, so reported-EPS multiples for FY26 will overstate the true multiple. Using $4.33, the stock is at 21.0x.
- **FY27.** Copper volumes guided lower, capex up ~$1.5bn, iron ore flat. Absent a further copper price increase, underlying EPS is more likely flat-to-slightly-down than up. At $4.25–$4.40, the stock is at 20.7x–21.4x.
- **What multiple is defensible?** BHP's own history says 11.7x–18.4x over five years for the same asset base with a higher iron ore weighting. Granting a genuine re-rating premium for a majority-copper earnings base with structural electrification and data-centre demand behind it, 17–18x on ~$4.35 underlying supports **$74–$78**. That range brackets the sell-side mean of $73.64.

The guardrail on treating analyst targets as stale floors does not apply here, because it holds for cheap stocks with rising estimates. BHP trades above the consensus mean ($73.64, −19% from spot) and median ($63.00, −31%), essentially at the consensus high ($91.00), with a recommendation distribution of 1 buy / 5 hold / 1 sell / 1 strong sell and three earnings misses in four quarters. Targets clustered materially below spot with a negative rating skew are a caution.

The absolute-yield support that historically underwrote BHP has also gone. A 12.6% (FY22) or 8.8% (FY23) yield gave holders paid-in-cash protection against a price cycle. A 2.9% yield, on a payout that absorbs roughly three-quarters of guided-capex-adjusted free cash flow, does not.

Valuation scores 3.5. The multiple is stretched against forward volumes and rising capital intensity, and there is no identifiable catalyst that reprices the business higher independent of the copper price itself.

## Competitive Landscape

BHP's asset quality is not in dispute. A 41.9% TTM operating margin and 18–22% FCF margins sustained through FY23–FY25 — a span that included a 17.3% revenue decline — are consistent with first-quartile cost position in both iron ore and copper. The caveat is that those years also spanned a favourable iron ore price environment, so cost-curve position and price cycle cannot be fully separated from aggregate data.

The competitive *trajectory* is where the score is set, and it is mixed-to-negative:

- **Copper.** Escondida grade decline is a structural degradation of the swing asset. Rio Tinto's H1 2026 underlying earnings rose 43%, the highest in four years, with copper and aluminium outshining iron ore profits for the first time and Reuters explicitly citing data-centre-driven copper demand. Rio's copper *earnings* are surging on price; BHP's copper *volumes* are guided down. In a copper price correction BHP would face falling price and falling volume together.
- **Iron ore.** The buy side of the seaborne market has partially consolidated in China to the point where a single counterparty could pause purchases from the world's largest supplier. BHP secured a supply agreement (confirmed April 2026) and still won higher realised quarterly prices in Q4 FY26, which reads as a win. The terms conceded to obtain the deal are not visible, and a buyer that can pause once can pause again. The counterparty identity, widely reported elsewhere, was not verified in this research.
- **Potash.** BHP enters as a new competitor against established Canadian incumbents, with Stage 1 first tonnes in mid-2027 and Stage 2 not until late FY2031.
- **Portfolio shape.** Rotating out of coal and nickel into copper concentrates rather than diversifies the earnings base. No customer-concentration disclosure was obtainable, so no claim is made in either direction on that dimension.

Competitive position scores 5.5: an outstanding asset base with a flat-to-slightly-deteriorating forward trajectory in its most important commodity.

## Risk Assessment

**1. Fundão/Samarco — liability established, quantum open.** On 14 November 2025 the English High Court found BHP liable for the 2015 Fundão tailings dam failure at Samarco (a 50:50 BHP/Vale joint venture) on two grounds under Brazilian law: strict liability as a polluter, and fault-based liability arising from BHP's "extensive control over Samarco," assumption of dam-risk-assessment responsibility, and participation in tailings operations. The court rejected the corporate-veil claim and rejected the limitation defence. The action covers over 700,000 claimants, brought by Pogust Goodhead. On 6 May 2026 the Court of Appeal refused permission to appeal, closing BHP's principal procedural defence and opening the route to a stage-2 damages trial.

The £36bn figure reported in the legal press is the **claimed aggregate**, not an award and not a provision. Treating it as expected loss would be wrong. BHP asserts that roughly 240,000 UK claimants already compensated in Brazil signed full releases, which it expects to cut claimant numbers "by about half" (the judgment reportedly referenced "more than 200,000"; the discrepancy is unreconciled). RBC's view in November 2025 was that the ruling "affirms" rather than creates liability, with cash impact "long dated and capped." That characterisation could not be traced to a company disclosure, and BHP's booked provision could not be verified from any source available in this research — a genuine gap, not an omission. The modal outcome is a multi-year quantum process settling well below the headline claim, with cash spread over a decade; the tail is that a stage-2 court accepting a broad damages methodology across even 350,000 claimants produces an obligation above whatever is provided, and an English judgment is not within BHP's control to cap.

A second-order factor cuts BHP's way: the claimant firm's UK entity reported a £91m loss for 2023, net current liabilities rising from £2.5m to £93.1m, and a going-concern material-uncertainty flag from its auditors, with its founder terminated as a director in September 2025 amid reported tension with its £500m funder. Claimant-side funding fragility could improve BHP's settlement leverage, though funders routinely replace counsel and distress at one firm does not extinguish 700,000 claims.

**2. Commodity price at what may be a cycle peak.** With copper above 50% of underlying EBITDA and iron ore the rest, roughly all of BHP's earnings variance is two prices. The multiple now embeds a persistent copper regime.

**3. Escondida geology.** A volume headwind that is not price-dependent and cannot be managed away near-term.

**4. Jansen execution.** Two consecutive annual disclosures of cost and schedule misses on the same project. The near-equality of the $2.0bn cost increase and the $2.3bn write-down suggests the incremental capital creates no value at current potash assumptions; an alternative reading is that the impairment principally reflects a lowered long-run potash price deck applied to the whole cash-generating unit. Both are consistent with the disclosed facts.

**5. China demand and buyer consolidation.** The consolidated Chinese buy side could withhold purchases again on renewal, and the terms conceded to secure the April 2026 supply agreement are not visible in available sources. [unverified — no macro data on China PMI or Politburo stimulus posture appears in the research brief or Phase 1 data.]

**6. Disclosure and governance opacity.** No proxy, no Form 4s, no verifiable compensation or insider-activity data. FY26 results are unpublished; the 20-F text was inaccessible, so no risk-factor delta analysis was possible.

Risk factors score 3.8: the Samarco quantum is a live, unquantified obligation with the appeal route closed, and the stock's position at the top of its multiple range suggests it is not priced.

### Options Market Signal

Near-term ATM implied volatility of 43.1% against 33.5% one-year realised volatility is rich (9.6-point spread, 77th percentile within the current chain), with a flat term structure (43.1% near, 41.1% far) and a balanced put/call skew of −0.8%. The nearest expiry (21 August, 10 days) prices a ±6.1% move, consistent with the mid-to-late-August FY26 result. Put/call volume ratio 0.74 and open-interest ratio 0.91 show no directional crowd. One print stands out: 1,259 contracts of the November $100 calls against 216 open interest (5.8x), +10.4% out of the money at 40.5% IV — a discrete upside bet on a strong FY26 result and a dividend surprise.

The market prices a sizeable event with no directional conviction, and prices it expensively. For holders reducing exposure, elevated near-term IV makes covered-call overwriting into the August print a reasonable way to monetise the trim. It does not challenge the fundamental view: the options market is not pricing the Samarco quantum tail, and short interest at 0.6% of float means no positioning cushion exists in either direction.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case — $105 (+15.5%)

The copper price regime has structurally shifted higher on electrification and data-centre power infrastructure, and Rio Tinto's +43% H1 underlying earnings and explicit data-centre attribution establish this as a broad rather than company-specific phenomenon. BHP's mid-August FY26 result confirms realised copper prices materially above consensus, net debt down toward $9bn from $14.7bn at H1 on BHP's own definition, and a final dividend that surprises to the upside given the H1 payout ran at ~66% of attributable profit against a 50% policy floor. Escondida grade decline is offset by throughput and the copper price does the rest; FY27 capex holds at ~$11bn, proving discipline elsewhere in the portfolio after Jansen. The Samarco quantum process settles at a fraction of the £36bn claim over a decade, aided by 240,000 signed releases and claimant-side funding distress. On $5.25 underlying EPS per ADS at 20x, the stock reaches $105.

### Bear Case — $60 (−34%)

Copper mean-reverts as Chinese industrial demand weakens and the AI-capex financing cycle slows, hitting BHP with falling price and falling Escondida volume simultaneously. Iron ore softens and the consolidated Chinese buyer applies pressure again on renewal terms. Capex at ~$11bn against flat operating cash flow drives free cash flow toward $8bn, pushing the payout ratio above 80% and forcing the dividend back to the 50% floor, which halves the yield support at a moment when the multiple has nothing else holding it. The stage-2 UK quantum trial produces an obligation above the booked provision, requiring a top-up disclosure. Underlying EPS falls toward $4.00 and the multiple reverts to the 14–15x that prevailed in FY25, giving $56–$60; the analyst low target of $60.00 anchors this case.

### Base Case — $76 (−16%)

FY26 results confirm a good year with a $2.3bn non-cash charge depressing reported EPS, and a solid final dividend. FY27 delivers roughly flat underlying earnings: lower copper volumes and ~$1.5bn more capex offset by stable-to-firm realised prices and record iron ore volumes. Underlying EPS of $4.30–$4.40 per ADS on a 17–18x multiple — a premium to BHP's own five-year average that credits the copper-weighted earnings mix — gives $74–$78. The Samarco quantum grinds on without resolution, keeping a discount attached.

Probability-weighted at 50% base / 25% bull / 25% bear: −12.5% price return, roughly −9.5% total return including the ~3% yield. That is negative expected return with an asymmetry skewed to the downside (34% bear loss versus 16% bull gain).

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Recommendation: REDUCE.** Base case $76 (−16% from $90.88), bull $105 (+15.5%), bear $60 (−34%). Horizon: 12 months, with the decisive checkpoint in the next two weeks.

**De-risking plan.** Trim into the mid-to-late-August FY26 result rather than after it. Reduce a full position to roughly one-third to one-half of target weight now, and treat the FY26 disclosure as the gate on the remainder. Near-term implied volatility at 43.1% versus 33.5% realised makes covered-call overwriting against retained shares an efficient way to execute the reduction. Long-term holders with embedded gains and a structural copper view may reasonably keep a residual core, but should not add at this price.

**What would restore HOLD:**
1. FY26 results disclosing a Samarco/Brazil provision that credibly brackets the UK exposure, with confirmed FY26 net debt at or below ~$10bn on BHP's own definition.
2. Price at $74–$78 (the base-case range) with underlying earnings power confirmed at $4.30+ per ADS.
3. FY27 copper guidance that quantifies the Escondida decline as shallower than feared, with FY27 capex reaffirmed at or below ~$11bn.

**What would upgrade to ACCUMULATE or BUY:**
1. Price in the low-to-mid $60s (analyst low $60, median $63) against unchanged underlying earnings — roughly 14–15x on $4.30, restoring a 4%+ dividend yield and a genuine margin of safety.
2. Evidence that the copper regime shift is durable in *volumes and realised prices simultaneously* — for example, a credible Escondida throughput or brownfield offset plan alongside two consecutive halves of realised copper above consensus.

**What would move this to AVOID:**
1. A stage-2 UK quantum ruling or settlement materially above the booked provision, requiring a P&L top-up of a scale that dents the balance sheet or dividend capacity.
2. FY27 capex guidance raised above ~$11bn, or a third consecutive Jansen cost or schedule revision.
3. A dividend cut to the 50%-of-underlying policy floor while the multiple remains above 18x — falling cash return with no multiple support.

**Next reassessment point:** the FY26 full-year result, expected mid-to-late August 2026. That single disclosure carries confirmed FY26 net debt, the final dividend, the Jansen impairment, FY27 guidance detail, and BHP's first full annual disclosure of its Brazil provision after losing the right to appeal.

## Conclusion

BHP is a well-run, low-leverage, structurally advantaged mining business trading at a price that requires the current copper price environment to persist. The re-rating from a 12–13x multiple and a 5%+ yield to 22.6x trailing earnings and a 2.9% yield has occurred alongside three developments that argue for less optimism, not more: FY27 copper volumes guided lower on Escondida grade decline, group capex guided from $9.5bn to ~$11bn against operating cash flow that has not grown since FY21, and a Fundão liability finding that became final in May 2026 with quantum unassessed and BHP's provision unverifiable from available sources.

The stock trades 23% above the consensus mean target and 44% above the median, with a rating distribution of one buy against two sell-side negatives, and it has missed consensus in three of the last four quarters. Probability-weighted scenarios give roughly −9.5% total expected return over twelve months, with 34% downside in the bear case against 15.5% upside in the bull. That asymmetry does not justify new capital and does justify trimming existing exposure ahead of the mid-August FY26 result. Re-engagement becomes interesting in the low-to-mid $60s, where a 14–15x multiple and a 4%+ yield would again pay holders to carry the commodity and litigation risk. **REDUCE.**
