**Research Date: July 20, 2026**

## Executive Summary

Cleveland-Cliffs is the largest flat-rolled steel producer in North America and the country's leading supplier of automotive-grade and electrical steels, operating a vertically integrated model that runs from iron ore mining through primary steelmaking to downstream finishing. The company is at an operational turn: after a two-year downturn that produced a FY2025 net loss of $1.43B and adjusted EBITDA of just $37M, Q1 2026 delivered a positive adjusted EBITDA swing to +$95M (from −$179M a year earlier) as domestic hot-rolled coil (HRC) prices climbed 24% YoY to $980/ton, propelled by 50% Section 232 steel tariffs and suppressed imports. The core Steelmaking segment turned from −$189M to +$80M EBITDA in a single year.

The problem is what sits beneath that turn. CLF carries roughly $7.3B of debt against $57M of cash, an Altman Z-Score of 1.81 that sits exactly on the distress-zone boundary, and negative free cash flow of roughly $1.0B in 2025. The company's own 10-K contains going-concern language conditional on a downside scenario. The equity survived 2025 only via a $951M October equity raise that diluted holders ~15% at a low share price. The bull case rests entirely on the persistence of a policy regime — 50% Section 232 tariffs and 25% auto tariffs — that management explicitly concedes, if "removed, modified or substantially weakened," would push imports up and U.S./Canadian steel prices "materially" down. This is a leveraged bet on the U.S. HRC price cycle and the durability of a single administration's trade posture.

The insider signal cuts against the public narrative. While management touts a non-binding POSCO partnership and tariff tailwinds, the CFO (the CEO's son) sold more than half his holdings at $13.41 in June 2026, and the COO sold $2.1M in February 2026, both near cyclical highs and immediately before Morgan Stanley downgraded the stock to Equal Weight on the thesis that "prices are nearing their peak." No open-market buying appeared. We rate CLF **AVOID**. The operational turn is real and the equity has convexity, but the balance-sheet fragility, policy dependence, and management signals leave the risk/reward unfavorable at $9.28.

## Company Overview

Cleveland-Cliffs transformed itself from a pure iron ore miner into an integrated steelmaker through the 2020 acquisitions of AK Steel and ArcelorMittal USA, then extended into Canada via the November 2024 ~$2.5B Stelco acquisition. It employs roughly 25,000 people, more than 90% of its ~20,000 hourly workers unionized (USW, UAW, IAM). The business reports one primary segment (Steelmaking) plus smaller Tubular, Tooling & Stamping, and European operations.

The differentiating asset is vertical integration: internal iron ore feedstock (Michigan, Minnesota), direct-reduced iron via HBI in Toledo, scrap processing, predominantly blast-furnace/BOF primary steelmaking, and downstream stamping, tooling, and tubing. This provides stable, predictable input costs relative to electric-arc-furnace (EAF) peers such as Nucor and Steel Dynamics that depend on volatile scrap and pig iron. With busheling scrap at ~$434/lt in Q1 2026, well above the ~$400 ten-year average, the integrated ore-based model carries a structural cost advantage in the current environment. Roughly 40–45% of flat-rolled shipments run under fixed-price contracts, dampening spot volatility, and CLF is the leading U.S. producer of electrical steels used in grid transformers, a structurally tight market driven by AI and data-center electricity demand.

## Financial Analysis

The trailing financials describe a company that fell into a deep trough:

- **Revenue trajectory:** $22.99B (2022) → $22.0B (2023) → $19.19B (2024) → $18.61B (2025). The decline reflects recession-like automotive and service-center demand, unfavorable mix, and the expiration of a low-margin (unprofitable) ArcelorMittal slab contract representing ~10% of volume.
- **Profitability collapse:** Operating income fell from +$1.94B (2022) to −$1.58B (2025). Net loss widened to −$1.43B in 2025 from −$714M in 2024. Adjusted EBITDA cratered from $1.89B (2023) to $773M (2024) to $37M (2025), with the core Steelmaking segment at −$16M.
- **FY2025 margins:** operating −0.3%, net −7.7%, ROA −7.1%, ROE −23.3%.
- **Cash flow stress:** Operating cash flow −$462M and FCF −$1.02B in 2025 (capex $561M). Retained earnings turned negative (−$529M at YE2025, −$766M at Q1 2026).
- **Balance sheet:** Total debt ~$7.3B (senior notes $6.9B plus ABL), D/E ~2.24, cash of only $57M. Altman Z-Score of 1.81 sits precisely at the distress boundary. The backstop is liquidity: ~$3.1–3.3B available under a $4.75B ABL facility, with the nearest senior note maturity pushed out to 2029.
- **Dilution:** The October 2025 raise issued 75 million shares for $951M net, taking share count from ~494M (Q1 2025) to ~570M (Q1 2026), a ~15% dilution executed at a depressed price.

The Q1 2026 turn is the pivotal data point. Revenue was $4.92B (+6% YoY), adjusted EBITDA swung to +$95M from −$179M, and Steelmaking segment EBITDA reached +$80M from −$189M. Operating loss narrowed to −$213M from −$543M; net loss narrowed to −$229M (−$0.42/sh) from −$486M (−$1.01/sh). The driver was HRC at $980/ton, up 24% YoY, supported by tariffs and depressed imports.

The turn is real but incomplete. The company remained cash-negative in Q1 2026 (operating cash flow −$325M on a receivables build tied to higher prices), ABL borrowings rose to $959M, and capex guidance of ~$800M for the next twelve months implies continued FCF pressure even as EBITDA recovers. The equity is a thin, highly levered slice atop a $7.3B debt stack: every incremental dollar of EBITDA is amplified in the equity, as is every dollar of downside.

The earnings-surprise record is mixed and low-visibility: over the last four quarters, one beat and three misses, including a −21.4% miss in Q1 2026 (−$0.42 actual vs −$0.35 estimate). That does not support high confidence in the precise trajectory of forward consensus.

## Growth Analysis

The forward estimate picture is improving at the near end and deteriorating at the far end, which is the signature of a cyclical peak debate rather than a structural growth story:

- **Current quarter:** −$0.21, essentially flat over 30 days but revised down −532.7% over 90 days (from a −$0.03 estimate, a sign the near-term loss is deepening versus prior hopes).
- **Next quarter:** +$0.19, revised up +17.5% (30d) and +300% (90d) — the market expects a return to quarterly profitability.
- **Current year:** −$0.33, revised +16.7% (30d), +5.1% (90d) — modestly improving.
- **Next year:** +$0.38, revised down −24.9% (30d) and −32.6% (90d).

The next-year revisions are the tell. Consensus expects CLF to turn profitable in 2026–2027, but analysts have cut the next-year estimate by roughly a third over 90 days. This is not the +30% revision pattern that reliably precedes sustained outperformance; it is the reverse at the horizon that matters most for a deleveraging thesis. The near-term improvement is a mechanical function of HRC prices that Morgan Stanley believes are peaking, while the out-year cuts reflect skepticism that those prices, and the tariffs supporting them, persist.

On the secular side, the one durable growth vector is electrical steel. CLF is the leading U.S. producer, and distribution-transformer shortages amid AI/data-center electricity growth are a genuine tailwind for a high-margin niche. But electrical steel is a small slice of an $18.6B revenue base dominated by cyclical flat-rolled and automotive volumes; it cannot by itself carry a $7.3B deleveraging thesis. Automotive, at ~27% of steelmaking revenue ($5.0B), remains constrained by North American light-vehicle production of 15.3M units, well below the ~17M pre-COVID level, with intensifying pricing competition for contracts.

We score Growth Prospects 5.2 — improved from prior reads on the confirmed Q1 EBITDA turn, but capped by the out-year estimate cuts and the fact that the recovery is a price-driven cyclical rebound off a trough, not durable secular expansion.

## Valuation Assessment

Standard multiples are uninformative here: trailing and forward P/E are both negative (~−4.0x on −$2.90 trailing EPS), and there is no dividend. The company is loss-making, so valuation must be framed on normalized mid-cycle earnings power, cash-flow recovery, and enterprise value against debt.

At $9.28 the equity market cap is ~$5.3B against ~$7.2B of net debt ($7.3B total debt less $57M cash), for an enterprise value of roughly $12.6B. On the Q1 2026 annualized run-rate of ~$380M adjusted EBITDA, EV/EBITDA is ~33x — expensive. On a more normalized mid-cycle EBITDA in the $1.5–2.0B range (comparable to 2023–2024 levels), EV/EBITDA compresses to ~6.3–8.4x, a reasonable multiple for a levered integrated producer. The valuation therefore hinges on which EBITDA number is the right anchor, which in turn hinges on tariff durability.

The analyst price-target range is instructive: low $8.60, mean $10.56, median $10.00, high $15.00. The current price of $9.28 sits below the mean and median, offering modest upside to consensus. But this is not a case where analyst targets act as a floor that will be revised upward: the out-year estimates are being cut, the recommendation distribution is dominated by holds (10 hold, 1 buy, 2 strong sell), and Morgan Stanley's June downgrade to Equal Weight with a $12.50 target explicitly argues the price rally is peaking. The consensus target dispersion (8.60 to 15.00) reflects genuine disagreement about the HRC cycle, not underestimation of a clear turn.

We are cautious about treating the compressed/negative multiple as "cheap." A stock that screens optically inexpensive on normalized earnings but faces a structural policy dependence and distress-boundary balance sheet is a value trap: cheapness without an independent, durable catalyst. The one plausible independent catalyst, a POSCO transaction or asset divestiture that deleverages the balance sheet, remains unconfirmed (non-binding MOU, no disclosed structure). We score Valuation 4.2. The stock is not egregiously overpriced, but the asymmetry does not favor the long: the upside requires tariffs to hold and a deleveraging catalyst to close, while the downside is amplified by the leverage if HRC rolls over.

## Competitive Landscape

CLF's competitive position is stable-to-modestly-favorable within its structural constraints. The vertically integrated, ore-based model is a genuine advantage over EAF peers when scrap prices are elevated, and the U.S. remains the only major steel market that consumes more than it produces, so tariff-suppressed imports support domestic pricing. CLF's automotive service capability, built over decades, generates higher through-cycle margins, and its leadership in electrical steels positions it well for grid/data-center demand.

The trajectory is where caution enters. Automotive contract competition is intensifying, North American vehicle production remains structurally below pre-COVID levels, and CLF idled or closed six operations in 2025 (Steelton, Conshohocken, Riverdale closures; Dearborn, Minorca, Hibbing idles), taking ~$300M in exit costs, with further Q2 2026 idling planned at Gary and Burns Harbor plate. Footprint rationalization improves cost structure but signals a company shrinking to fit demand rather than expanding share. The moat is real on cost structure and electrical steel; it is neutral-to-eroding on the core flat-rolled and automotive franchise. We score Competitive Position 5.8, consistent with the prior improving trend but not a high-conviction advantage.

## Risk Assessment

The idiosyncratic risks here are severe and not fully priced given the equity's leverage:

1. **Leverage and liquidity at the core.** With a Z-Score of 1.81, negative FCF, and $7.3B debt against $57M cash, the company explicitly warns it "may be unable to continue as a going concern" in a downside scenario. A springing 1.0x fixed-charge covenant on the ABL was not triggered as of Q1 2026, but recovery depends on sustained high steel prices; the covenant math tightens quickly if HRC rolls over before deleveraging catalysts close.

2. **Extreme tariff dependence — a single-administration policy bet.** The entire bull case rests on the persistence of 50% Section 232 steel tariffs and 25% auto tariffs. Management concedes that weakening these would push imports up and prices "materially" down. A USMCA joint review begins in 2026, adding a specific, dated policy risk vector.

3. **Automotive concentration and cyclicality.** ~27% of steelmaking revenue tied to a structurally depressed vehicle-production environment with intensifying contract competition.

4. **Commodity whipsaw.** Direct HRC leverage; fixed-price contracts help stability but expose CLF when input costs rise faster than contract adjustments.

5. **Goodwill and legacy liabilities.** $1.8B goodwill with no impairment taken yet, but flagged as at-risk on sustained price declines; underfunded pension/OPEB, multiemployer exposure, and a disputed UMWA 1974 Plan withdrawal liability.

6. **Controls yellow flag.** A Q3 2025 "immaterial error" correction understated COGS across 2022–2025 periods. Management deemed it immaterial and controls effective, but it warrants monitoring in subsequent filings.

7. **Unconfirmed catalysts.** The POSCO MOU is non-binding with no disclosed structure, and the J.P. Morgan-led asset divestitures are unclosed. Both are the pillars of the deleveraging path, and both are conceptual.

### Options Market Signal

The options market is pricing imminent, elevated stress. Near-term ATM IV is 137.5% against 67.9% one-year historical volatility (a rich 69.6pp spread, 90th percentile within the chain), with the term structure in steep backwardation (137.5% near vs 72.3% at six months), pricing a near-term catalyst or binary. Put/call skew is roughly balanced (0.4% near-term), so this is a two-way volatility bet rather than a one-sided crash-hedge posture, consistent with the live bull/bear debate over the HRC peak. IV has collapsed from 236.7% in mid-June to 137.5% as the stock fell 24% to $9.28, so some prior event stress has resolved, and the P/C OI ratio rose from 0.27 to 0.44 (more put positioning). Short interest at 14.5% of float (4.1 days to cover) confirms meaningful institutional bearishness without extreme squeeze risk. The market is pricing high uncertainty and does not treat the recovery as settled.

## Investment Thesis

**Bull Case.** The Q1 2026 turn is the leading edge of a sustained recovery. HRC prices hold near $980/ton as tariffs persist and imports stay suppressed, adjusted EBITDA rebounds toward $1.5–2.0B, and the integrated ore-based model captures margin as EAF peers struggle with elevated scrap. A POSCO transaction and asset divestitures close, injecting proceeds that pay down debt and repair the balance sheet. Electrical-steel demand from AI/data-center grid buildout provides a high-margin secular tailwind. On normalized mid-cycle EBITDA and successful deleveraging, the levered equity re-rates sharply; the bull target of $15.00 (roughly +62% from $9.28) reflects this scenario.

**Bear Case.** Morgan Stanley is right and the supply-driven price rally peaks. HRC rolls over in H2 2026, the tariff regime is weakened in the USMCA review or a policy shift, and imports rise. EBITDA falls back toward breakeven, FCF stays negative, and the debt load forces further dilution or covenant stress. Goodwill impairment on $1.8B follows sustained price declines. The out-year estimate cuts prove prescient. In this scenario the equity, as a thin leveraged slice, falls back toward the 52-week low of $7.73 or below — a bear target of ~$7.00–7.50, roughly −20% to −25% from current, with tail risk deeper if going-concern conditions materialize.

The insider signal favors the bear: the CFO and COO sold near the highs without any offsetting open-market buying, immediately before the Morgan Stanley downgrade. The pay-for-performance disconnect ($19M CEO comp in a −$1.4B-loss year, cumulative TSR indexed at 91.2 vs a peer group at 324.78, roughly 3.5x below peers) compounds the governance concern in a founder-led, father-son C-suite.

## Short Candidate Assessment

**Catalyst.** The identifiable bearish catalyst is a rollover in U.S. HRC prices during H2 2026 as the supply-driven rally exhausts (Morgan Stanley's thesis), combined with the 2026 USMCA joint review as a policy risk to the tariff regime. Neither is a hard-dated binary: HRC is a continuous price series, and the USMCA review lacks a specific decision date with material downside odds. The Q2 2026 earnings print is the nearest dated event, but with next-quarter consensus already at +$0.19, a beat is plausible on lagging high-price contracts, which would work against a short.

**Timing window.** Diffuse. The HRC-peak thesis could take 3–9 months to play out, and the tariff regime could persist indefinitely under the current administration. This is longer than a clean short setup warrants.

**Short interest and squeeze risk.** Short interest is 14.5% of float with 4.1 days to cover — elevated and confirming institutional bearishness, but not at the >20% extreme where crowding limits further downside. The concern is the opposite: a low-float, high-beta cyclical with 137.5% near-term IV can squeeze violently on any positive tariff headline, POSCO confirmation, or HRC uptick.

**Borrow and options signal.** Rich near-term IV (90th percentile) makes outright put purchases expensive; the balanced skew means the options market is not confirming a clean directional bearish edge. High carry (rich IV, borrow cost embedded in the vol surface) erodes short economics.

**Downside target and risk/reward.** Bear target ~$7.00–7.50 (−20% to −25%); upside risk on a squeeze or POSCO/tariff catalyst is easily +40–60% given the leverage and IV. That is worse than 1:1 reward:risk in the wrong direction for a short; the asymmetry favors avoiding rather than shorting.

**Verdict: NOT SHORTABLE.** The bearish thesis on the balance sheet and tariff dependence is coherent, but there is no hard-dated catalyst, the timing is diffuse, IV is expensive, skew is balanced, and squeeze/headline risk on a leveraged low-float cyclical is asymmetric to the upside. This is a stock to avoid owning, not one to actively short.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

This is an AVOID with a defined re-entry framework.

**Re-entry criteria:**
- **Confirmed deleveraging catalyst.** A signed, disclosed POSCO transaction or asset divestiture with proceeds explicitly applied to debt paydown, moving the Altman Z-Score meaningfully off the 1.81 distress boundary and net debt below ~$5.5B. This is the single most important gate.
- **Sustained positive FCF.** Two consecutive quarters of positive operating cash flow and FCF, confirming the EBITDA turn converts to cash rather than being absorbed by receivables builds and $800M annual capex.
- **Tariff durability confirmation.** A clean pass through the 2026 USMCA review with Section 232 steel tariffs intact, removing the single-administration policy tail that underpins the entire pricing thesis.
- **Insider signal reversal.** Open-market buying by the CEO or CFO would materially change the read; the current pattern of selling into the highs with no buying is a caution flag we would want reversed.

**Timeline for headwinds to persist.** The balance-sheet and policy overhangs are structural and likely to persist through at least the next 12–24 months. The HRC price cycle and tariff regime will define the equity's path, and neither offers visibility beyond a few quarters. We would reassess at the Q2 2026 print and again after any USMCA review clarity or POSCO/divestiture confirmation.

## Conclusion

Cleveland-Cliffs presents a live, two-sided setup. The bull case is real: Q1 2026 marked a genuine operational turn, the integrated ore-based model carries a cost advantage over scrap-dependent peers, and electrical-steel demand offers a secular tailwind. But the equity is a thin leveraged slice atop $7.3B of debt, with an Altman Z-Score at the distress boundary, negative FCF, going-concern language in its own filings, and a survival that required 15% dilution in October 2025. The recovery depends on the persistence of a tariff regime that management concedes could reverse the entire pricing thesis if weakened, and out-year consensus estimates have been cut roughly a third over 90 days even as near-term estimates improve. The CFO and COO sold into the highs with no offsetting buying, immediately before Morgan Stanley called the price rally near its peak.

We rate CLF **AVOID** at $9.28, overall score 4.1. This is consistent with the prior three reads while acknowledging the improving trend: the operational turn is confirmed and lifts our Growth and Competitive scores, but the balance-sheet fragility, tariff/policy dependence, and management signals keep the risk/reward unfavorable. The stock is not a short (no hard catalyst, expensive IV, squeeze risk), but the asymmetry does not support ownership until a confirmed deleveraging catalyst closes and cash generation turns durably positive. We would reassess on a signed POSCO/divestiture transaction, two quarters of positive FCF, or a clean USMCA review outcome.
