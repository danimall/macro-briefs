## Executive Summary

Encompass Health is the largest inpatient rehabilitation facility ("IRF") operator in the United States: 173 hospitals across 39 states and Puerto Rico, 11,465 licensed beds, 263,299 discharges in FY2025, and 81.8% of revenue sourced from Medicare fee-for-service (65.4%) plus Medicare Advantage (16.4%). The business has three durable properties that most hospital operators lack: demand is non-discretionary and referral-driven (92% of admissions arrive from acute-care hospitals following stroke, hip fracture, or brain injury), national IRF supply has been essentially flat for fourteen years (1,179 facilities in 2010 versus 1,170 in 2024) against a 75+ population growing roughly 4% annually, and only about 15% of clinically eligible acute-care discharges actually reach an IRF. Occupancy has climbed 380bp in three years to 75.9% while the company simultaneously added 687 licensed beds, direct evidence the demand is real rather than mix-driven.

The problem is the next eight quarters of cash. FY2025 produced $1,177.0M of operating cash flow against roughly $736M of capex — $441M pre-minority free cash flow. FY2026 capex is guided to $920–995M, of which only $225–240M is maintenance, with a further ~$441M still required to complete projects already under construction. Cash taxes step up to a guided $150–180M from $124M. On plausible operating cash flow of $1.25–1.27B, pre-minority FCF compresses to roughly $255–350M, and after distributions to joint-venture partners (minority net income was $192.9M in 2025 and rising) owner-level free cash flow plausibly lands between $25M and $150M — a 0.2–1.4% yield on an $11.0B market capitalization. The company is nonetheless raising the dividend (twice in twelve months, to an implied $0.21 quarterly) and buying back stock ($71.6M in Q1 2026), funded in part with $500M of 5.875% senior notes due 2034 issued in May, versus a legacy stack coupon of 4.50–4.75%. Q1 2026 adjusted free cash flow already fell 12.9% year over year to $193.8M while Adjusted EBITDA grew 11.2%.

Two other things changed at the margin. Same-store discharge growth halved from +3.4% in FY2025 to +1.6% in Q1 2026, and management's explanation is not in the public record I can verify. FY2026 guidance implies Adjusted EBITDA margin of 21.25% versus 21.36% actual in 2025 — the end of a three-year margin-expansion run, consistent with de novo ramp drag from seven 2026 openings but also consistent with the cost pressure the sell-side is now asking about. At 18.5x the $6.00 adjusted-EPS guidance midpoint and 17.0x next-year consensus of $6.54, with roughly 11% upside to my base case, I move to HOLD from the prior BUY. The stock is essentially unchanged since the last review; the change reflects a narrower base case, a cash-flow trough that arrives before the payoff, and a binary earnings print in hours with implied volatility at 56.4% against 26.1% realized.

## Company Overview

EHC operates a single line of business. Inpatient rehabilitation is substantially all of revenue; outpatient visits fell 24.4% in 2025 to 86,238 and are a shrinking residual. Average length of stay is 12.1 days, the average Medicare patient is roughly 77 years old, and geographic concentration is disclosed in Florida and Texas.

Three structural features define the model:

**Regulatory barrier plus static supply.** To bill as an IRF a facility must meet the "60% Rule" (60% of patients carrying one of 13 specified diagnoses), a three-hours-per-day therapy standard, physician face-to-face visits at least three days weekly, and 24/7 RN coverage. Roughly 36% of EHC's licensed beds sit in certificate-of-need states. Combined with the flat national facility count, this is a genuine moat — though Florida repealed IRF CON in 2019 and South Carolina in 2023, and Florida is EHC's most active de novo market.

**Joint ventures as referral insurance.** 67 of 173 hospitals are JVs, in substantially all cases with a nonprofit hospital or system. This converts the referral source into an equity partner, the structural answer to the fact that acute-care hospitals' own rehab units are EHC's primary competitor. It also means EHC shares the economics: minority net income rose from $111.0M (2023) to $140.9M (2024) to $192.9M (2025), or 25.4% of continuing-operations income.

**Organic, capital-intensive growth.** Since 2012 EHC has opened or acquired 78 hospitals and added roughly 4,809 licensed beds (+72%). It owns about 79% of its hospital real estate. Seven de novos open in 2026 (Irmo SC, Concordville PA, Loganville GA, Norristown PA, San Antonio TX, Bangor ME, Avondale AZ) plus 150–200 beds; five more de novos and another 150–200 beds are slated for 2027.

Recomputed on market equity, Z is approximately 3.3 (safe zone); substituting book equity including minority interest yields roughly 2.1, so the reading is input-sensitive but the covenant and coverage data corroborate the safe conclusion independently.

**Cash flow is the pressure point.** The FY2026 capex guide of $920–995M against $736M spent in 2025 is a step-change of roughly $220M at midpoint, layered on a $26–56M cash-tax headwind and increased shareholder returns. Item 1A states plainly that IRF construction costs are rising faster than reimbursement rates and general inflation, which puts incremental return on invested capital at risk precisely as the spend peaks. ROIC is one of three named executive performance metrics, which at least aligns the incentive.

I score financial health 7.0, down modestly from the prior 7.3. Leverage, coverage and covenant headroom are genuinely strong; the deductions are the FCF trough, the step-up in marginal cost of debt from ~4.6% to 5.875% in a regime where the long end is at multi-year highs, and the increase in cash returns funded partly with borrowing rather than internal cash generation.

## Growth Analysis

The demographic case is durable and quantified: 75+ population growth of roughly 4% annually through 2030, flat national IRF supply, and a ~15% conversion rate from clinically eligible acute discharges. Against that backdrop, FY2026 guidance of $6,375–6,470M revenue (+8.2% at midpoint) and $1,350–1,380M Adjusted EBITDA (+7.7%) is respectable.

Three qualifications matter.

**Volume is decelerating and the reason is unresolved.** Total discharge growth went +8.3% (2024) → +6.0% (2025) → +4.3% (Q1 2026). Same-store discharges went +3.4% (FY2025) → +1.6% (Q1 2026). The benign explanation is capacity: at 75.9% occupancy, mature hospitals run out of beds, and the de novo/bed-addition program exists precisely to relieve that. The less benign explanations are Medicare Advantage denials and convener-driven steering toward home health and skilled nursing (the 10-K explicitly warns that large MA plans have acquired home-health operators and may shift patients to reduce cost, and that MA referral-to-admission conversion is lower than traditional Medicare), or EHC declining marginal admissions in the tightening claims-review environment. These are not distinguishable from the data available, and I could not locate management's explanation. Tonight's call is the highest-value evidence on this question.

**Margin expansion ends in 2026.** Guided Adjusted EBITDA margin of 21.25% versus 21.36% actual is flat-to-slightly-down. Seven de novo openings carry ramp drag, so this is expected mechanically; it also removes the earnings driver that produced 24.2% net income growth on 10.5% revenue growth in 2025. Next-year consensus EPS of $6.54 against a guided $6.00 this year is 9.0% growth — good, not inflecting.

**Estimate revisions are flat, not rising.** Current-year consensus $6.00 (−0.1% over 30 days), next quarter $1.36 (−0.1% / −0.2%), next year $6.54 (+0.9% over 90 days). The high-conviction pattern that argues for aggressive positioning — 30%+ estimate revisions alongside accelerating revenue — is absent here. What is present is a clean four-for-four beat record (+5.8%, +11.3%, +1.9%, +14.6%), which supports confidence that the $6.00 guide is achievable or beatable but does not argue for multiple expansion.

The gross-up in "other revenue" deserves a note for modelers: state directed and supplemental payments of roughly $148M in 2025 against roughly $127M of provider taxes paid netted only about $21M, and within the year the +$39.0M in supplemental payments was offset by +$33.4M in taxes. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act limits both mechanisms mostly from 2027, and the company warns the offset may not move symmetrically. At ~1.7% of Adjusted EBITDA this is not thesis-changing, but the line should not be extrapolated as margin-accretive.

Growth prospects: 6.5, down from 7.0. The structural demand case is intact; the near-term rate of change has slowed and the margin lever is exhausted.

## Valuation Assessment

At $110.89:

## Competitive Landscape

EHC's position is stable-to-improving operationally and stable-to-eroding on payer structure.

**Improving:** occupancy up 380bp in three years while adding beds; the largest scale in a fragmented industry; a construction capability using pre-fabrication that compresses build time; outcomes data (higher discharge-to-community rates, shorter stays than alternative settings) that management says has driven better Medicare Advantage payment, corroborated by the 10-K's statement that the traditional-Medicare-to-MA rate differential has been shrinking. Managed-care contract increases ran 2–4% in 2025.

**Eroding:** the primary competitive threat is payer vertical integration, not rival operators. MA penetration was 54% of Medicare beneficiaries in 2025 and CBO projects roughly 64% by 2034. MA plans deny IRF coverage more often than traditional Medicare, convert referrals at a lower rate, pay less, and generate higher bad debt — and some now own home-health assets with an incentive to route patients there. Non-provider "conveners" steer post-acute placement toward lower-acuity settings. This headwind grows mechanically with MA share regardless of EHC's execution.

**New and quantified:** the Transforming Episode Accountability Model became mandatory January 1, 2026 for acute-care hospitals in 189 markets. Only about 2% of EHC discharges relate to the five covered surgical episodes in covered markets, so the direct exposure is small. The mechanism matters more than the magnitude: EHC discloses that for each of the five procedures, 2024 30-day total spend where an IRF stay followed acute discharge *exceeded* the CMS target price. Inside TEAM markets, referring a patient to an IRF has been running above target — a financial reason for the referral source to route elsewhere. If TEAM expands in scope or episode count, the referral channel that generates 92% of admissions faces a structural disincentive.

Certificate-of-need erosion cuts both ways: Florida's 2019 repeal eased EHC's own expansion in its most active de novo market while removing the barrier against others.

Competitive position: 6.4.

## Risk Assessment

**Reimbursement policy is the dominant variable.** At its January 2026 meeting MedPAC approved recommending to Congress a 7% reduction to the IRF-PPS base payment rate, and an external report dated June 15, 2026 indicates MedPAC reiterated the position. Context on magnitude: EHC's realized updates were +3.0% (FY2025 rule) and roughly +2.9% (FY2026 rule), so a 7% base cut would be roughly a 10-point swing on approximately 65% of revenue. The strongest argument against alarm is the track record — in connection with every CMS IRF-PPS rulemaking since 2008, MedPAC has recommended either no update or a reduction, and CMS has delivered positive updates anyway. Neither Congress nor CMS is obligated to adopt the recommendation. That is a track record, not a guarantee, and it does not price the tail: MedPAC's unified post-acute PPS work previously carried an estimate that IRF reimbursement would fall 15%, with aggregate dollars shifting from for-profit freestanding IRFs (EHC's exact profile) to nonprofit and hospital-based facilities.

**Claims review is the concrete near-term risk.** Review Choice Demonstration subjects 100% of an IRF's Medicare claims to pre-claim or post-payment review. EHC elected pre-claim review in Alabama in August 2023; all participating hospitals cleared the 80% cycle-1 threshold, but **no Alabama hospital achieved the opt-out validation rate in cycle 2 (≥85%) or cycle 3 (≥90%)**. Cycle 4 began September 1, 2025 at ≥90%. In December 2025 CMS announced expansion to Texas (effective March 2, 2026) and California (May 1, 2026), bringing 33 hospitals and approximately 11.9% of EHC's IRF Medicare claims under the program. Texas is one of the two largest concentrations. Part of the deterioration is explained by a rising threshold rather than degrading documentation, and EHC's position is that many non-affirmations apply standards conflicting with Medicare IRF coverage criteria — a position supported by its UPIC appeal history, where an ALJ overturned $12.5M of a $30.5M extrapolated recoupment. Reported denials under review within noncurrent patient receivables actually fell from $30.6M to $25.5M year over year, which argues collections were improving through 2025 — before the Texas and California expansions took effect. The primary near-term mechanism is cash-collection timing and administrative cost; the secondary mechanism is unreimbursed care on subsequently denied claims. The company states it cannot predict the collectability impact over RCD's five-year term.

**A dated regulatory catalyst sits inside the investment horizon.** The 10-K states EHC expects HHS-OIG to issue its nationwide IRF claims audit report in 2026. OIG's 2018 precedent extrapolated an approximately 80% error rate from a 220-claim sample — methodology EHC disputes — and such a report can be used to argue for payment-system change. CMS released FY2027 proposed rules on April 2, 2026; the proposed IRF update is a near-term data point I could not confirm.

**Audit extrapolation.** Roughly $18.0M remains under appeal from the 2017 UPIC matter, plus approximately $12M at the ALJ level, $21M at the Departmental Appeals Board and $6M in federal district court as of year-end. No EHC hospital has progressed beyond the third round of Targeted Probe and Educate, so the consequences of failing TPE are untested.

**Concentration and partner fragility.** The filing specifically names Texas and Florida as regions where catastrophe risk is more acute given hospital density; five of the seven 2025 de novo openings were in Florida. JV partners are substantially all nonprofit systems under their own cost pressure, with a realized precedent: the January 2024 closure of the acute-care hospital housing the Eau Claire, Wisconsin JV produced a $10.4M impairment.

**Governance verification gap.** The 8-K index shows Item 5.02 filings dated on or about February 2 and February 20, 2026 whose contents were not read. Whether these reflect a director election, an officer appointment, a departure or a compensation action is unknown. Mark Tarr remained CEO through the Q1 release. This is an unresolved item rather than a negative finding.

Risk factors: 5.0. The regulatory risks are chronic and partly discounted — a 17–18.5x multiple on a business with this demand profile embeds a policy discount — but the RCD expansion took effect inside the current quarter, the OIG report window is 2026, and the FCF trough removes the balance-sheet cushion an investor would otherwise lean on.

## Investment Thesis

### Bull Case ($145, +31%)

Supply is fixed, demand grows 4% annually with the 75+ cohort, and only 15% of eligible patients reach an IRF — the addressable gap is large and structural. Occupancy at 75.9% and rising proves the referral funnel works. The 2026–2027 capex program (twelve de novos, 300–400 additional beds) converts a capacity constraint into a multi-year volume tailwind, and as the cohort seasons in 2027–2028 free cash flow inflects because maintenance capex is only $225–240M of the current $920–995M run rate. Leverage at 1.9x against a 4.50x covenant funds the program without stress. MedPAC has recommended no update or a cut in connection with every CMS IRF rulemaking since 2008 and CMS has raised rates anyway, including +3.0% and +2.9% in the last two rules. Four consecutive EPS beats and a raised FY2026 guide argue the $6.00 guidance midpoint is conservative. At 22x $6.54 next-year EPS, still below the ENSG/NHC end of the peer range, the stock is $144. Thirteen analysts with a $125 low target frame the downside as limited.

### Bear Case ($88, −21%)

The volume engine is slowing where it matters: same-store discharges +1.6% in Q1 2026 versus +3.4% in FY2025, and the 10-K names the mechanisms that would explain it structurally — MA denials, lower MA conversion rates, and conveners steering to home health as MA penetration heads from 54% toward 64%. The margin lever is exhausted: contract labor is down to roughly 1.2% of the workforce, and FY2026 guidance already concedes flat-to-down Adjusted EBITDA margin. Simultaneously the company enters a two-year window where growth capex exceeds free cash flow, cash taxes rise $26–56M, and shareholder returns increase — funded with 5.875% paper against a 4.50–4.75% legacy stack, in a rate regime where the long end is at multi-year highs. Owner FCF of $25–150M against an $11.0B market cap gives no valuation support. Item 1A states construction costs are rising faster than reimbursement, so the incremental ROIC on the largest capex program in company history is genuinely uncertain. Layered on that: RCD now covers 11.9% of Medicare claims with no Alabama hospital meeting the opt-out threshold in two consecutive cycles, an HHS-OIG nationwide report is expected in 2026, MedPAC has formally recommended a 7% base cut, and a unified post-acute PPS would, on MedPAC's own prior estimate, cut IRF payment 15% and shift dollars away from for-profit freestanding operators. Any one of those headlines takes the multiple to 14–15x and the stock to the high $80s.

## Investment Horizon & Exit Criteria

**Recommendation: HOLD (growth_hold).** The 12-month growth thesis is intact — mid-single-digit volume plus ~3% rate, 9% consensus EPS growth, and a capex program that relieves a real capacity constraint. What is missing is the entry asymmetry: roughly 11–14% to base case against 21% to bear case, with the two nearest catalysts (tonight's print, the FY2027 IRF final rule) both two-sided.

- **Base case: $123** — 18.8x next-year consensus $6.54, cross-checked at 10.5x an FY2027 Adjusted EBITDA estimate of ~$1,474M less ~$2.9B net debt across 99.4M shares ($126). **+11% from $110.89.**
- **Bull case: $145** (+31%) — 22x $6.54, requiring same-store discharges back above 3%, no adverse FY2027 rulemaking, and FY2027 capex guidance below $800M with visible FCF recovery.
- **Bear case: $88** (−21%) — 14.5x $6.00, roughly 5% below the $92.77 52-week low.
- **Timeframe:** 12 months, with three dated checkpoints — Q2 2026 results tonight, the CMS FY2027 IRF final rule (typically late summer), and the HHS-OIG nationwide IRF report expected sometime in 2026.

**Upgrade to BUY if:**
1. Same-store discharge growth returns to 3%+ for two consecutive quarters, with management attributing the Q1 figure to capacity rather than payer denial or admission selectivity; **or**
2. FY2026 capex lands at or below the $920M low end with FY2027 guided below $800M, and adjusted free cash flow returns to year-over-year growth — the combination that converts this from a cash-consuming to a cash-generating story; **or**
3. Price falls toward $95–100 (roughly 16x guided adjusted EPS) on non-fundamental weakness, which would restore 25%+ upside to base case.

**Downgrade to AVOID if:**
1. Congress or CMS advances any material IRF base-rate reduction or a site-neutral post-acute payment framework — the site-neutral tail is the only scenario that permanently impairs the model;
2. RCD non-affirmation in Texas or California drives a quantified increase in uncollectibility reserves (the 2023 precedent was $21.9M), or receivables denied-under-review reverse from $25.5M back above the $30.6M prior-year level;
3. FY2026 Adjusted EBITDA guidance is cut, or adjusted free cash flow turns negative on a trailing-four-quarter basis while buyback and dividend outlays continue.

**Reassessment: immediately after tonight's Q2 release** — the four items to check are same-store discharge growth, updated FY2026 capex and free-cash-flow guidance, any commentary on Texas/California RCD claim validation, and whether the guided flat EBITDA margin held in the quarter.

## Conclusion

Encompass Health is a high-quality operator in a structurally supply-constrained corner of healthcare, financed conservatively at 1.9x net leverage and 10.3x interest coverage, with a demographic tailwind that is arithmetic rather than speculative. The prior BUY rating was earned on margin expansion, volume growth and an improving balance sheet. Two of those three have now turned: FY2026 guidance concedes flat Adjusted EBITDA margin after three years of expansion, and same-store discharge growth halved to +1.6% in Q1 without a public explanation. Meanwhile the company enters the peak of the largest capital program in its history — capex up roughly $220M at midpoint, cash taxes up $26–56M, dividend raised twice, buybacks continuing, funded partly with 5.875% notes — which leaves owner-level free cash flow between roughly $25M and $150M on an $11.0B market capitalization.

At 18.5x guided adjusted EPS and 17.0x next-year consensus, EHC trades at a modest premium to a heterogeneous peer median with roughly 11–14% upside to my base case and 21% downside to the bear case. The unanimous sell-side Buy rating and $140.50 mean target reflect the same demographic story I already credit; with flat estimate revisions, they are not independent confirmation. Add to this a Q2 print hours away with implied volatility at 56.4% versus 26.1% realized and a ±9.6% priced move, plus an HHS-OIG nationwide IRF audit report expected within the year and RCD claims review newly covering 11.9% of Medicare claims. Own the position; do not commit new capital until either the volume question is answered or the price offers the margin of safety the cash-flow trough requires. HOLD, overall score 6.24.
