## Executive Summary

The week ending April 6 was dominated by a single binary: a 45-day ceasefire proposal from Pakistan, Egypt, and Turkey arrived alongside Trump's Tuesday deadline threatening to destroy Iranian power infrastructure if the Strait of Hormuz isn't reopened. Oil whipsawed accordingly — pulling back on peace hopes, then stabilizing as physical market reality reasserted. Saudi Arabia charging record $20/barrel premiums to Asian customers is the week's hardest data point on actual supply tightness.

Three structural developments crystallized this week. First, the rate regime is settled. March NFP at +178K (3x consensus), unemployment falling to 4.3%, IMF confirming no cuts in 2026, and Musalem/Williams reinforcing the hold stance collectively buried the rate cut narrative. The debate has moved permanently from "cut vs. hold" to "hold vs. hike," with April CPI as the trigger. FRED confirms Fed Funds at 3.64%, 5Y breakeven rising to 2.61% (up 4bp), and the 10Y-2Y spread stable at 51bp. Second, the private credit stress thesis gained its highest-profile institutional validation yet: Jamie Dimon's annual letter warned private credit losses will exceed expectations, PE buyouts slumped 36% to $172B in Q1, and Muddy Waters pivoted to shorting credit derivatives. This is the 14th independent data point for the cascade framework, and the first from JPMorgan's CEO. Third, the physical commodity supply chain is fracturing beyond oil — IRGC drone strikes damaged Kuwait's Mina al-Ahmadi refinery, Bahrain's Gulf Petrochemical Industries, and Abu Dhabi's Borouge plant, extending the supply disruption into petrochemicals, aluminium, and refined products that have no pipeline bypass.

The ceasefire proposal creates concentrated binary risk into Tuesday. UAE's demand that Hormuz access be guaranteed in any deal is the most revealing signal: even Gulf allies don't trust that Hormuz reopens automatically under a peace framework. OPEC+ pre-committing to boost output "when Hormuz reopens" confirms the cartel itself treats reopening as conditional. US intelligence assesses Iran is unlikely to ease its chokehold soon. India resuming Iranian oil purchases after a 7-year hiatus shows sanctions enforcement is breaking down. The peace-without-Hormuz framework from last week's deep dive remains the central structural thesis: peace ends the shooting but doesn't reopen the strait, leaving oil at $100-110 minimum under any negotiated outcome.

## Key Events & Analysis

### The Tuesday Binary and the Peace-Without-Hormuz Framework

Trump set a Tuesday night deadline for Iran to reopen Hormuz or face strikes on power infrastructure. A 45-day ceasefire proposal from Pakistan, Egypt, and Turkey was simultaneously delivered to both sides. Trump indicated a deal could happen as early as Monday. Iran signaled willingness to talk but has not accepted.

The market is pricing this binary symmetrically, with S&P futures rising on the ceasefire proposal. The pricing is wrong because the outcomes are asymmetric. Escalation (Iran rejects, Trump strikes power grid) produces a sharp, concentrated move higher in oil and defense, lower in equities. "Peace" produces a temporary relief rally that gets clawed back as markets discover that Hormuz doesn't reopen — because Iran has built institutional toll infrastructure, because insurance markets don't re-enter immediately, and because the US president has signaled he'll accept peace without Hormuz reopening.

Three data points from this week reinforce the structural chokepoint thesis: (1) Saudi $20/barrel premiums to Asian customers — physical tightness that ceasefire rhetoric cannot resolve in days; (2) UAE demanding Hormuz access guarantees — even allies doubt the strait reopens; (3) OPEC+ conditioning output increases on Hormuz reopening — the cartel itself treats reopening as a separate question from peace. All three come from parties with operational knowledge of the physical market; all three indicate the strait's status is negotiated separately from the war.

India's resumption of Iranian oil imports after a 7-year hiatus creates a new structural dynamic. Under supply pressure, the largest democracy has defied US sanctions policy to secure energy. This trade flow, settled outside the dollar system, won't reverse if a ceasefire is reached.

### Labor Market: Mixed Signals Beneath Strong Headlines

FRED data shows unemployment at 4.30% (down from 4.40%), nonfarm payrolls +178K, initial claims at 202K (down 9K). These are unambiguously healthy headline numbers that give the Fed complete cover to hold rates. FRED continuing claims at 1,841K (up 25K) provides the contrary signal. The pattern — falling initial claims (companies aren't firing) plus rising continuing claims (displaced workers can't find new jobs) — is consistent with a hiring freeze regime.

The 2-year auction at 21.7% dealer allocation from March 24 remains the weakest demand signal at the most rate-sensitive tenor. Combined with IMF's no-cut validation and the +178K print, the front end of the curve is locked: rates stay at 3.64% for the foreseeable future. FRED retail sales at +4,411 (February) showed a 0.6% month-over-month rebound, but this preceded the full oil shock transmission and likely reflects pre-war consumer behavior rather than forward strength.

### Dimon's Warning and the Credit Architecture

Jamie Dimon's annual letter warning that private credit losses will be "larger than feared" carries specific weight because JPMorgan is simultaneously the largest US bank, a major CLO underwriter, and an institution with extensive counterparty exposure to private credit funds. When Dimon warns publicly, he is signaling to his own institution's risk committee as much as to investors.

The causal chain connecting the macro themes: (1) IMF validates no rate cuts → (2) floating-rate private credit borrowers (85-95% of the market) get no relief → (3) AI disrupts SaaS revenue (7+ data points) → (4) leveraged SaaS borrowers (26% of private credit) struggle → (5) PE buyouts slump 36% → (6) exit liquidity evaporates → (7) NAV marks deteriorate → (8) fund gating spreads → (9) redemption pressure cascades through insurance and pension allocators.

The FT's $14B HY outflow figure and the Bloomberg leveraged loan / HY bond divergence (attributed to AI disruption of levered borrowers) connect the SaaS disruption thesis directly to credit positioning. Muddy Waters' shift from equity shorts to credit derivatives reflects the same assessment: the mispricing is in credit, not equity.

FRED HY spread data remains limited (most recent observation pre-dates the week's events), but the structural architecture is clear. Cascade probability holds at 60-68%, with March 31 NAV marks as the decisive data point. These should now be reporting through mid-week.

### Commodity Supply Chain Fracture: Beyond Oil

The Bloomberg Commodity Index's 24% Q1 surge led by energy is the broadest commodity rally since 2021-2022. The specific infrastructure damage this week — Kuwait's Mina al-Ahmadi refinery, Bahrain's Gulf Petrochemical Industries, Abu Dhabi's Borouge plant — extends the supply disruption into three categories that have no pipeline bypass: refined products, petrochemicals, and industrial gases. Combined with the prior aluminium smelter destruction (EGA, Alba) and Qatar's LNG force majeure, the non-oil dimensions of the commodity shock now encompass fertilizers, aluminium, helium, petrochemicals, LNG, LPG, and refined fuels.

FAO's warning about continued food price increases if the war persists adds the 15th CPI channel (food prices from fertilizer disruption → crop yields → consumer food costs, with a 3-6 month lag). FRED CPI at 327.46 (February) and Core CPI at 333.51 are pre-shock readings; the 14-15 active channels have not yet fully transmitted into measured inflation. April CPI exceeding 4.0% remains a 65-75% probability event.
