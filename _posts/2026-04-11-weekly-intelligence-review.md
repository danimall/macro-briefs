# Weekly Intelligence Review: April 5–11, 2026

## The Week's Story

This was a week defined by a ceasefire that solved less than markets assumed. The US-Iran conflict moved from acute escalation (Kharg Island strike, Iran rejecting a 45-day ceasefire, Trump's Tuesday deadline) to a fragile two-week truce announced Wednesday — and by Friday the truce was already fraying, with oil rebounding above $97, Hormuz still functionally closed at 15 ships/day, and three destabilizing events (Saudi pipeline attack, Iran breach accusations, Israeli killing of Hezbollah chief Qassem) eroding the agreement's credibility. Oil traced a $94-to-$116-to-$94-to-$97+ round trip. The S&P 500 nearly erased its entire war-period decline. The rate futures market repriced to 43% probability of a 2026 cut. By Friday, the options market had split cleanly into two contradictory assessments: large-cap equities priced full resolution (SPY IV at 15.4%, essentially fair value) while credit (HYG OI P/C at 3.89, near-term IV 24.6%), small caps (IWM OI P/C 2.07), Japan (EWJ IV 59.9%, escalating every single day), and Europe (VGK newly stressed) continued pricing material deterioration.

The week's most important analytical development was the convergence of four previously independent credit stress channels into a single narrative. The FT reported $20 billion in Q1 redemption requests hitting Apollo, Ares, and Blackstone — the first hard flow data converting the private credit thesis from positioning signal to operational reality. This arrived alongside $14 billion in YTD junk bond outflows, Bloomberg's documentation of AI-driven leveraged loan divergence, and private credit fund bonds already at one-year lows. Jamie Dimon's "larger than feared" warning from the prior week now reads as prescient rather than cautionary. Meanwhile, March CPI printed 3.3%, the first reading to capture initial war-period energy passthrough, confirming that inflation is embedding structurally even as the ceasefire temporarily compressed oil futures. The FOMC minutes, published the same day markets repriced to 43% cut odds, showed a growing faction open to hikes. Physical crude benchmarks in Europe and Africa hit records while Brent futures traded at $97 — a divergence that tells you the financial market is underpricing the actual cost of delivered energy.

## Narrative Arcs

### Arc 1: From Escalation to Fragile Ceasefire — The 72-Hour Round Trip

The week opened with the sharpest binary risk since the conflict began. By Sunday/Monday, Trump had struck Kharg Island (Iran's primary oil export terminal, 2.5-3M bpd, 3-5 year rebuild), Iran had rejected the 45-day ceasefire proposal, and Trump set a Tuesday evening deadline. Oil pushed past $115. SocGen floated a $200 scenario. IWM $247 puts traded at 102.4x volume/OI — institutional-scale bets on small-cap downside within one week. QQQ $573 puts traded at 205x vol/OI. The options market was pricing a catastrophic outcome with real money.

Then Wednesday: ceasefire. Oil crashed 16% intraday — Brent to $94, the largest single-session decline since 2020. S&P futures surged 2.6%. Rate cut odds jumped to 43%. The market treated this as resolution.

By Thursday, the resolution was unraveling. Oil rebounded above $97. Iran accused the US of breaching terms. Israel killed Hezbollah chief Qassem — a move outside the ceasefire's scope that complicates Iranian domestic politics around continued negotiations. The Saudi East-West pipeline, the single most important piece of energy infrastructure mitigating the crisis, was attacked during the ceasefire. Maersk explicitly stated it remains cautious about resuming Hormuz shipping. The ADNOC CEO said the strait is "effectively shut."

By Friday, the physical-futures divergence had become the week's most important price signal. European and African crude benchmarks hit records while Brent futures sat at $97. European airports faced jet fuel shortages within three weeks. Hormuz was processing 15 ships/day under an Iranian crypto-toll system that no major P&I club can legally engage with. Hapag-Lloyd estimated 6-8 weeks for normalization even under stable peace — and the peace was not stable.

The net effect: oil's round trip from $107 (week open) to $115+ (Tuesday) to $94 (Wednesday) to $97+ (Friday) left the structural floor intact at $95-105 under a durable ceasefire scenario, but the ceasefire's durability is now assessed at 35-40% probability of collapse within two weeks.

### Arc 2: Credit Markets Refuse to Celebrate

The most analytically significant thread of the week was the persistent divergence between equity and credit market assessments. This divergence widened through every phase of the week — escalation, ceasefire, and ceasefire erosion — suggesting credit markets are pricing a structural deterioration that is independent of geopolitical outcomes.

The data accumulated in sequence. Monday: HYG OI P/C at 5.85, escalating through a 6% equity rally. Tuesday: 6.04, with volume P/C at 0.20 (overwhelmingly put-buying). Wednesday ceasefire: HYG near-term IV spiked to 33.3% — the highest reading of the entire crisis — at the exact moment equities surged and oil collapsed. This was anomalous and alarming: the ceasefire that eased equity stress appeared to intensify credit stress, possibly because the $21 oil decline created mark-to-market losses for energy-exposed private credit portfolios. Thursday: HYG shifted to contango as the spike normalized. Friday: re-steepened to 24.6% near-term IV (19.8pp above HV), with OI P/C still at 3.89.

The fundamental driver crystallized midweek with the FT's report of $20 billion in Q1 redemption requests hitting major private credit funds. This converts the credit cascade thesis from positioning analysis to operational data. Redemptions force managers to either liquidate assets at potentially distressed prices or gate investors, creating further confidence damage. The timing is critical: these requests were filed before the ceasefire and before oil crashed from $115 to $94, meaning the oil decline creates an additional impairment vector on top of the pre-existing stress.

Four independent channels are now active simultaneously: (1) $20B private credit redemptions, (2) $14B public junk bond outflows, (3) AI-driven leveraged loan divergence documented by Bloomberg, and (4) private credit fund bonds already at one-year lows before any of these catalysts materialized. Dimon's warning, Muddy Waters' credit short, and Ackman's "complacency fund" all point in the same direction. The March 31 NAV marks — the data that converts thesis to fact — should be reporting now.

By Friday, large-cap equities (SPY IV at 15.4%, perfectly balanced OI P/C at 1.04) and credit (HYG at 24.6%, OI P/C 3.89) were making flatly contradictory assessments. Historical precedent says credit wins this disagreement roughly 70% of the time.

### Arc 3: The Rate Repricing That Shouldn't Be

The market's jump to 43% rate cut probability on Wednesday was the week's most aggressive single-variable repricing. It was based on one input — oil dropping from $115 to $94 — applied to a 15+ variable inflation model. By Friday, the hard data was already contradicting it.

The FOMC minutes, released Wednesday (the same day as the ceasefire), showed a "growing faction" open to rate hikes. March CPI printed 3.3% on Friday — the first reading capturing initial war-period energy passthrough, with core PCE already at 3.0% in February and rising for three consecutive months before the war. The San Francisco Fed published research documenting "nonmarket-based inflation" keeping headline elevated — an intellectual framework that cuts both ways, providing cover for patience but also documenting why inflation may be resistant to rate tools.

The institutional consensus was unanimously against cuts. The IMF stated "little room for cuts." Musalem backed hold. Wells Fargo abandoned 2026 cut expectations. Only Miran advocated cuts, isolated without institutional support. The 5-year breakeven at 2.56% by week's end was 120-180bps below the model's H2 2026 central case of 3.8-4.3%.

The critical point: even if oil stays at $97, six weeks of $100+ oil already produced diesel at $5.29/gallon (embedded in trucking contracts), fertilizer supply disruption (86% of Gulf-to-East Africa flows ceased), petrochemical feedstock cost increases contracted into manufacturer pricing, and shipping reroute surcharges from Cape of Good Hope diversions (+112% in vessel traffic). These costs flow through the CPI pipeline on 4-6 week lags, meaning April CPI — the first full-month capture of war-period pricing — will be more inflationary than March regardless of what oil does from here.

### Arc 4: Japan as the Week's Emerging Stress Signal

The most unexpected persistent signal of the week was Japan. EWJ options IV escalated every single day: 50.2% (Tuesday) → 53.3% (Wednesday) → 58.6% (Thursday) → 59.9% (Friday). This was the highest, most persistent stress reading across the entire options dataset — all markets, all time periods — and it escalated through the ceasefire rather than easing.

The mechanism is a triple bind with no resolution under current conditions. Japan is 100% energy-import dependent, meaning the Hormuz disruption hits its cost structure directly regardless of ceasefire headlines. The BOJ maintains a hawkish stance. The IMF urged continued BOJ tightening during the energy crisis. Japanese corporates face rising input costs from energy and rising financing costs from rates simultaneously. Yen weakness accelerates the cost of energy imports denominated in dollars.

This received minimal commentary in the daily briefs relative to its options market intensity. The daily briefs flagged it with probability assignments (25-30% initially, revised to 28-33% by Friday), but the four consecutive days of IV escalation through a ceasefire suggests the market is pricing something more urgent than the briefs' moderate probability suggests.

## Signal vs. Noise

### Overrated

**The ceasefire announcement itself.** Wednesday's 16% oil crash and equity surge treated the ceasefire as resolution. By Friday, Hormuz remained effectively closed, physical crude was at records, and three destabilizing events had occurred. The market's initial reaction was a binary repricing of a non-binary event. The ceasefire matters — it prevents the escalation tail — but it doesn't address the insurance closure, infrastructure destruction, crypto-toll friction, or multi-year industrial rebuild timelines that constitute the structural disruption.

**Goldman's "generational buying opportunity" in tech.** The QQQ call skew went from -68.7% (Wednesday, call-dominant euphoria) to normalized by Friday. The tech rally trade was expressed and partially unwound within 48 hours. The opportunities in specific hardware names (AVGO, GOOG, TSM) are real, but the "generational" framing applied to broad tech is contradicted by the rate regime (no cuts) and SaaS displacement thesis.

**Tuesday's binary deadline.** The daily briefs spent significant analytical capital on Trump's Tuesday 8pm ET deadline. The IWM 102x vol/OI puts and QQQ 205x vol/OI puts represented institutional-scale bets on a specific hour's outcome. The deadline passed, events took a different course, and the extreme near-term positioning expired. Binary event analysis consumed attention that could have been spent on the structural signals (physical-futures divergence, credit flow data) that proved more durable.

### Underrated

**Physical-futures crude divergence.** This received one mention in Friday's brief but is arguably the week's most important price signal. When European and African crude benchmarks hit records while Brent futures trade at $97, the implication is that financial markets are systematically underpricing the actual cost of energy in the real economy. Every company buying physical crude — refiners, airlines, chemical producers — is paying more than the futures curve suggests. This divergence, if it persists, means inflation passthrough will be larger than models based on futures prices predict.

**EWJ's four consecutive days of IV escalation.** Japan received paragraph-level treatment in the daily briefs but escalated from a monitoring item to the highest-stress reading in the entire global options dataset. A 59.9% near-term IV on a major developed-market equity ETF, escalating through a ceasefire, suggests something more than passing concern. Japan's triple bind (energy dependency + BOJ tightening + IMF pressure) has no resolution mechanism under current conditions.

**The AI-driven leveraged loan divergence.** Bloomberg's report that leveraged loans are underperforming high-yield bonds due to AI disruption of specific borrowers opens a credit stress channel that most macro models miss entirely. This is a structural default risk vector that operates independently of energy prices, interest rates, or geopolitical outcomes. Combined with Muddy Waters' explicit AI-labor-displacement credit short, it suggests the credit cycle has a technology-driven component that warrants dedicated analysis.

**Iran's crypto-toll requirement.** This received brief mentions but its implications for insurance market normalization are profound. P&I clubs cannot authorize payments to a sanctioned entity, especially not in cryptocurrency, without regulatory and sanctions liability. This means Hormuz cannot normalize through normal commercial channels regardless of diplomatic outcomes, until Iran either drops the crypto requirement or the sanctions framework changes. The crypto-toll is the most durable barrier to Hormuz normalization and received less analysis than the military dimensions.

## Lessons for Next Week

1. **The physical-futures crude divergence needs dedicated tracking.** If European/African physical benchmarks remain at records while Brent futures trade $10-15 lower, every macro model calibrated to futures prices is underestimating actual inflationary passthrough. Build explicit physical-delivery premium tracking into the daily framework.

2. **When equity and credit options disagree, assign higher weight to credit.** This week's HYG signal persistence through every market phase — escalation, ceasefire, ceasefire erosion — was the most reliable analytical signal. SPY normalization (15.4% IV) while HYG remains crisis-priced (24.6%, OI P/C 3.89) is the setup for either credit recovery or equity decline. Historical base rates favor credit being right roughly 70% of the time. Next week, if NAV marks begin reporting, credit will either be vindicated or this divergence collapses — either outcome is high-information.

3. **Binary event framing consumed too much analytical bandwidth this week.** The Tuesday deadline dominated Monday and Tuesday briefs with detailed scenario trees, unusual activity analysis, and probability assignments for an event that was superseded by a different ceasefire framework within 24 hours. The structural analysis (Sunday deep dive, physical supply assessment, credit flow data) proved far more durable than the event-driven analysis. Allocate more space to structural signals, less to hour-by-hour binary outcomes.

4. **Company-level research should be cross-checked against thematic inclusions.** The AVAV example — added to the defense beneficiary list on thematic grounds while company research independently rated it AVOID — illustrates the risk of sector-theme reasoning overriding fundamental analysis. Every name added to a thematic watchlist should be cross-referenced against available company research before inclusion.

5. **Ceasefire durability should be assessed against the number of parties not bound by the agreement.** This week's ceasefire was bilateral (US-Iran), but Israel, Houthi proxies, and potentially IRGC-aligned militias operate outside its scope. The Qassem killing and Saudi pipeline attack both occurred within 48 hours from parties the ceasefire doesn't restrain. Any future ceasefire analysis should explicitly enumerate the actors not covered and assign destabilization probability accordingly.
