# Weekly Intelligence Review: March 10–16, 2026

## The Week's Story

The week of March 10–16 was defined by the collision between two forces: an oil market whipsawing $32 in five days as diplomatic rhetoric and physical reality diverged, and a credit deterioration thesis that crossed from pattern recognition into institutional confirmation. Oil opened the week near $110 after Trump declared the Iran war "very complete," dropped to $88 on the IEA's record 400M-barrel SPR release, rebounded to $101 as six vessel attacks and 90% tanker traffic decline demonstrated the physical disruption was intensifying rather than resolving, and closed the week near $100 with Trump explicitly rejecting ceasefire talks and Israel announcing 3+ more weeks of combat. The $32 round-trip destroyed capital at three confirmed hedge funds (Caxton lost $630M, Citadel and ExodusPoint also reported losses) and forced five position-sizing revisions in the daily briefs — an honest demonstration that conflict-driven commodity prices cannot be optimized through headline-driven adjustment.

Beneath the oil volatility, the credit cascade thesis accumulated from 12 to 21 independent data points across the week, crossing the most important qualitative threshold yet: JPMorgan marking down software loan portfolios (a balance sheet action, not commentary), four separate fund complexes imposing withdrawal restrictions (BlackRock, Starwood, Morgan Stanley, Cliffwater), Partners Group's chair warning default rates could double to above 5%, and Davidson Kempner's Yoseloff stating a "substantial portion" of PE firms are stressed or distressed. The macro data completed the stagflationary picture with unusual clarity: Q4 GDP revised to 0.7%, core PCE at 3.1%, NFP at -92K, retail sales at -0.2%, gasoline up 21%, and quit rates declining. Historical episodes where negative payrolls coincided with above-target inflation (1974, 1980, 2008) all produced recessions. The March 18–19 FOMC enters with no good options.

## Narrative Arcs

### Arc 1: The Oil Whipsaw — From $120 to $88 to $101

**Tuesday March 10:** Trump declared the Iran war "very complete," announced sanctions waivers, and Saudi Aramco began planning Red Sea rerouting of 70% of crude exports. Oil dropped 7–8% from $120 toward $110–112, the sharpest single-day decline of the conflict. Brief #53 raised de-escalation probability from 8–12% back to 15–22% and recommended resuming energy scale-out at moderate pace (targeting 40–50% reduction over two weeks). BofA's derivatives desk warned it was "too early to celebrate."

**Wednesday March 11:** The IEA announced a record 400M-barrel SPR release — the largest coordinated reserve deployment in history. Oil dropped to approximately $88, a 25% retrace from the $120 peak. Brief #55 recommended accelerating the energy scale-out to 30–40% retained, estimating a 4–6 week SPR buffer. The oil equity market diverged from spot crude: energy shares lagged, pricing the spike as temporary. A Greek tanker successfully transited Hormuz with Saudi cargo.

**Thursday March 12:** Oil rebounded $13 to $101 within 24 hours. Six merchant vessel attacks, 90% tanker traffic decline through Hormuz, and the IEA labeling this the "largest supply disruption in history" all occurred *after* the SPR release. Brief #57 acknowledged the $88 was an overshoot and reverted energy positioning to 50–60% retained — the fifth revision in six briefs. The brief explicitly stated: "the correct lesson is to set a position and stop adjusting."

**Friday March 13:** Trump stated the US "benefits from high oil prices" and that the priority is "stopping Iran." The US eased sanctions on stranded Russian oil. Mojtaba Khamenei publicly declared Hormuz should remain closed. Oil settled near $100 with the largest weekly gain since the 1980s.

**Saturday March 14:** Israel announced 3+ weeks remaining. Saudi Arabia cut production 20% to 8M bpd, removing ~2M bpd from supply. SPR buffer arithmetic worsened from 4–6 weeks to 9–14 days. De-escalation probability dropped to 5–8% for rapid resolution.

**Net assessment:** The week proved that the physical disruption is intensifying while SPR and diplomatic measures provide temporary price suppression that the market initially overshoot-priced. The central oil range ended the week at $98–108, with the SPR buffer now estimated at 9–14 days against a confirmed 3+ week conflict floor. The asymmetry has shifted: the downside from a ceasefire (oil to $85–90) is smaller than the upside risk from SPR depletion (oil to $115–130).

### Arc 2: The Credit Cascade — From Thesis to Institutional Confirmation

The credit cascade thesis entered the week with 11 data points and 42–48% probability. By Sunday it had 21 data points and 55–60% probability. The progression was qualitatively different from prior weeks: instead of adding anecdotes and sell-side recommendations, this week added bank balance sheet actions, fund-level withdrawal restrictions, and industry insider warnings.

**Tuesday March 10:** Boaz Weinstein's Saba Capital launched a tender offer on Blue Owl's non-traded private credit fund at 34.9% discount to NAV. This was the first price discovery event — a sophisticated credit investor publicly asserting private credit assets are worth roughly 65 cents on the dollar. Citadel and ExodusPoint reported losses from oil/bond volatility. Goldman Sachs was marketing short-loan strategies targeting AI-disrupted software companies.

**Wednesday March 11:** JPMorgan marked down private credit loan portfolios and pulled back lending to private credit firms after writing down software loans. This was the week's most consequential credit development — the largest US bank taking balance sheet action rather than issuing commentary. The mechanism is self-reinforcing: JPM devalues collateral → borrowing capacity for private credit firms declines → origination slows → companies relying on private credit refinancing face higher costs → credit quality deteriorates → more markdowns.

**Thursday March 12:** Morgan Stanley and Cliffwater capped withdrawals from private credit funds — the 14th and 15th data points. This brought total fund-level withdrawal restrictions to four (adding to BlackRock and Starwood). Partners Group's chair publicly warned default rates could double to above 5%. A Goldman executive privately admitted clients were "glad" the Iran war distracted from private credit scrutiny. Deutsche Bank announced expansion of its €26B private credit book on the same day Partners Group warned about doubling defaults — the classic late-cycle pattern of late entrants scaling up as early movers restrict.

**Friday–Saturday March 13–14:** Davidson Kempner's Yoseloff warned that a "substantial portion" of PE firms are stressed or distressed. The FT confirmed billions in retail redemptions from private credit funds, adding a new vector (retail, not just institutional) to the redemption pressure. Amazon's €14.5B euro bond issuance contributed to credit market crowding-out: institutional investors absorbing investment-grade Big Tech paper have less capacity for BB/B-rated private credit.

**Net assessment:** The credit cascade probability rose from 42–48% to 55–60% across the week. The key remaining threshold is Ares/KKR semi-liquid fund redemption data, with March 31 quarter-end creating a structural focal point for retail redemptions. The APO/BX pair trade received its strongest fundamental confirmation: APO originated a $500M bilateral bond deal during the stress while BX peers faced withdrawal restrictions.

### Arc 3: The Stagflation Data Set Completes

The macro data released or confirmed during the week filled in the final dimensions of a textbook stagflation regime, arriving with unusual simultaneity.

**Growth:** Q4 GDP revised to 0.7% — the weakest quarter since the pandemic recovery.

**Inflation:** Core PCE at 3.1%, February CPI at 2.4% (stale, pre-oil spike). Six confirmed supply disruption channels (oil, fertilizer/sulphur, pharmaceuticals, semiconductors via petrochemicals, fashion/consumer goods, helium from Qatar LNG halt) with staggered CPI lags through H2 2026.

**Employment:** NFP remained at -92K, now explained by the Section 301 trade probes against 60 economies as a rational corporate hiring freeze under policy uncertainty rather than a cyclical demand decline. Weekly claims at 213K provided a genuine counterpoint suggesting the -92K may overstate structural damage, but the Section 301 framework implies weakness persists until the uncertainty resolves (6–12 months).

**Consumer:** Retail sales -0.2%. Gasoline up 21% in one month. The SAVE student loan plan terminated, adding a fifth headwind to consumer spending. Campbell's hit a 23-year stock low with both segments declining. Ulta Beauty issued weak guidance explicitly citing "global conflicts" — the first major retailer to directly attribute spending weakness to the Iran war.

**Globally synchronized:** UK consumer spending slowed (BRC data). China raised fuel prices by the largest amount in four years. India's Sensex fell 1,000+ points with accelerating FII outflows. China and India imposed petrochemical force majeure declarations.

**Central bank response:** Collins, Hammack, and Goolsbee all signaled inflation is "too high" while Miran argued for cuts based on -92K NFP. The internal Fed split reflects the impossibility of the policy problem: the inflation driver (oil) is exogenous to monetary policy, meaning neither cuts nor hikes address the root cause.

### Arc 4: Supply Chain Disruption Broadens to Six Independent Channels

What began as an oil price shock progressively revealed itself as a multi-channel supply disruption with compounding CPI implications. By week's end, six confirmed channels were operating simultaneously:

1. **Oil/energy:** $88–$101 range, $100+ sustained. 90% tanker traffic decline through Hormuz.
2. **Fertilizer/sulphur:** Gulf sulphur disruption during Northern Hemisphere spring planting (March–May). Farmers making input decisions now face costs that embed into the 2026 crop production cost structure for 6–12 months regardless of conflict resolution.
3. **Pharmaceuticals:** ₹5,000 crore Indian pharma export impact, 10–20% medicine price increases, temperature-sensitive cancer drug air routes disrupted.
4. **Semiconductors:** Samsung Display CEO confirmed cost pressures from petrochemical input disruption. Wafer start constraints assessed at 15–20% probability within 4–8 weeks.
5. **Fashion/consumer goods:** Zara and H&M supply delays confirmed.
6. **Helium:** Qatar LNG halt disrupted helium supply for semiconductor manufacturing, MRI equipment, and aerospace. JPMorgan upgraded an industrial gas supplier on this thesis.

Chinese petrochemical force majeure (March 9 effective date) and Hindalco's aluminium force majeure moved the thesis from estimate to legal declaration. Standard CPI models built on single-channel oil passthrough will understate the cumulative price impact. Combined plausible peak CPI was revised to 3.0–4.8% by week's end, with the upper bound reflecting compounding across channels.

## Signal vs. Noise

### Overrated

**Trump's "very complete" declaration (Tuesday).** This dominated Tuesday morning's brief and triggered a 7–8% oil decline. Within 72 hours, Trump had shifted to "US benefits from high oil prices," rejected ceasefire talks, and struck Kharg Island military targets. The rhetorical signal was completely reversed by the end of the week. Five data points supporting de-escalation on Tuesday (Trump rhetoric, sanctions waivers, Aramco rerouting, SPR release, Putin discussions) were overwhelmed by structural factors favoring continuation.

**The $88 oil trough (Wednesday).** The IEA's 400M-barrel SPR release drove oil to $88, which appeared to validate the aggressive scale-out thesis. Within 24 hours, oil rebounded $13 as the physical disruption proved immune to supply interventions. The $88 print was a brief overshoot that would have generated significant losses for anyone who accelerated exits at that level.

**February CPI at 2.4% (Thursday).** This number was universally acknowledged as stale (pre-oil spike) and irrelevant to forward-looking analysis. Yet it received significant coverage. The actionable number was core PCE at 3.1%, which was pre-conflict but at least partially captured the direction of travel.

**Dan Ives calling IGV 19% decline "massively overblown" (Thursday).** One sell-side analyst versus JPMorgan balance sheet markdowns, Goldman institutional short-loan strategies, and four fund-level withdrawal restrictions. The asymmetry in evidentiary weight was clear at the time and was correctly identified as noise.

### Underrated

**JPMorgan marking down software loan portfolios (Wednesday).** This received appropriate coverage within the briefs but deserved even more emphasis relative to oil headlines. A Tier 1 bank taking balance sheet action against a specific sector is categorically more informative than any amount of sell-side commentary. This was the data point that moved the credit thesis from "accumulating warnings" to "institutional confirmation."

**The Saudi 20% production cut to 8M bpd (Saturday).** This received one paragraph in the daily brief but fundamentally altered the SPR buffer arithmetic — from 4–6 weeks to 9–14 days. Removing 2M bpd of Saudi supply on top of the Hormuz disruption makes the total supply loss approximately 7–9M bpd at peak, which is substantially larger than the SPR can address.

**The helium supply disruption from Qatar LNG halt (Friday).** This was the sixth confirmed supply disruption channel and was barely on any mainstream radar. Qatar is one of the world's largest helium producers as an LNG processing byproduct. Helium scarcity affects semiconductor wafer fabrication, MRI equipment, and aerospace — creating a supply-side inflation vector that standard CPI models do not capture.

**Section 301 probes against 60 economies as the explanation for the claims-vs.-NFP divergence (Friday–Saturday).** The NFP-claims puzzle persisted for three weeks without satisfying resolution until the Section 301 framework provided it: companies facing simultaneous trade, conflict, credit, and monetary policy uncertainty rationally freeze hiring while retaining existing workers. This framing has direct implications for the persistence of labor market weakness — it resolves only when the uncertainty resolves, and Section 301 adds 6–12 months to that timeline.

**Government bonds failing as safe haven (Saturday–Sunday).** Treasuries, gilts, and bunds all sold off during an active geopolitical crisis. In every prior geopolitical crisis since 1970, developed-market sovereign debt rallied. This structural break invalidates the 60/40 portfolio for this cycle, has direct mechanical implications for mortgage rates (higher yields → higher mortgage costs → weaker housing), and creates an institutional void where traditional defensive allocators have no clear destination for capital.
