# Sunday Deep Dive: The Hormuz Toll Corridor — How a Permanent Chokepoint Regime Is Repricing Global Commerce, Energy, and Inflation for a Decade

## The Setup

Every day before February 28, 2026, approximately 20.9 million barrels of oil — one-fifth of the world's entire petroleum consumption — passed through a channel of water 21 miles wide at its narrowest point. Eighty million tons of liquefied natural gas. Thirty percent of global fertilizer trade. Half the world's sulfur shipments. One-third of the planet's helium, critical for semiconductor fabrication and MRI scanners. The aluminium that becomes airplane fuselages and beer cans. The petrochemical feedstocks that become plastic packaging, synthetic fibers, and medical devices. All of it squeezed through the Strait of Hormuz between Iran and Oman, a waterway that the U.S. Navy had spent forty years guaranteeing would remain open to commercial shipping.

Then Iran closed it in 72 hours. Not with mines or missiles — though it has 5,000-6,000 of the former and fired plenty of the latter — but with something far more modern and arguably more durable: it made the strait uninsurable. War risk premiums surged from 0.01% of hull value to 1.0-1.5%, a 100-to-150-fold increase. Seven of the twelve Protection & Indemnity clubs that collectively cover roughly 90% of global commercial tonnage simply cancelled war risk coverage for the Persian Gulf entirely. The cost of insuring a single VLCC transit leapt from $10,000-$12,000 to $1-1.8 million per voyage — and then became unavailable at any price. Traffic through the strait collapsed by 90%.

Five weeks later, as of early April 2026, the market is treating this as a temporary war premium. Oil drops sharply on any peace headline — it fell from $116 to $100 in a single session when Trump hinted the war could end within 2-3 weeks, before snapping back above $107 when his primetime address clarified he meant 2-3 more weeks of "extremely hard" strikes. Options traders bid down near-term implied volatility on the S&P 500 to 16.3%, *below* realized volatility, the first time that has happened during this entire crisis. The consensus view embedded in futures curves and analyst notes is that this is a disruption measured in weeks or months — ugly, expensive, but ultimately transient.

The physical world disagrees. Gulf states are commissioning multi-year bypass pipelines that cost $5-20 billion and take 3-7 years to build. Iran's parliament has passed legislation formalizing a transit fee regime. Ships are paying up to $2 million per vessel to transit through Iranian territorial waters near Larak Island, with payments settled in Chinese yuan through Chinese maritime intermediaries. And the most underappreciated signal of the week: the Wall Street Journal reported that President Trump has told aides he is willing to end the war without reopening the Strait of Hormuz. White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt confirmed that Hormuz reopening is not among the "core objectives" of Operation Epic Fury.

This is the most important sentence in global macro right now, and almost nobody is trading it. If the United States — the only military power capable of forcing the strait open — will accept peace without doing so, then the Hormuz disruption is not a war premium. It is the new regime.

## How We Got Here

To understand why the current crisis is structurally different from anything in the modern energy era, you need to understand two things: why the strait closed so fast, and why no one's plan to deal with it works.

### The Insurance Weapon

The conventional playbook for Hormuz risk was built on the 1980s Tanker War. Between 1981 and 1988, Iran and Iraq attacked 411 commercial ships in the Persian Gulf. It was violent. It was headline-generating. And it barely mattered: fewer than 2% of Gulf shipping was disrupted. The strait never closed. Oil prices actually *declined* during the period. Every scenario-planning exercise conducted by the International Maritime Organization, every war game run by naval strategists, every risk assessment built by energy analysts drew on this experience. The lesson was: the strait can absorb punishment. Shipping continues. Insurance adjusts. Commerce routes around individual incidents.

This assumption died in the first 72 hours of Operation Epic Fury.

The difference wasn't primarily military, though Iran's capabilities have transformed — GPS-guided naval mines, Khalij-e Fars anti-ship ballistic missiles traveling at Mach 3-4, electronic warfare systems that jam and spoof navigation, IRGC fast attack boat swarms. The difference was the insurance market's response. In the 1980s, war risk insurance was expensive but available. In 2026, it evaporated. As the House of Saud's comprehensive analysis noted, "the shipping industry prepared for the wrong war." The IMO's three scenario-planning exercises never modeled an insurance-driven closure because the precedent didn't exist. Actuaries, not admirals, shut the Strait of Hormuz.

This matters enormously for the permanence of the disruption. Military blockades end when the military force is removed. Insurance-market blockades end when underwriters decide the risk is acceptable again — and insurance markets, by nature, are backward-looking and slow to re-enter exited risk categories. After the Houthi disruptions in the Red Sea beginning in late 2023, war risk premiums for the Bab el-Mandeb strait took many months to normalize even after the acute threat diminished. For Hormuz, where the threat is orders of magnitude greater and the demonstrated damage to commercial vessels is ongoing (an Iranian missile struck a tanker in Qatari waters as recently as April 1), the insurance market's return to pre-war pricing will be measured in years, not weeks.

### Iran's Pivot: From Blockade to Toll Booth

Here is where the story gets genuinely novel. Iran didn't simply close the strait and leave it closed. Within weeks, it established something that has no modern precedent in international maritime law: a permission-based transit regime through its own territorial waters.

The mechanism works like this. Ships wishing to transit the strait are directed to the Larak Island corridor, a passage through Iranian territorial waters south of the island. They must submit their IMO identification numbers, crew manifests, and destination information to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps for screening. If approved, they receive a clearance code and are escorted through the corridor. Since March 13, 2026, all 57 commercial transits logged through the strait have used this detour — there is no alternative route that avoids Iranian-controlled waters.

Iran's parliament approved legislation formalizing this into a toll collection system. Ships are reportedly paying up to $2 million per vessel. The payments are settled in Chinese yuan through Chinese maritime intermediaries — a detail that carries geopolitical significance we'll return to. Countries with "friendly relations" to Iran — China, India, Pakistan, Malaysia — receive preferential treatment. The first confirmed crossing by a major Western container carrier (a CMA CGM ship, per the Financial Times) didn't happen until early April and confirmed the model rather than contradicting it: Iran allowed selective passage, demonstrating that it can monetize control over the chokepoint without outright closure.

Legal experts note that Iran has no authority under UNCLOS (the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea) to impose tolls on passage through a natural strait — the legal right of "transit passage" through international straits is well-established. But the Suez and Panama Canal comparison that some commentators have drawn is legally invalid; those are man-made channels whose operating nations have recognized sovereign authority to charge fees. Hormuz is a natural strait, and Iran's toll regime is an illegal assertion of sovereignty over international waters. The problem, of course, is that international maritime law is enforced by navies, and the U.S. Navy — the only force capable of enforcement — has been told by its commander-in-chief that reopening the strait isn't a core objective.

### The White House Signal

This is the piece most market participants are missing, and it's worth dwelling on. Secretary of State Marco Rubio hedged by saying the strait would "reopen one way or another," suggesting some kind of multinational coalition effort. But retired General James Mattis — arguably the most respected living American strategic thinker on the Gulf — said publicly at CERAWeek that the U.S. is in a "tough spot," that Iran has a "vote" on when the war ends, and that regime change is "delusional."

The operational reality supports Mattis over Rubio. Iran's mine-laying capability (5,000-6,000 naval mines) means that even if the IRGC Navy were completely destroyed, clearing the strait for commercial traffic could take months. GPS jamming creates an electronic dimension of blockade with no established counter-protocol for commercial vessels. And the U.S. Navy itself admitted it cannot currently provide convoy escorts for commercial tankers — it doesn't have enough ships positioned in-theater.

The combination of these factors — insurance market closure, Iran's institutional toll regime, the White House's stated indifference to Hormuz reopening, and the practical impossibility of forcing the strait open quickly — creates a structural condition that persists regardless of whether a ceasefire is reached. Peace ends the shooting. It does not reopen the strait.

## How the Machine Works

### The Bypass That Isn't

The single most important mitigating asset in the world right now is Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline, running from the Eastern Province oil fields to the Red Sea port of Yanbu. Operating at its full capacity of 7 million barrels per day, it allows Saudi Arabia to export crude oil without transiting Hormuz. The UAE's Abu Dhabi-Fujairah pipeline adds approximately 1.5 million bpd through a route to the Gulf of Oman that partially avoids the chokepoint. Aramco CEO Amin Nasser has described the East-West pipeline as "the main route we are capitalizing on," and it is genuinely a masterstroke of strategic infrastructure planning.

Combined, these pipelines can handle roughly 8.5 million barrels per day — approximately 28% of the 20.9 million bpd that normally transits Hormuz. That number sounds meaningful, and for crude oil, it is a genuine partial offset. Without it, the crisis would be far worse. The Saudi pipeline is the single reason oil is at $107-120 rather than $150-175.

But here is the critical limitation that most analysis glosses over: **pipelines carry only crude oil.** They cannot transport liquefied natural gas, which requires cryogenic tankers operating at -162°C. They cannot transport liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), the cooking and heating fuel that 1.5 billion people in South and Southeast Asia depend on daily. They cannot transport refined petroleum products like jet fuel or diesel. They cannot transport petrochemical feedstocks, fertilizers, aluminium, helium, or any of the dozens of other commodities that transit Hormuz by ship.

The IEA's characterization of this as the "biggest oil supply disruption in history" is technically correct but understates the problem. It is simultaneously the biggest LNG disruption in history, the biggest LPG disruption in history, the biggest fertilizer shipping disruption in history, and the biggest helium supply disruption in history — all at once, with no pipeline bypass for any of them.

Forbes estimates a daily supply deficit of approximately 15 million barrels of oil equivalent once the pipeline bypass is accounted for. OPEC output plunged by 7 million bpd. Iraq was forced to shut its massive Rumaila oil field and cut 1.5 million bpd of production as southern export terminals ran out of tanker capacity — Iraq's entire fiscal model depends on Gulf exports, and the Washington Examiner called it "the biggest state casualty of the Hormuz crisis." Qatar declared force majeure on its LNG contracts. And the IEA warned that April's supply losses would be double March's — a forward-looking assessment from the world's most authoritative energy forecaster that the market seems determined to ignore every time a diplomatic headline appears.

### The Commodity Cascade

The non-oil dimensions of this crisis are where the analysis gets truly alarming, because they receive a fraction of the attention while potentially causing comparable economic damage.

**LNG and LPG:** India gets 90% of its LPG imports from the Middle East. Domestic production covers only 40% of demand. LPG is not a luxury in India — it is the cooking fuel for hundreds of millions of households. The Indian Navy escorted the LPG tanker *Nanda Devi* through the Gulf of Oman, with Indian television tracking its progress in real-time like a wartime supply mission. The ship received routing instructions from IRGC commanders to use the Larak channel. India secured passage through direct diplomatic negotiation — but the fact that a routine commodity shipment required naval escort, live TV coverage, and diplomatic back-channels tells you everything about the new regime. China's LPG prices hit a 12-year high. TotalEnergies CEO Patrick Pouyanné reported jet fuel at $200 per barrel and diesel at $160 per barrel. China banned fuel exports. Thailand imposed gasoline rationing.

**Fertilizer:** Thirty percent of global fertilizer trade passes through Hormuz. Fifty percent of global sulfur shipments transit the strait. According to maritime intelligence firm Windward, 86% of fertilizer ships from the Gulf to East Africa have ceased operations. Fifteen percent of global ammonia trade and 21% of urea trade are affected. The UN World Food Programme warned of "record levels of hunger" as sub-Saharan African farmers enter planting season unable to treat their crops. This is a famine risk with a 3-6 month lag — the food price inflation from disrupted fertilizer supplies won't show up until harvest season, but it is already structurally embedded.

**Aluminium:** Iranian missiles and drones struck Emirates Global Aluminium's Al Taweelah complex (the Middle East's largest aluminium producer) and Alba in Bahrain. EGA warned that full production recovery could take up to one year. This is because aluminium smelter "pot lines" — the electrolytic cells that reduce alumina to aluminium metal — cannot simply be restarted once disrupted. The cells must be completely rebuilt, and the specialized equipment takes months to manufacture and install. Gulf countries account for approximately 10% of global aluminium supply, but over 80% of Gulf aluminium is exported (unlike China, where domestic production is consumed internally). The U.S. imports more than 20% of its aluminium from the Gulf. LME aluminium jumped 6% in a single session and hit its highest level since March 2022.

**Helium:** Qatar produces approximately one-third of the world's helium, an irreplaceable input for semiconductor fabrication (where it's used as a cooling agent in chip manufacturing processes) and MRI scanners. Damage to Qatar's LNG facilities — which produce helium as a byproduct — could take up to five years to fully repair. South Korea and Taiwan reported approximately two months of helium stocks, a thin buffer for industries that cannot function without it.

### The Shipping Ecosystem Transformation

The rerouting is staggering in scale. Vessel traffic passing the Cape of Good Hope has increased 112%. Maersk, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, and MSC — the world's four largest container shipping lines — all suspended Hormuz transits. The diversions add thousands of nautical miles and weeks of transit time to voyages that previously took days through the strait. A Cheniere LNG cargo from the U.S. Gulf Coast to Asia now takes 28 days rather than the 17-18 it would take via Suez and Hormuz. Every extra day at sea is an extra day of fuel consumption, crew costs, and tied-up capital — costs that ultimately flow through to consumers.

VLCC day rates hit an all-time record of $423,736, reflecting the sudden scarcity of available tonnage caused by the longer voyages. When every ship takes 50-70% longer to complete a round trip, the effective global fleet shrinks by a corresponding percentage — you need more ships to carry the same amount of cargo, and ships take years to build.

### The Human Cost

The most visceral evidence that this is not a temporary premium lives in the developing world. Bangladesh and Pakistan implemented "energy lockdowns" — Covid-style restrictions on movement and economic activity designed to reduce fuel consumption. Gas station workers have been killed in fuel-related violence. India experienced fuel riots. These are not market inconveniences. They are the physical manifestation of a supply shock hitting economies with minimal strategic reserves and complete dependence on imported energy.

The energy-importing emerging markets that ring the Indian Ocean — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Thailand, the Philippines — are experiencing balance-of-payments pressure that could tip into crisis. When your fuel import bill doubles and you can't pass the full cost through to consumers without risking social unrest, the difference comes out of foreign exchange reserves. When those reserves deplete, you go to the IMF. The EEM options market is pricing this with a distinctive "hump" structure — three-month implied volatility exceeding both near-term and six-month vol, indicating that the market expects EM stress to peak in the May-June timeframe as accumulated import costs deplete reserves and force emergency measures.

## What the Market Is Missing

### The False Precedent of 1973

The closest historical analogy is the 1973 Arab oil embargo, and it is instructive primarily for what it tells us about the gap between how long a crisis lasts and how long its consequences persist. The OAPEC embargo lasted only five months — October 1973 to March 1974. Oil prices quadrupled from $2.90 to $11.65 per barrel. And then, after the embargo was lifted: *prices stayed elevated permanently.* They never returned to pre-embargo levels. The five-month event triggered the creation of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, CAFE fuel efficiency standards, a decade of inflation that the Federal Reserve failed to control, the petrodollar system, and a permanent restructuring of the U.S.-Saudi relationship.

The Burns-era Fed made a specific analytical error that is being repeated today: it treated oil-driven cost-push inflation as "a structural problem not amenable to macro-economic measures," which became a self-fulfilling prophecy as expectations unanchored. The current Fed, with Core PCE already at 3.06% before the full oil shock hit, faces an identical trap. Powell's comment that "patience with oil-driven inflation has limits" is a direct callback to this history — he is trying to avoid Burns's mistake, but the tools available to him are no better.

The current crisis is larger than 1973 in every measurable dimension. The 1973 embargo removed approximately 7-8% of global oil supply. The current disruption has removed approximately 20% of global petroleum liquids from their normal transit route. And unlike 1973, the current crisis simultaneously disrupts LNG, LPG, fertilizers, petrochemicals, aluminium, and helium — commodity categories that didn't exist at scale in the 1970s energy economy.

### The Toll Corridor as Permanent Institution

What is genuinely new about the current situation — the thing that has no precedent — is Iran's creation of an institutional framework to monetize chokepoint control on a sustained basis. This isn't a blockade that ends when a military operation concludes. It is an emerging regulatory regime, complete with parliamentary legislation, fee schedules, screening protocols, and preferential treatment for geopolitical allies.

The geopolitical sorting mechanism is particularly significant. China and India — which together account for approximately 35% of global oil demand — receive preferential access. Payments are settled in Chinese yuan. Chinese maritime intermediaries facilitate the transactions. This is not merely a toll regime; it is a mechanism for de-dollarizing energy trade through the world's most critical chokepoint. Every ship that pays Iran in yuan to transit Hormuz is a data point in the slow erosion of dollar hegemony in commodity markets.

Johns Hopkins professor Vali Nasr, one of the most respected Iran scholars in the U.S., said at CERAWeek that Tehran's actual strategic objective is not a ceasefire but a "grand bargain" that includes permanent recognition of Iran's Strait control, economic compensation, and security guarantees. If that's correct — and it's consistent with Iran's institutional investment in the toll regime — then the chokepoint premium doesn't resolve with peace. It resolves only if Iran's government falls (which Mattis called "delusional") or if physical bypass infrastructure eliminates Hormuz's strategic significance (which takes 5-10 years and doesn't work for non-crude commodities).

### The Supply Response That Isn't Coming

In every prior oil shock, the price signal eventually triggered a supply response. High prices made marginal production profitable, capital flowed into drilling, and within 12-24 months, supply expansion moderated prices. This mechanism is broken.

U.S. shale producers maintained capital discipline throughout the price surge. Three independent sources — Reuters, CNBC, and structural analysis of capital allocation patterns — confirm that no meaningful increase in drilling activity occurred even as oil hit $116. This isn't irrational behavior; it reflects a decade of investor pressure on E&P companies to prioritize returns over growth, the physical maturation of the most productive shale formations, and management teams that remember the 2014-2016 price collapse that punished overinvestment. Oilfield services companies Halliburton and Baker Hughes — the companies that would see the first orders if drilling ramped — reported no uptick.

Replicating bypass infrastructure is equally constrained. Building a pipeline equivalent to Saudi Arabia's East-West corridor would cost at least $5 billion and take years. More ambitious cross-border routes (Iraq-Jordan-Syria-Turkey) would require $15-20 billion and face security risks, engineering challenges, and political disputes over ownership and control. Cat Group CEO Christopher Bush estimated these timelines at CERAWeek, and no one disagreed.

The implication is that the supply-demand imbalance at current prices has no self-correcting mechanism on any timeline shorter than 3-5 years. The oil floor isn't $100-110 because of a temporary war premium. It is $100-110 because the physical infrastructure to route around Hormuz doesn't exist and can't be built quickly.

### Destroyed Capacity Doesn't Care About Peace

Even if a ceasefire were announced tomorrow and the strait magically reopened, the damage to Gulf industrial capacity would persist for months to years. EGA's aluminium smelters take up to a year to rebuild. Qatar's LNG facilities — the ones that also produce one-third of the world's helium — could take up to five years to fully restore. The SAMREF refinery at Yanbu was damaged by Iranian strikes. Kuwait's refining capacity took hits. Iraq's Rumaila field, once shut, doesn't restart at full capacity overnight.

This is the analytical error embedded in every "oil pulls back $15 on peace headlines" trade. The market is pricing a binary — war continues or war ends — when the reality is a continuum where even the best-case scenario leaves significant supply offline for an extended period. ConocoPhillips CEO Ryan Lance said it plainly at CERAWeek: "You can't take 8-10 million bpd of oil and 20% of LNG off the world stage without significant repercussions." Kuwait Petroleum's CEO called it "an attack holding the world's economy hostage" with "domino effects." Chevron CEO Mike Wirth warned that physical supply was much tighter than futures prices indicated.

The CEOs of the world's largest energy companies unanimously agreed that markets are underpricing the disruption. That's not a dispositive signal — CEOs talk their own book — but the unanimity, at a venue (CERAWeek in Houston) where bearish and bullish views normally compete vigorously, was striking.

## The Scenarios That Matter

### Scenario 1: Peace With Persistent Toll Corridor (40-45% probability)

**Trigger:** A ceasefire is negotiated in the next 2-8 weeks. Trump accepts a deal that doesn't require Hormuz reopening. Iran retains the Larak Island toll regime. Insurance markets remain cautious.

**How it plays out:** Oil settles in the $100-115 range — lower than the acute crisis peak but permanently higher than the pre-war $70-75. The toll corridor adds $1-3 per barrel to transit costs for crude and proportionally more for LNG and other commodities. Insurance premiums normalize partially but remain 10-20x pre-war levels for at least 12-18 months. Traffic through Hormuz recovers to perhaps 40-50% of pre-war levels as insurance gradually returns, but the rerouting via Cape of Good Hope remains the default for many shippers. Gulf industrial capacity (aluminium, LNG, refining) rebuilds over 6-18 months, with helium taking longer.

**Investment implications:** This is the most likely scenario and the one the market is least prepared for, because it combines "peace" (which the market assumes means normalization) with persistent elevated costs (which the market is not pricing). Energy producers with access to non-Gulf supply win structurally: U.S. E&P (COP, EOG, OVV), U.S. refiners (VLO, MPC, PSX), and U.S. LNG exporters (LNG/Cheniere) all benefit from a $100-115 oil floor that doesn't trigger the demand destruction of $130+. CF Industries benefits from persistent fertilizer supply disruption. Tanker operators (STNG) benefit from sustained rerouting demand. The losers are energy-importing EM nations whose balance-of-payments math doesn't work at $100+ oil, Gulf states whose non-oil industrial exports (aluminium, petrochemicals) are impaired, and any business model predicated on cheap energy and short supply chains.

**What to watch:** Insurance market behavior is the leading indicator. When P&I clubs begin re-entering Gulf war risk coverage — even at elevated premiums — that signals the toll corridor is being accepted as a manageable cost rather than an uninsurable risk.

### Scenario 2: Escalation and Infrastructure Destruction (25-30% probability)

**Trigger:** Iran rejects peace proposals. Trump follows through on threats to destroy Kharg Island, Iran's primary oil export terminal. Ground operations commence. The conflict expands to include sustained Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping, activating a true dual-chokepoint crisis.

**How it plays out:** Oil surges to $130-150. Kharg Island destruction alone removes 2.5-3 million bpd of Iranian supply permanently — this infrastructure would take 3-5 years to rebuild even under optimal conditions. The dual-chokepoint scenario (Hormuz + Bab el-Mandeb) disrupts the Saudi pipeline's utility because Yanbu ships into the Red Sea, which is also under Houthi threat. Physical allocation regimes replace market pricing for refined products across Asia and potentially Europe. Shell CEO Wael Sawan warned that fuel shortages would spread from Asia to Europe by April — under this scenario, that timeline accelerates.

**Investment implications:** JP Morgan projected $150 oil if Hormuz remains closed through mid-May; this scenario makes that call conservative. Energy equities surge further — U.S. shale could earn an extra $63.4 billion in 2026 at $100 WTI, and this scenario puts WTI well above that. But the demand destruction is severe: airlines face existential fuel costs, consumer discretionary collapses, and the Fed faces an impossible choice between hiking into recession or tolerating inflation above 5%. Treasury market dysfunction becomes a real risk as the hedge fund basis trade ($1 trillion+ in leveraged positions per the FT) unwinds. Gold, which has underperformed during this crisis due to dollar strength and hawkish Fed rhetoric, could finally break out as the ultimate crisis hedge — institutional options positioning (GLD put/call OI ratio declining for five consecutive readings to 0.51, meaning persistent call buying) suggests smart money is already positioning for this.

**What to watch:** Iran's response to the current peace proposal is the immediate trigger. If rejected while Trump has publicly committed to infrastructure destruction, the escalation path narrows to either a credibility-damaging backdown or a strike that permanently reshapes global oil markets.

### Scenario 3: Rapid De-Escalation and Normalization (10-15% probability)

**Trigger:** Iran accepts a comprehensive peace deal that includes credible mechanisms for Hormuz reopening. The U.S. provides or arranges naval escort operations. Insurance markets re-enter Gulf coverage within 3-6 months.

**How it plays out:** Oil drops to $90-100 within weeks, then gradually reverts toward $80-85 over 6-12 months as Gulf capacity rebuilds. LNG, LPG, and petrochemical flows resume. The acute crisis fades into a war premium of $5-10 per barrel that persists for 1-2 years. Consumer spending recovers modestly. The Fed breathes easier but doesn't cut — Core PCE remains above target through 2026.

**Investment implications:** Energy equities give back gains — the $116-to-$100 dip on peace headlines showed the market's eagerness to reprice. Defense stocks also pull back short-term but follow the post-9/11 pattern of sustained outperformance through the appropriations cycle (the $1.5 trillion budget proposal is already in motion). The biggest beneficiaries are the positions that have been crushed: airlines, homebuilders, consumer discretionary, and EM equities. HYG spreads tighten. The credit cascade thesis loses its most powerful catalyst.

**What to watch:** Gen. Mattis's assessment that Iran has a "vote" on when the war ends and that regime change is "delusional" is the structural constraint on this scenario. It requires Iran to want normalization — and Vali Nasr's analysis suggests Tehran wants something far more ambitious.

### Scenario 4: The New Petro-Order (20-25% probability)

**Trigger:** The crisis doesn't resolve cleanly into peace or escalation but instead settles into a semi-permanent contested status — the "frozen conflict" model familiar from Ukraine, Korea, or the Taiwan Strait. Iran maintains the toll corridor. The U.S. eventually draws down military operations without forcing Hormuz open. Gulf states invest heavily in bypass infrastructure that takes 5-10 years to complete. The world gradually adjusts to permanently higher trade costs.

**How it plays out:** This is the true regime change scenario, comparable to the post-1973 energy order. Oil stabilizes in a new $90-110 structural range (vs. the pre-war $60-75 range). LNG and LPG trade reorganizes around non-Gulf supply sources — the U.S. becomes the world's most important marginal LNG supplier. Petrochemical production shifts from the Gulf to the U.S. Gulf Coast, where feedstock (cheap natural gas at $3-4/MMBtu) is abundant and not subject to chokepoint risk. Aluminium production shifts toward North America and Australia. The fertilizer trade reroutes through longer supply chains with permanently higher costs. Global inflation runs 0.5-1.5% higher than it otherwise would for a decade, as the efficiency gains of the pre-2026 trade architecture are permanently reversed.

**Investment implications:** This is the scenario with the most profound long-term portfolio consequences. It favors U.S.-based producers across energy, chemicals, and industrial metals who inherit structural cost advantages from geographic insulation. It is deeply negative for energy-importing nations and any business model built on globalized supply chains transiting Gulf chokepoints. It accelerates the trend toward regionalized trade blocs and nearshoring. And it creates a new class of geopolitical risk premium that reprices sovereign credit, corporate insurance costs, and shipping rates on a permanent basis.

**What to watch:** Gulf state pipeline investment announcements are the revealed-preference signal. When Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar commit tens of billions to bypass infrastructure with 5-7 year construction timelines, they are telling you with their capital allocation decisions that they expect the Hormuz risk to persist for at least that long.

## Positioning

### Theme 1: U.S. Energy Producers as Structural Winners (High Conviction)

The logic is straightforward: if the Hormuz chokepoint remains contested under any scenario, then energy produced and refined in the Western Hemisphere commands a permanent premium over Gulf-sourced energy. U.S. crude doesn't transit Hormuz. U.S. LNG ships from Sabine Pass, not Ras Laffan. U.S. refiners process domestic crude into products that Gulf refiners can't currently export.

**Exposure:** COP, EOG, and OVV for E&P at current oil prices ($107+) with a $100-110 structural floor. Valero (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC), and Phillips 66 (PSX) for refining — Gulf refinery damage (SAMREF, Kuwait) directly increases margin advantage for U.S. Gulf Coast refiners. Cheniere (LNG) for LNG exports — Asia-bound LNG from the U.S. Gulf Coast now takes 28 days, long but insurable, versus an indefinite transit through an Iranian toll booth. The Energy Select SPDR (XLE) is up 27% year-to-date; Exxon is up 30%. U.S. shale could earn an extra $63.4 billion at $100 WTI — and WTI is currently at $107.

**What this is not:** A bet on $150 oil. That's Scenario 2, which has meaningful probability but isn't the base case. This is a bet on $100-115 oil for years, not months — a floor that is high enough to generate exceptional free cash flow for disciplined producers but not so high that it triggers the demand destruction that would break the thesis.

### Theme 2: Fertilizer Insulation (Maximum Conviction)

CF Industries produces nitrogen-based fertilizers using U.S. natural gas as feedstock, at a cost advantage of 6-7x over European producers. Thirty percent of global fertilizer trade transits Hormuz. Fifty percent of global sulfur — a critical fertilizer input — transits the strait. Under any scenario including rapid de-escalation, the disruption to Gulf fertilizer supply chains persists for months. Under the more likely scenarios (toll corridor or frozen conflict), it persists for years.

**Exposure:** CF Industries (CF) at approximately 9x forward earnings. This has been a maximum-conviction position with 12+ independent confirmations across supply disruption, cost advantage, and demand inelasticity. Fertilizer demand is seasonal and non-discretionary — farmers plant when they plant, and they need fertilizer regardless of price. The FAO's warning about global food price increases is a second-order confirmation.

## What Could Prove Me Wrong

The strongest counterargument to the permanent regime thesis is simply that markets have correctly priced temporary disruptions before. The 2019 Saudi Aramco drone attack removed 5.7 million bpd of supply; prices spiked and normalized within weeks. The 2024 Houthi Red Sea disruptions caused shipping reroutes and insurance spikes that eventually stabilized. In both cases, the market's instinct that the disruption was temporary proved correct.

What's different now? Three things: (1) Iran has built institutional infrastructure to monetize chokepoint control, which didn't exist in prior disruptions; (2) the White House has signaled willingness to accept the status quo, removing the main enforcement mechanism for reopening; and (3) the industrial damage (aluminium smelters, LNG facilities) has physical rebuild timelines that are independent of geopolitics. But I could be wrong about all three. Iran's toll regime might collapse under pressure from a multinational naval coalition that Rubio assembles. Trump might change his mind about Hormuz objectives. And the industrial damage might be less severe than initial assessments suggest.

The specific data that would change my view: (1) If P&I clubs begin re-entering Gulf war risk coverage within 60 days of a ceasefire at premiums below 0.1% of hull value — that would signal the insurance market sees the risk as genuinely resolved. (2) If Iran dismantles the Larak Island toll infrastructure as part of a peace deal — that would eliminate the institutional permanence thesis. (3) If March 31 NAV marks from private credit funds show write-downs below 3% and the EA $18 billion junk bond offering prices successfully — that would significantly reduce the credit cascade probability that compounds the energy thesis.

I am most uncertain about timing. The structural thesis (Hormuz never fully normalizes, $100+ oil floor, accelerated deglobalization of energy infrastructure) could take 6-18 months to be fully priced by markets. In the interim, sharp tactical rallies on peace headlines will test the conviction of anyone positioned for the long game.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The $100-110 oil floor is structural, not cyclical.** Under any peace scenario — including the White House's stated willingness to end the war without reopening Hormuz — oil holds this range because bypass infrastructure handles only 28% of normal Hormuz flows and covers crude oil only, not LNG, LPG, fertilizers, or petrochemicals.

2. **The non-oil supply shock may be more economically damaging than the crude shortage.** India's LPG crisis, the fertilizer disruption threatening sub-Saharan agriculture, the aluminium smelter destruction requiring up to a year to rebuild, and Qatar's helium supply damage (potentially 5 years to restore) represent cascading commodity crises with no pipeline bypass.

3. **Iran's toll corridor is an institution, not a tactic.** Parliamentary legislation, yuan-denominated payments, IRGC screening protocols, and preferential treatment for friendly nations constitute a de facto regulatory regime that is being built to persist regardless of whether active military operations continue.

4. **The insurance market is the structural barrier to normalization.** Actuaries closed the strait faster than mines could have. Insurance market re-entry after comparable disruptions has historically taken 1-3 years. Every investor pricing a return to normal shipping patterns within months should explain their theory of how seven P&I clubs reverse their decisions.

5. **The Fed is trapped in a 1970s dilemma.** Core PCE was already at 3.06% before the Hormuz shock. Fourteen active CPI channels will push inflation toward 4.0-4.5% by H2 2026. The Fed can't cut (inflation) and may have to hike (four hawkish FOMC voices). The 5-year breakeven at 2.57% is dramatically below the model's 4.0-4.5% central case — either the market or the model is wrong, and the physical commodity data supports the model.

6. **U.S. energy, refining, and LNG producers are the clearest structural winners.** Geographic insulation from Hormuz risk, disciplined capital allocation (no supply response diluting the price signal), and the destruction of competing Gulf refining and industrial capacity create a multi-year advantage that persists under every scenario except rapid, complete normalization (10-15% probability).

7. **Gulf states commissioning multi-year bypass pipelines is the strongest revealed-preference signal.** When nations with the deepest knowledge of regional geopolitics commit tens of billions in capital to infrastructure that takes 5-7 years to build, they are telling you with their money that they expect this risk to persist for at least that long. Listen to what they spend, not what they say.
